**Tweet 2（72候の説明）**
Japan doesn't just have 4 seasons.

The traditional calendar divides the year into 72 micro-seasons — each about 5 days long, each named after something happening in nature right now.

This one is called 蛙始鳴.
*Kawazu hajimete naku.*
"Frogs begin to sing."

---

**Tweet 3（なぜこの時期か）**
The timing isn't random.

In early May, Japanese farmers flood their rice paddies.

Water flows in. Fields become shallow mirrors.

And frogs — who've been waiting underground for exactly this warmth — appear almost immediately.

They've been doing this for 10,000 years.

---

**Tweet 4（言葉遊び）**
Here's something that doesn't translate.

The Japanese word for frog: *kaeru* 蛙
The Japanese word for "to return home": *kaeru* 帰る

Same sound. Different characters.

So Japanese people keep frog figurines in their wallets — to make money *return*. Near doorways — to bring travelers *home*.

---

**Tweet 5（芭蕉）**
The most famous haiku in Japanese was written about a frog.

Matsuo Bashō, 1686:

古池や　蛙飛び込む　水の音

*An old silent pond.*
*A frog jumps into the pond.*
*Splash! Silence again.*

Scholars have debated it for 300 years. I think Bashō just stopped and listened.

---

**Tweet 6（現代との接続）**
In Japan's cities, this sound is disappearing.

Rice paddies become parking lots. Frogs lose their water.

But in the countryside — and in the suburbs, near canals and parks — if you step outside after dark this week, you might still find them.

Wet. Insistent. Alive.
