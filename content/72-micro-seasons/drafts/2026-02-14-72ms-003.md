# Rising Through the Thinning Ice

*Micro-season #3 of 72 — February 14–18*

---

If you kneel beside a pond in mid-February and look through the ice — not at it, but through it — you will see something happening below. The bottom is still there, still cold, still dark. But there is movement now. Shapes that were absent in the deepest weeks of winter have begun to appear near the surface, pressing upward against the underside of the ice, drawn by the longer light above.

This is a moment that requires stillness to witness. You have to be the kind of person who kneels beside frozen ponds in winter. But the ancient Japanese who assembled this calendar clearly were that kind of people. They watched. They waited. They gave names to what they saw.

This third micro-season of the year does not arrive with drama. The ice has not broken. The fish have not leapt. But the direction of things has decisively reversed. Life, having retreated into depth, is now pressing upward.

---

## 魚上氷 — Uo kōri o izuru

Three characters, economical as a haiku. 魚 (*uo* or *sakana*) means fish. 上 (*ue*, or here used as a verb, *agaru* / *izuru*) means to rise, to ascend, to emerge above. 氷 (*kōri*) means ice.

The phrase does not say the ice has melted. It says the fish have risen *through* it — or *above* it, or *out of* it. The ambiguity is intentional and revealing. The ice is still present. The fish are still acting in relation to it. What has changed is the direction of the fish's movement, which in the logic of the seasonal calendar is enough.

This is the third and final micro-season within Risshun, the "standing up of spring" solar term. We have moved from the first stirring of the east wind (loosening the ice), through the uguisu's first song (spring announcing itself by voice), and now to the underwater world responding in kind. Three realms — wind, air, water — have all registered the turn.

---

## What's Actually Happening

As the days lengthen past the winter solstice and February brings slightly stronger sunlight, a crucial physical phenomenon occurs in ponds and shallow lakes: the deep cold begins to release. Ice, which is less dense than liquid water, forms a cap on still water bodies. This cap keeps the water beneath it insulated — paradoxically, the ice protects the fish below from even colder temperatures above.

When sunlight strengthens and the surface ice begins to thin, light begins to penetrate to the lower water. This triggers biological responses in fish that have been in a winter torpor — slowed metabolism, reduced feeding, minimal movement. As water temperature edges upward and oxygen levels change, fish like yamame (landlocked cherry trout) begin to shift toward the surface and toward areas of open water near shores and inflows.

In the rivers of coastal Japan, February marks the beginning of the *shirauo* season. The shirauo — sometimes called icefish or noodlefish — are small, nearly transparent fish that migrate upriver from the sea to spawn in the late winter shallows. They are delicate, ethereal creatures, best eaten raw or lightly blanched. Their appearance in the fish markets and along the riverbanks is one of the clearest signs that the water, like the air, has begun to change its mind about winter.

---

## Fish and Islands

Japan is, fundamentally, a fish-watching culture. As an archipelago of more than 6,000 islands, surrounded by seas that bring cold currents and warm currents into collision, Japan has developed one of the world's most sophisticated relationships with the sea and its creatures. Fish appear in almost every dimension of Japanese culture: as food (raw, dried, fermented, pickled, grilled), as art (the carp scrolls of *koinobori*, the dried tuna carvings of Oshino Hakkai), as spiritual symbols (the carp that climbs the waterfall to become a dragon in Buddhist iconography), and as seasonal markers in the agricultural calendar.

The presence of fish in the 72-season system — appearing twice in spring alone, here and in the form of the herring in later seasons — reflects how carefully the system was calibrated to actual coastal and riverside observation. These were not metaphorical fish. They were the fish that the people who built this calendar could see from their bridges and rice paddies, moving through the water in ways that told them what the season was doing.

There is an old fishing village wisdom: you do not need a calendar to know when the fish are running. You need only to watch the water. The calendar exists to give a name to what the water already knows.

## A Poem for This Season

Yosa Buson (1716–1784), one of the great haiku masters of the Edo period, was drawn often to water and its creatures:

*鮎の子の / 白玉川を / 上りけり*

*Ayu no ko no / shiratama gawa o / nobori keri*

"Young sweetfish / ascending / the white-jeweled river."

The *ayu* (sweetfish) is a spring and summer kigo in haiku — a fish deeply associated with the freshness of moving water and new growth. Buson captures the upstream movement, fish pressing against the current, working their way into the year. That upward persistence — fish rising through cold water, through thinning ice, toward light — is exactly the spirit of this micro-season.

The river in Buson's poem is "white-jeweled" (*shiratama*): a description of the light on moving water, of the shimmer of late-winter sun on a stream that is just beginning to remember itself.

## How to Meet This Season

Find a body of still water — a park pond, a river backwater, a flooded rice paddy that hasn't been planted yet. Look at the surface for any open patches where ice has receded. Then look through the ice where it remains, toward the darker water beneath. Be patient.

What you are looking for is movement below the surface. A shadow that shifts. A flash that might be a scale. The water below ice is not empty. It is waiting.

If there is no ice where you live, look instead for the returning fish at waterside fish markets. The arrival of seasonal species — whitebait, small trout, the first river fish of the year — is how this micro-season announces itself in the places where the ice has already given way.
