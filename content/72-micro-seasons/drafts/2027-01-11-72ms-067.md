# What Moves in the Dark Underground

*Micro-season #67 of 72 — January 11 to January 15*

---

The coldest weeks of the Japanese year are now.

*Daikan* (大寒), "Greater Cold," arrives later this month, and January 10 through 20 falls within *Shōkan* (小寒), "Lesser Cold" — but in practical terms, this is when the cold is hardest: when the ice on ponds is thickest, when mountain roads are most treacherous, when even the cities of the Pacific coast see their lowest temperatures.

And yet.

The calendar, with its characteristic preference for finding life where it seems to have withdrawn, chooses this moment to describe movement in the deep places underground. Not on the surface. Not visibly. But underneath, where frost doesn't reach and where the bedrock holds its ancient heat: the springs begin to move.

---

## 水泉動 — Shimizu Atataka o Fukumu

The full classical phrasing of this season is longer than its abbreviated name: *shimizu atataka o fukumu* — "the clear spring water holds warmth within it." But the micro-season heading reduces it to three characters:

*Sui* (水) is water. The character is one of the most ancient in Japanese writing — a flowing, sinuous form that even in its modern simplified version suggests the movement of water in channels.

*Sen* (泉) is spring — specifically, a natural spring, water emerging from the ground. The character shows water (水) at the bottom and white (白) above it, possibly evoking the white foam or the bright clarity of spring water as it surfaces. A *sen* is an emergence point, a place where the underground meets the above-ground.

*Dō* (動) is motion, movement, activity. The character combines the word for heavy (重) with force (力), suggesting something substantial beginning to stir.

Water. Spring. Movement. The underground water is beginning to stir.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Deep aquifers and bedrock springs maintain a relatively constant temperature year-round, regulated not by surface weather but by the thermal mass of the earth itself. In temperate Japan, deep spring water typically emerges at temperatures between 10 and 15 degrees Celsius — warmer than the air in winter, cooler than the air in summer.

But in mid-January, even these deep temperatures are at their annual minimum, the cold having slowly worked its way down through weeks and months of low temperatures. The spring water, though still above freezing, is at its coldest point in the annual cycle.

What the micro-season captures is the first moment of the turn. From here — imperceptibly, over the next weeks and months — the deep water will slowly begin to absorb more heat from the surrounding bedrock, whose own temperature lags behind air temperature by weeks or months. The springs don't burst forth with warmth. They accumulate it, slowly, from below.

This is geology on a seasonal scale: the deep earth as a thermal buffer, holding and releasing heat on a schedule entirely its own, unaffected by the drama on the surface.

For farmers, spring water in January was a life-essential resource. While surface ponds and rivers froze, many springs continued to flow — and their water, warmer than ambient, prevented the areas around spring heads from freezing solid. These were the places where animals gathered to drink. They were also watched carefully for any change in flow rate or temperature, which could indicate underground shifts.

In farming villages near volcanic areas, hot springs (*onsen*, 温泉) were often the primary source of winter warmth for both bathing and domestic heating. The *onsen* was the community's shared heart through the cold months. And unlike surface warmth, which came and went with the sun, the underground warmth was constant.

---

## Listening to What's Underground

There is a concept in Japanese traditional thought — *chiryoku* (地力), literally "earth power" — that refers to the soil's natural fertility and regenerative capacity. A healthy *chiryoku* is one of the farmer's greatest assets: the ability of the land to keep giving, year after year, without exhausting itself.

But *chiryoku* extends beyond soil chemistry. It encompasses the whole system of underground energy: water moving through root systems and aquifers, minerals cycling between bedrock and topsoil, microbial networks threading through the dark. The spring is one of the visible signs of this invisible vitality.

In Shinto cosmology, springs are particularly sacred. The word *izumi* (泉) — spring — appears in the sacred place name *Izumo* (出雲), one of the most important Shinto sites in Japan. The *yōmono* (世物, things of the world) are sustained by what comes up from the ground. Springs are where the earth exhales.

To say, in mid-January, that the springs are moving — that something is beginning to stir underground — is to say that the earth is still alive. This might seem obvious. But in the darkest weeks of winter, surrounded by frozen surfaces and bare branches and a sun that barely clears the southern horizon, the news from underground matters.

## A Poem for This Season

The haiku poet Matsuo Bashō, on a journey through the cold of mid-winter, wrote:

> 寒さかな　山里々の　水の音
> *samusa kana / yamazato-zato no / mizu no oto*

*The cold —*
*from mountain village to mountain village,*
*the sound of water.*

The cold is absolute — *samusa kana*, "this cold!" — but the poem's movement is toward sound rather than away from it. In the cold, one hears more clearly. The sound of water, moving from one village to the next in the network of streams and irrigation channels, carries through the cold air. The water is not frozen. It moves.

The poem is one of Bashō's simplest, and perhaps most precise. It says: in the cold, there is sound. The water is moving. The world is still happening.

---

## How to Meet This Season

In Japan, mid-January is the heart of winter bathing culture. *Kanmisogi* (寒禊) — cold-water purification — involves immersing oneself in cold water (rivers, the sea, shrine ponds) in January as a spiritual practice. The contrast between the outside cold and the shock of cold water, paradoxically, creates a sense of fierce aliveness.

You don't need to go that far. But this week, find running water.

Not a tap. Not a shower. An actual moving body of water — a stream, a river, a fountain if that's all you have. And spend some time near it, listening to the sound the water makes. In January, with the surrounding world quiet and cold, the sound of moving water carries differently than it does in the warm months. It sounds more determined. More essential.

Touch the water if you can. It will be cold on the surface. But deep springs, where they exist, run warmer than the air. The earth is holding something for us: warmth accumulated over months and years of geological time, slowly releasing.

The underground is moving. We just can't see it yet.
