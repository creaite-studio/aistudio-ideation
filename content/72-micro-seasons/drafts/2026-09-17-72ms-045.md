# The Sky Goes Quiet

*Micro-season #45 of 72 — September 17 to September 21*

---

There's a specific quality of quiet that follows the last storm of the season.

It's different from ordinary quiet. Ordinary quiet is just the absence of sound. Post-storm quiet has texture to it — the air has been washed, there's a particular clarity to the light, the trees are still moving from the memory of the wind. It feels like the world has been rearranged slightly. Cleaned. Set down with care.

In Japan, the storms of late summer are formidable. The typhoon season runs roughly from July through October, with peak activity in September. The thunderstorms of August and September — often called *guerrilla rainstorms* (*gerira gōu*, ゲリラ豪雨) in modern weather reporting — can be sudden, violent, and disorienting. They hit with no warning, dump extraordinary rainfall in minutes, and then pass.

And then, sometime around this third week of September, they stop.

The sky goes quiet. The 72 micro-seasons have a name for this.

---

## 雷乃収声 — Kaminari Sunawachi Koe o Osamu

The name is one of the longer ones in the autumn sequence, and it unfolds like a formal announcement.

*雷* (kaminari) is thunder. The character contains *ame* (雨, rain) at the top and *ta* (田, rice field) at the bottom — you can read it as "the rice field's rain," or as the power that comes from storm. In classical Japanese, thunder was understood as the voice of the gods (*kaminari* literally contains *kami*, 神, deity, though etymologists debate whether this connection is folk etymology or genuine). Thunder was not simply weather; it was communication from above.

*乃* (no, or sunawachi) is the same classical particle we saw in the rice-ripening season: "and so," "therefore," "in accordance with nature."

*収声* (shūsei, or koe o osamu) breaks into: *収* (to gather in, to collect, to store away) and *声* (voice, sound). *Koe o osamu* is a beautiful phrase: to put away one's voice, to gather the sound back in, to store the thunder. The thunder is not merely stopping — it is *collecting itself*, withdrawing, returning to wherever it came from.

*Thunder gathers its voice away.* The sky's speech is finished for this year.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The thunder season in Japan is connected directly to atmospheric dynamics.

Summer thunderstorms are powered by convective heating: the sun heats the ground, the warm air rises, moisture condenses into massive cumulonimbus clouds, and the charge separation inside those clouds creates lightning and thunder. The more heat there is at the surface, the more powerful the convection, the more dramatic the storms.

As autumn sets in and the Pacific High retreats, the ground temperature falls, the atmosphere stabilizes, and convective thunderstorms become less frequent. The transition typically happens in September, though it varies by region. The Japan Sea coast, which gets its winter storms from cold air crossing the warmer sea, actually sees an increase in thunder in autumn and winter — but in central and Pacific-coast Japan, September is the turning point.

There's also the typhoon dimension. Japan's major typhoons tend to peak in August and September. By late September, the typhoon tracks shift southward and the season begins to close. The combination of fewer convective storms and the end of typhoon season makes this period genuinely quieter than what came before.

## The Gods Who Go Traveling

October has a traditional Japanese name: *Kannazuki* (神無月) — the month without gods.

According to ancient belief, the eight million deities of the Shinto pantheon leave their local shrines in October and travel together to the Grand Shrine at Izumo, on the Japan Sea coast of Shimane Prefecture. There, they hold their annual meeting — primarily to arrange marriages and coordinate human fates for the coming year. Meanwhile, the local shrines stand temporarily empty of their divine residents.

The fading of thunder at this micro-season was sometimes understood as part of this divine withdrawal. The thunder gods retreating. The sky clearing of their presence. October's stillness as the absence of the divine, not merely the absence of weather.

In Izumo alone, October carries the opposite name: *Kamiarizuki* (神在月) — the month *with* gods. Because while the rest of Japan empties of its deities, Izumo is receiving all of them at once.

I find something almost humorous in this cosmology: eight million gods doing annual HR administration, coordinating across the country, arriving at the one shrine that's hosting this year's meeting. And the thunder falling silent while they travel.

## A Poem for This Season

The 13th-century poet Fujiwara no Teika left poems about autumn silence that feel relevant here — the particular silence after storm, the specific quality of cleared air:

> 心なき　身にも哀れは　知られけり
> 鴫立つ沢の　秋の夕暮れ
>
> *Kokoro naki / mi ni mo aware wa / shira re keri / shigi tatsu sawa no / aki no yūgure*

*Even someone without feeling / would know the pathos — / snipe rising from the marsh / in the autumn dusk.*

The poem begins with a disclaimer ("even someone without feeling") and then makes the case: the autumn dusk at this marsh, with the snipe startling up from the water, is powerful enough to pierce even a numb heart. The cleared sky after storm. The particular silence of autumn evening. The sudden movement of a bird that makes the silence more complete.

Teika's autumn is not golden-warm. It's luminous and a little desolate, in the way that clarity after storm can be desolate.

## How to Meet This Season

Pay attention to the quality of September silence.

If you're in Japan — particularly in late September — step outside after what might be one of the last major storms of the season. Notice the sky. Notice whether it looks different. Post-storm skies in Japan often have a distinctive clarity; the humidity that saturates August air has been washed down, and the light changes.

If you're outside Japan: every climate has its equivalent of this. The point at which the summer storms lose their conviction. The moment when the sky, having been dramatic for months, begins to settle into the more austere beauty of autumn.

The thunder gathers its voice. The world exhales.
