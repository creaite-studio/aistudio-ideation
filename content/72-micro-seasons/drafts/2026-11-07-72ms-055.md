# The Ground Has Made Its Decision

*Micro-season #55 of 72 — November 7 to November 11*

---

There's a morning in early November when you step outside and the ground sounds different beneath your feet.

Not frozen yet — not crunching with ice — but something has changed in the register. The soil feels denser. It gives less. Where last week there was a certain softness, a willingness to receive the impression of a boot, now there's a resistance. The earth is bracing. It has made up its mind.

I noticed it this week near the vegetable patch behind the house. The last of the shiso had collapsed. The basil was long gone. But when I crouched to pull out the dead roots, the top layer of soil was stiff in a way I hadn't felt since March. Something was happening underground that I couldn't see yet.

That's what this micro-season is about.

---

## 地始凍 — Chi Hajimete Kōru

The name of this season breaks into three pieces that feel almost like a sentence in slow motion.

*Chi* (地) means earth. Ground. Soil. Not earth in the abstract cosmic sense but the actual stuff underfoot — the particular loam of a particular field, the mud of a particular riverbank. In the kanji system, 地 is the character that anchors things. It's what stands still while everything else moves.

*Hajimete* (始) means "for the first time." This character contains within it the radical for woman (女) and the radical for beginning (台) — one of those etymological tangles that is probably coincidental but that generations of poets found irresistible anyway. What matters here is the sense of a threshold being crossed: not repetition but genuine firstness.

*Kōru* (凍) means to freeze. To solidify with cold. The character contains the ice radical (冫) on the left side, pairing it with the character for east (東) — though scholars debate whether this was ever intentional. What you see when you write it is a person (人) inside a tree (木) inside a covering of ice. Something alive, encased.

Put together: *The earth freezes for the first time.* Five syllables. A whole season.

---

## What's Actually Happening

November 7th falls within the solar term *Rittō* (立冬) — the official start of winter in Japan's traditional calendar. But the 72 micro-seasons don't much care about official starts. They track what is actually occurring in fields and forests, in soil and water.

What is occurring now is something farmers have always watched carefully: the first frost of the season penetrating below the surface.

When air temperatures drop consistently below freezing for the first time, the ground doesn't freeze instantly. The soil is a poor conductor of heat — it has been storing summer's warmth all this time, slowly releasing it. What happens in early November across central Honshu is that the topmost centimeters begin to undergo nightly freeze-thaw cycles. Water in the soil expands as ice, then contracts again as the afternoon sun briefly warms the surface.

This process is called *shimo bashira* (霜柱) in Japanese — "frost pillars" — when needle-like crystals of ice push up through the soil surface, sometimes raising small stones and clods of dirt with them. It's one of those phenomena that's easy to miss entirely if you're not in the habit of looking at the ground in early morning.

The farmers of old knew that this first freezing of the earth marked the end of the growing season in earnest. Root vegetables still in the ground would benefit from one or two light frosts, which can convert starches to sugars — this is why late-autumn daikon and burdock taste sweeter than their summer counterparts. But a hard, sustained freeze was another matter. The window for harvest was closing.

## Standing Between Two Worlds

There's a concept in Japanese thought that this season embodies well: *ma* (間), the meaningful pause, the threshold space. November is perhaps the year's greatest *ma* — neither the last warmth of autumn nor the full cold of winter. The earth itself is caught between two states.

In Shinto, the ground is not inert matter but living presence. The kami of earth and soil — generically called *kunitsukami* (国つ神) or earthly deities — are understood to be active agents in agricultural cycles. The freezing of the earth in autumn was traditionally seen as a kind of withdrawal, a retreating inward of these spirits for the cold months ahead. Farmers would observe small rituals of gratitude at this time, marking the end of the year's work with offerings left at field corners or at local shrines.

This is also the season of *nōson no yasumi* — rural rest. In pre-industrial Japan, the freezing of the earth was a kind of permission slip. The planting and harvesting were over. The land itself was telling you: go inside. Rest. The work is done.

There's a quiet dignity to that. The ground doesn't freeze apologetically. It doesn't try to hold onto warmth it no longer has. It simply changes state, as the season requires.

## A Poem for This Season

The great haiku master Matsuo Bashō wrote of winter earth with characteristic sparseness:

> 冬の日や　馬上に凍る　影法師
> *fuyu no hi ya / bajō ni kōru / kagebōshi*

*A winter's day —*
*astride a horse, freezing:*
*my shadow.*

The poem is devastating in its quiet way. The rider doesn't say he is cold. He notices his shadow — that trace of self cast on the frozen ground — and in noticing it, tells us everything about how it feels to move through a world that has gone hard and still around you. The shadow is what connects the rider to the earth: it touches the frozen ground that he himself cannot quite reach.

What Bashō understood is that winter is not absence. It's a different kind of presence. The freezing earth is not dead — it is holding. Storing. Waiting. The same way a poem holds its meaning below the surface, waiting for the reader to find it.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Look down this week.

Go out in the early morning — before the day warms up — and find a patch of bare soil somewhere. Look closely at the surface. If you're lucky, if the nights have been cold enough, you might find *shimo bashira*: those thin needles of ice pushing up from below, sometimes half a centimeter tall, sometimes more. They vanish within an hour of sunrise.

If the ground is still too soft for frost pillars, simply press your palm flat against the soil for a moment. Feel what's there — not cold exactly, but a kind of gathered heaviness. A potential. The earth is working on something.

That's the invitation of this season: to notice the things that are changing state slowly, invisibly, before they announce themselves. The earth has made its decision. The question is whether we are paying enough attention to receive the message.
