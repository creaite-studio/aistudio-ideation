# The Cricket at the Door

*Micro-season #50 of 72 — October 13 to October 17*

---

It's ten o'clock at night and the cricket has moved closer to the house.

Earlier in autumn it was somewhere in the garden, in the middle of the grass. Then near the edge of the garden, by the fence. Now it's at the threshold — somewhere at or near the step to the front door. I can hear it clearly with the window cracked: one cricket, its call slower and lower than the September crickets were, the rhythm of it like a conversation that keeps restarting.

In Japan, this movement of crickets toward human spaces — toward doorways, under floor boards, into the shelter of walls — is precisely what this micro-season describes. The crickets come inside, or near inside, as the cold increases outside. They find the warmth that human habitation provides. They sing at the threshold.

The season's name is not "crickets chirp in fields." It is *crickets chirp in doorways.*

---

## 蟋蟀在戸 — Kirigirisu To ni Ari

A strange and specific name.

*蟋蟀* (kirigirisu, or shitsushitsu in the more classical reading) refers to the cricket — but here a complication enters. In classical Japanese, the word *kirigirisu* referred to what we now call the *kōrogi* (cricket, family *Gryllidae*). The terminology shifted in the Meiji period, when Western biological taxonomy was imported, and *kirigirisu* came to refer instead to the katydid or long-horned grasshopper (*Tettigoniidae*). So when the ancient calendar says *kirigirisu*, it means what we would now call cricket; contemporary Japanese would say *kōrogi*.

The point is not the taxonomy but the sound. The chirping at the door.

*在* (ari, or iru) means to be, to exist, to be located at. It's a verb of presence — not *going toward* the door but *being at* the door.

*戸* (to) is door, gate, threshold. In classical architecture, the *to* marked the entry point of a dwelling — the boundary between inside and outside, shelter and world. It's the same character in *amado* (雨戸, storm shutters) and in the compound for household (*kōto*, 戸).

*The cricket is at the door.* Not just any cricket, in any field. This cricket, at this threshold. The calendar gives the cricket an address.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Japanese crickets (*Gryllus bimaculatus* and related species) are ectothermic — their body temperature and metabolism follow ambient temperature. As October cools, their chirp rate slows: this is so consistent that an 1897 formula, known as Dolbear's Law, allows you to estimate temperature from cricket chirp rate. (Count chirps in 14 seconds, add 40, and you have the Fahrenheit temperature. It actually works.)

The crickets' movement toward sheltered spots — walls, foundations, doorways, wood piles — is a behavioral response to falling temperatures. They're seeking thermal refuge, not companionship. But the effect, from a human perspective, is intimacy. The cricket comes to the threshold. The human is on the other side.

October is the final weeks of the cricket's adult life in most of Japan. The eggs have been laid in the soil; the next generation is already waiting underground. The adult cricket's remaining days are being spent near warmth, calling, slowing with the cold. Each night's chirp is slightly slower, slightly lower, than the night before.

## Insects as Aesthetic Experience

In Japan, insects have been kept as pets for their sound for well over a thousand years.

The practice of keeping singing insects — crickets, suzumushi, matsumushi — in decorative cages was a tradition of both the Heian court and the Edo townspeople. Small wooden or woven-grass cages with the insect inside were placed in rooms or carried in sleeves, providing a personal, portable autumn soundscape. The sale of singing insects at autumn markets (mushi-uri, 虫売り) was a seasonal business in Edo-period Tokyo, with vendors carrying hundreds of cages through the streets.

This aesthetic appreciation is not incidental to Japanese culture — it's central to it. The fact that an insect makes an interesting sound is treated, in Japan, as sufficient reason to keep it near you, listen to it carefully, and compose poems about it. The insect is not merely a background noise. It is a presence, a companion of the season.

The *Man'yōshū* contains poems addressed to crickets directly, as if they were beings capable of understanding address:

> ひとりする　床の下鳴く　草の虫
> 哀れいかなる　心なるらん
>
> *Hitori suru / toko no shita naku / kusa no mushi / aware ikanarum / kokoro naru ran*

*Beneath the bed / the small field insect chirps alone / sleeping alone — / what kind of heart, I wonder, / could it have?*

The poet attributes interiority to the cricket — wonders what kind of heart is behind that lone singing. The question is genuine. Not rhetorical. *What kind of heart?*

## A Poem for This Season

Matsuo Bashō left what is perhaps the definitive cricket haiku:

> きりぎりす　鳴くや霜夜の　さむしろに
> *Kirigirisu / naku ya shimoya no / samushiro ni*

*The cricket sings — / on this frosty night / on a cold, thin mat.*

The poem is spare and cold. The cricket's call, the frost, the thin sleeping mat — the three elements of a winter about to begin. *Samushiro* contains both *samu* (cold) and *mushiro* (rush mat), but the *samu* can also be read as simply cold — the poem puns on its own materials. The poet is lying on a mat, in the cold, listening to the cricket. The simplicity is total.

Bashō doesn't romanticize the cricket or the cold. He simply reports the conjunction, and leaves you with it. Here is the cricket. Here is the frost. Here is the thin mat. All three are real. What you make of that is up to you.

## How to Meet This Season

Leave a window or door slightly open this week, in the evening, and listen for the cricket.

If there is one near your house — near a foundation, under a porch, in the gap between garden and wall — you'll hear it. The call in mid-October is slower and more solitary than August's dense cricket chorus. It's distinct. Individual. You can often track it to a specific location.

Sit with it for a few minutes. Notice the rate of the chirping. Notice whether it changes. Notice that you're listening to the final weeks of a creature that has no idea you're on the other side of the door, no idea that what it's doing has been observed and named and written about in Japan for over a thousand years.

The cricket is at your door. The season is almost over. Let it sing.
