# The Sky's First Word of the Year

*Micro-season #12 of 72 — March 31–April 4*

---

The first spring thunder is almost always a surprise. Not because you didn't know it was coming — the sky had been building something for hours, the air felt different, the clouds had that particular anvil shape — but because you had not heard thunder in so long that when it arrives, your body responds before your mind can categorize the sound. A low, distant roll, felt as much as heard, coming from somewhere behind the hills.

Winter in Japan is thunder-free by and large. The silence of winter skies is part of their quality: clear and cold, or gray and snow-laden, but not electric. Then, in late March or very early April, the atmospheric conditions that generate thunderstorms begin to reassemble. The first one is tentative — a single roll of distant thunder, perhaps, nothing close, nothing dramatic. But it is unmistakable.

The 72-season calendar names this first thunder as its own micro-season. Not a thunderstorm. Not dramatic electrical phenomena. Just: the sky's voice beginning to return.

---

## 雷乃発声 — Kaminari sunawachi koe o hassu

Four characters (plus particles). 雷 (*kaminari*) is thunder — a character that, structurally, shows rain (雨) over a field (田). Thunder-over-the-fields: the deity that waters the rice paddies, in one interpretation. This character for thunder is layered with agricultural meaning even at the level of its visual construction.

乃 (*no* / *sunawachi*) is a classical particle meaning "therefore," or "that is," or "then" — creating a connection of logical implication. 発 (*hassu* / *hatsu*) means to emit, to send forth, to begin. 声 (*koe*) means voice, sound, the acoustic expression of something.

The full phrase might be rendered: "Thunder, that is, begins to voice itself" — or more naturally: "Thunder begins to sound." Or: "The sky finds its voice again."

The word *koe* — voice — is the most interesting choice here. It attributes to thunder a quality of intentional speech. The sky is not simply making noise. It is saying something. In the context of the agricultural calendar, what it is saying is: the season for water has begun. The rain that accompanies spring thunder is the rain that the rice paddies need.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Spring thunderstorms in Japan occur when warmer, moisture-laden air masses from the Pacific begin to push northward, encountering the still-cooler air over the interior of Honshu. The collision creates instability: warm air rises rapidly, cools, and condenses, forming the tall cumulonimbus clouds characteristic of thunderstorms.

The first spring thunderstorms are typically brief and not severe — the atmosphere is not yet warm enough to generate the intense convection of summer storms. They are more what the Japanese call *kaminari* in its most modest sense: a rumble, some rain, perhaps a brief flash. Enough to announce that the season has turned.

The Chinese calendar tradition, from which the Japanese system inherits this micro-season, explicitly associated this first thunder with the waking of hibernating creatures — the *jingzhe* (startling of insects) tradition we encountered in the seventh micro-season. The thunder startles the underground world into wakefulness. In Japan, the two phenomena are separated into their own micro-seasons — insects first, thunder later — but the connection remains implied.

The character for thunder (雷) showing rain over a field is not mere coincidence. In the agricultural tradition, spring thunder reliably preceded the planting rains. The farmer who heard the first thunder knew that rain was coming, that the paddies would be flooded soon, that the growing season was beginning in earnest.

---

## The Thunder God and the Rice Fields

*Raijin* (雷神), the Japanese thunder god, is typically depicted as a fierce, muscular figure surrounded by a ring of drums, beating out the sound of thunder with his mallets. He is often paired with Fujin (風神), the wind god. Together they appear in famous paintings at Kennin-ji temple in Kyoto and Taiyuin-byo in Nikko.

But Raijin's relationship to agriculture is gentler than his appearance suggests. In many regional traditions, he was understood as *ta no kami* — the god of the rice paddies — in one of his aspects. The thunder god who beats his drums in spring is announcing the water that the paddies need. His terrifying appearance is the terrifying appearance of a spring storm: dramatic, overwhelming in the moment, but ultimately necessary and beneficial.

In some rural communities, the first spring thunder was celebrated as a signal to begin paddy preparation. Farmers hearing the first thunder on the last day of March or the first days of April would understand it as permission to flood the fields.

## A Poem for This Season

Spring thunder (*haruikazuchi*, or simply *kaminari* as a spring kigo) appears in haiku as a sudden, clarifying disruption:

*春雷や / 逃げるものなき / 野の中に*

*Shunrai ya / nigeru mono naki / no no naka ni*

"Spring thunder — / nothing to escape to / in the middle of the plain."

The speaker is caught out in the open, and the storm comes. But there is something almost freeing in the image: in the middle of the plain, with nothing between you and the sky, the storm is complete. You are part of it. The thunder sounds, and there is nowhere else to be.

---

## How to Meet This Season

When the first spring thunder comes — and if you are in a temperate region, it will — stop what you are doing and go to a window or door. Count the seconds between the lightning flash and the thunder to estimate distance. Smell the air before and after: the ozone released by lightning gives the post-storm air a particular clean, electric quality called *petrichor*'s electric cousin.

Then think about the rice fields. Somewhere in Japan, a farmer is hearing this same sound and thinking about planting. The thunder is not just weather. It is a message from the season to the land.
