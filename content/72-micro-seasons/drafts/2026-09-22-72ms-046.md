# The Underground Year Begins

*Micro-season #46 of 72 — September 22 to September 27*

---

You don't see it happen. You just notice, one day, that the world below the surface is quieter.

The beetles that were working through the soil of the garden all summer are gone — or rather, they're there, but sealed away, pulling inward. The crickets that have been noisy through September are thinning. You walk along the edge of a rice paddy in the evening and hear, for the first time, that the sound has changed. There's still insect noise, but there's also space in it now. The thick, solid wall of summer's insect chorus has developed gaps.

Something has turned a knob.

The 72 micro-seasons say: this week, the insects are sealing their doors.

---

## 蟄虫坏戸 — Mushi Kakurete To o Fusagu

Four characters that tell a story of deliberate withdrawal.

*蟄* (chitsu, or kakurete) means to go into hiding underground. It's the character of hibernation, of things that burrow in and seal themselves away from the world. You'll see it in 啓蟄 (*keichitsu*) — the spring micro-season when "insects leave their underground dens" and emerge. This is its autumn counterpart: *returning* underground.

*虫* (mushi) is insect, or more broadly, small crawling creatures. In classical Japanese, *mushi* encompassed insects, worms, small reptiles, and anything small that crept. The category was broader than modern taxonomy suggests — anything small and living that moved through the lower world.

*坏* (fusagu) means to block, to seal, to close. It appears in words for obstruction and closure. Used with a door, it specifically means to bar the entrance — to close from the inside with intention.

*戸* (to) is door or gate — a threshold between inside and outside, between the underground world and the surface.

Together: *The insects hide, sealing their doors.* Not just retreating — sealing themselves in. The underground world closes its entrances. The surface world loses its connection to what moves below it.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Autumn equinox falls within or adjacent to this micro-season. Around September 23, day and night are equal in length, and then — for the first time since March — the nights become longer than the days.

For insects, daylength is one of the primary triggers for seasonal behavior. As nights lengthen, many species enter states of dormancy or diapause — a slowed metabolic state that allows them to survive winter. They do this in different ways: some overwinter as eggs, some as pupae, some as adults buried in soil or leaf litter or beneath bark. The mechanism varies, but the timing is similar across species.

What you stop hearing in late September is not just individual insects dying (though some do). It's the disappearance of the adult, above-ground stage. The insects are still there, in the soil, in the leaf litter, under the bark of trees. They've just sealed the door.

The equinox itself — *shūbun no hi* (秋分の日) in Japan, a public holiday — is associated with the Buddhist observance of *higan* (彼岸), which literally means "the other shore" — a reference to the state beyond death in Buddhist cosmology. During the week of the equinox, families visit graves, clean and offer flowers, and burn incense. The living move toward the dead; the border between realms is thought to thin.

The insects sealing their underground doors and the living visiting their underground dead happen at the same moment. The calendar holds both.

---

## The Spring Counterpart

This season echoes across the year.

The third micro-season, 啓蟄 (*keichitsu*), falls in early March and marks the moment "insects emerge from their underground dens." The earth opens its doors from below; the insects push up into spring light. That emergence is one of the most celebrated moments in the Japanese seasonal calendar — a signal that winter is finally, truly loosening its grip.

Here, the process runs in reverse. 蟄虫坏戸 closes the bracket that 啓蟄 opened. The insects that emerged in March — the beetles, the grasshoppers, the butterflies that were once caterpillars in their underground chrysalises — are returning now. The cycle completes.

What strikes me about this pairing is the symmetry. The 72 micro-seasons are not simply a list of observations. They form a structure, a shape. The year is not a line but a circle, and certain moments on the circle are mirrors of each other. Spring's opening corresponds to autumn's closing. The swallows' arrival mirrors their departure. The frogs that begin to sing in May will eventually go silent.

The structure helps you locate yourself. You know where you are in the year not just by date but by narrative position.

## A Poem for This Season

The autumn equinox and higan week are associated with the *higanbana* (彼岸花) — the cluster amaryllis (*Lycoris radiata*), also called the spider lily. These flowers bloom with extraordinary precision in Japan, typically within a few days of the equinox, and their bright red blossoms appear in graveyards, along rice paddy edges, in places associated with the boundary between life and death.

One of the most haunting haiku about higanbana comes from Kobayashi Issa:

> 曼珠沙華　一本一本　散ってゆく
> *Manjushage / ippon ippon / chitte yuku*

*Spider lilies — / one by one / falling away.*

*Manjushage* is the Sanskrit name for the higanbana — Buddhist terminology for a sacred flower, one that appears in descriptions of the Pure Land. Issa watches the flowers fall — one by one, a slow subtraction. The poem sits with the equinox week, with higan, with the sense that the living and the dead are momentarily closer than usual.

The insects are sealing their doors. The spider lilies are falling. The ancestors are near.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Find the equinox this week, and go outside at the exact moment when day and night are equal.

Then look at the ground.

Not at the sky, not at the trees — at the soil, at the grass, at wherever small things live. Try to imagine what's happening below the surface: the slow withdrawal, the sealing in, the metabolic settling. The underground world turning inward for its long season of waiting.

If you're in Japan during higan week, visit a graveyard — not as a tourist but as a participant, even loosely. Bring something. Leave something. The custom of higan is about maintaining the connection between the living and the dead, which is also the connection between what is above ground and what is below.

The doors are closing. The underground year has begun.
