# The Reed Remembers Water

*Micro-season #16 of 72 — April 20–24*

---

At the edge of the river, where the water is shallower and the silty bottom holds the last of the winter's cold, something is pushing through. Thin, pointed, slightly waxy — not yet the tall green wall the reeds will become by July, but the first inch or two of growth, the first green gesture from the waterside. The reed is remembering that it is a reed.

The common reed (*Phragmites australis*), called *ashi* or *yoshi* in Japanese, is among the most widespread plants on Earth. It grows on every continent except Antarctica, in freshwater marshes, river banks, lake edges, estuaries, rice paddy margins, and anywhere that water sits for part of the year. In Japan, it is inseparably associated with the flat, watery landscape of river mouths and lowland plains — the kind of landscape that was once far more extensive before modern land reclamation.

The 16th micro-season of the year is named for the reed's first appearance of the season. After a winter as dry brown stalks bent by wind and holding seed heads like disheveled brushes, the reed sends its first new growth upward through the water. This is its announcement that the warm season has truly begun at the waterline.

---

## 葭始生 — Ashi hajimete shōzu

Three characters. 葭 (*yoshi* / *ashi*) is the reed — one of those plants with two names that reflect regional variation: in the Osaka area it was *ashi*, in the Tokyo area *yoshi*, and the calendar uses a character that can be read both ways. 始 (*hajimete*) means "for the first time." 生 (*shōzu* / *shō*) means to grow, to be born, to emerge into life.

The full phrase: "Reeds grow for the first time." Or: "The reeds begin to sprout."

This is the first micro-season of the Kokuu solar term — 穀雨, "grain rain," the rain that helps the grain grow. The agricultural significance is clear: the rains of late April fall on newly flooded paddy fields and on the broader wetland ecosystem. The reed's sprouting is part of the same story as the rice seedlings that will appear in the next micro-season.

The name of this solar term (*Kokuu*) tells you what the rain is doing: falling on grain. The reed's appearance tells you where the rain is falling: on the edge of the water, where the boundary between land and river dissolves.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Common reeds (*Phragmites australis*) spread primarily by underground rhizomes — horizontal root-stems that extend through the substrate, producing new shoots at intervals. After a winter during which the above-ground growth dies back, the rhizomes survive in the mud, storing the energy that will fuel the next season's growth.

In mid- to late April, when water temperatures rise above approximately 10°C (50°F) and day length triggers growth hormones, the rhizomes begin sending up new shoots. These early shoots are fast-growing — in good conditions, a reed can grow several centimeters per day in May and June, eventually reaching two to four meters in height by late summer.

The ecology of reed beds is complex and rich. A single reed bed can support dozens of species: marsh harriers nest in the reeds, warblers and buntings breed in the taller growth, herons hunt the margins, dragonflies and damselflies lay their eggs on reed stems. The reed is not just a plant; it is a habitat.

In Japan, the reed beds of river mouths and coastal wetlands — some of the most important wildlife habitats in the country — are now greatly reduced from their historical extent due to land reclamation. The reed's appearance in this micro-season carries a slight elegiac quality: a plant that was once so ubiquitous that it gave its name to Japan itself.

---

## Ashi, Yoshi, and the Name of Japan

The reed's role in Japanese cultural identity is ancient and profound. One of the oldest names for Japan is *Ashihara no Nakatsukuni* — "The Middle Land of the Reed Plains." The poem that opens the *Nihon Shoki* (720 CE), Japan's second oldest chronicle, uses this name to describe the archipelago before the gods shaped it into its final form.

The switch from *ashi* to *yoshi* in some regional uses is itself a linguistic curiosity. *Ashi* sounds like the word for "bad" (悪し, *ashi*), while *yoshi* sounds like the word for "good" (良し, *yoshi*). In the Osaka region, merchants and townspeople who were sensitive to auspicious language preferred *yoshi* for this reason. Both names persist, and the calendar character can be read either way.

In Japanese architecture, reed thatch was used for the roofs of farmhouses and shrines — including, notably, the inner sanctuary of Ise Jingu, Japan's most sacred Shinto site, whose roof is traditionally thatched with reeds from nearby wetlands. The reed, growing from water, became the material that touched the sacred.

Reed flutes appear in the oldest Japanese musical traditions. *Ashi no fue* — the reed flute — was played in *gagaku*, the ancient court music, and the image of wind through reeds appears throughout Japanese poetry as an image of the melancholy, searching quality of autumn sound.

## A Poem for This Season

The reed appears in the *Man'yōshū* in a poem attributed to Hitomaro (c. 7th–8th century) that uses the reed bed as a landscape for longing:

*葦原の / みずほの国は / 神ながら / 言挙げせぬ国*

This is a formal text about Japan's sacred nature, not a personal poem. For a more intimate image of the reed's spring emergence, we turn to a later haiku by Masaoka Shiki:

*葦の芽の / 水面に出でし / 春の雨*

*Ashi no me no / minomo ni ideshi / haru no ame*

"The reed tips / emerging above the water's surface — / spring rain."

Shiki's poem places the reed's first appearance in its natural context: the rain of the Kokuu season, falling on the water from which the new growth emerges. The reed tips barely visible above the surface, the rain creating rings around them — this is exactly the image the micro-season describes.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Find water with a shallow margin — a river bank, a lake edge, a pond, the margin of a wetland. Look for the first reed shoots emerging: they will be thin, pointed, slightly waxy, in clusters where the water is not too deep. They may be only a few centimeters above the waterline.

If you can, return to the same spot over several weeks. The growth rate of reeds in spring is dramatic, and watching a reed bed transition from bare water to dense green wall over the course of April and May is one of the more quietly spectacular seasonal transformations.
