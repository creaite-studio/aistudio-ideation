# The Stream That Has Gone All the Way

*Micro-season #70 of 72 — January 26 to January 30*

---

By late January, the ice on a mountain stream in Japan has had weeks to build.

It starts at the edges, where the water is shallow and slows near the bank. A thin crystal rim appears, delicate, transparent. Then each cold night adds another layer — the rim thickens, advances toward the center, until the sound of the stream is buried under silence, muffled by an inch, two inches, three inches of solid ice. Stand on the bank and look at what used to be moving water, and see instead a white, opaque surface, dented by footprints, catching the flat light of a January afternoon.

But underneath — this is what the season knows — the water is still moving.

The ice is thick now. It has reached its maximum density for the year. But it has not stopped the stream. A winter stream in Japan is not a frozen lake; it is a living thing wearing a shell. Under the ice, the current continues. The fish are there, slowed, conserving energy, but present. The water runs toward the sea.

---

## 水沢腹堅 — Sawamizu Kōri Tsumeru

The full poetic reading of this season is *sawamizu kōri tsumeru* — "stream-water accumulates ice."

*Sawa* (沢) is a mountain stream, specifically — a fast-moving, clear stream running through a ravine or valley. It's the kind of stream that you can step across, that makes a sound you can hear from fifty meters away in summer, that tumbles over smooth stones worn round by decades of current. Not a river, not a pond: a *sawa*.

*Sui* (水) — water.

*Fuku* (腹) is one of the most unexpected characters in this season's name. It means belly, abdomen, the interior of something. When used metaphorically, it means "thoroughly," "all the way through," "to the core." *Mizusawa fukuken* — the stream's belly is hard. Not the surface, not the edges: the center, the interior, the very core of the stream has become firm with ice.

*Ken* (堅) means hard, firm, solid. The character shows a hand grasping something tightly, combined with the earth radical.

The belly of the stream-water has gone hard. The cold has penetrated to the interior.

---

## What's Actually Happening

*Daikan* (大寒) — Greater Cold — the final major solar term of winter, runs from approximately January 20 to February 3. This micro-season falls in its second week, which is statistically the coldest period of the year in most of Japan.

What "greater cold" means in physical terms is that the accumulated cold of winter has had its maximum time to work downward. Air temperatures have been below freezing on many nights for weeks or months. The soil is at its coldest. The ponds are at their thickest ice. And running water, which resists freezing because of its movement, finally accumulates enough ice to form a substantial surface cover.

Ice formation in flowing water works differently than in still water. In ponds and lakes, ice forms from the top down as the surface water cools, becomes less dense, and eventually freezes. In moving streams, the process is more complicated: the current keeps mixing the water column, making uniform cooling difficult. Ice instead forms as *frazil ice* — tiny disk-shaped crystals that form throughout the water column and accumulate in sheltered spots — before eventually consolidating into a surface layer.

The ice of late January in a mountain stream has had weeks to build and consolidate. In some streams, it can reach ten or fifteen centimeters in thickness. The belly of the stream is hard.

Traditional industries in Japan used this maximum winter cold deliberately. The production of *kōji* mold (*Aspergillus oryzae*), essential for making sake, miso, and soy sauce, benefits from cold: *kanzukuri* (寒造り), "cold-season brewing," produces sake of higher quality because the low temperatures slow fermentation and allow more complex flavors to develop. The *Daikan* period was the traditional time for beginning the most premium sake brewing precisely because the cold was at its deepest and most reliable.

Similarly, *kankiriboshi daikon* (寒切り干し大根) — daikon radishes dried in the cold of *Daikan* — were considered the finest quality, dried quickly and cleanly by the combination of cold and dry winter air.

## The Frozen Moment Before the Turn

There is a phrase in Japanese: *donzoko* (どん底) — the absolute bottom, the very lowest point. *Daikan* is the *donzoko* of the thermal year. After this week, the trend turns.

It will not be obvious for a while. February is still cold. March can still surprise with late snow. But the trajectory — measured in the accumulating light, in the minute-by-minute lengthening of days — has been pointing toward spring since December 22. By late January, even in the coldest weeks, experienced observers can feel something shifting in the quality of the light.

In Japanese traditional culture, *Daikan* was treated not just as the coldest time but as a kind of threshold. *Kangeiko* (寒稽古) — cold-season training — was practiced in martial arts, music, and meditation during this period. The logic was that working at maximum difficulty forges maximum resilience: the practitioner who trained through the *donzoko* of winter would carry that resilience through the year.

*Kan-no-iri* (寒の入り) marks the entry into *Daikan*; *kan-no-ake* (寒の明け) marks its end on February 3rd, the same day as *Setsubun* (節分), the traditional bean-throwing festival that clears out evil spirits before spring's *Risshun* (立春) the following day.

The ice is thickest now. And in being thickest, it has reached its limit. It cannot get thicker than this. From here, it can only thaw.

## A Poem for This Season

Yosa Buson, one of the 18th century's great haiku masters, captured the particular quality of the *Daikan* cold:

> 春立つや　新年ふるき　米五升
> *haru tatsu ya / shinnen furuki / kome go shō*

This is actually a *Risshun* (beginning of spring) poem about the moment the new season starts. For the ice poem, the great winter poet Kobayashi Issa is more appropriate:

> 大寒の　真っ只中よ　小綬鶏
> *daikan no / ma-tada-naka yo / kojukei*

*Right in the middle*
*of the great cold —*
*a bamboo partridge calls.*

The poem is spare and surprising. *Daikan* — Greater Cold — is at its exact center: *ma-tada-naka*, "the very middle of the very middle." And in this absolute moment, a small bird calls. The bamboo partridge (*kojukei*) has a distinctive piping call, bright and persistent. In the deepest cold, the bird calls anyway.

Issa, who lost his mother as an infant and suffered remarkable hardship throughout his life, had a particular gift for finding small, stubborn vitality in harsh conditions. The bird calling in the heart of *Daikan* is a pure expression of his sensibility: not optimism, not denial — just the fact that something small and alive is here, in the coldest moment, and making noise.

## How to Meet This Season

This week is the last week before the cold formally begins to turn. It is, in some sense, the bottom of the year's cold arc.

Go to ice, if you can find it. Not managed ice — not a skating rink — but natural ice forming on still or slow-moving water. Look at it closely: the texture of the surface, the way it has clouded and changed from the first transparent pane of early winter. Look at the edges where ice meets liquid water — the active frontier, where freezing is still occurring. Listen to the sounds a freezing body of water makes: the ticks and cracks as thermal stress moves through the ice.

Then, if you're near a partially frozen stream, notice the sound of water underneath. Kneel close to the ice surface and listen. You may hear it: the muffled sound of the current continuing below, the stream that is still moving inside its shell.

The belly of the stream is hard. But the stream is still there.

The cold has gone as far as it goes. Something has been reached. Something is about to begin its long way back.
