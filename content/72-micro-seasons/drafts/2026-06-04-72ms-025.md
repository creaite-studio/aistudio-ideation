# Miniature Predators, Perfectly Made

*Micro-season #25 of 72 — June 4 to June 8*

---

They arrive from foam.

Sometime in the autumn before, a female praying mantis attached an egg case to a twig or fence post or blade of tall grass. The case — called an *ootheca* — looks like nothing in particular: a small, light-brown mass of hardened foam, about the size of a quail's egg, unremarkable enough to be missed entirely by anyone not looking for it. It spent the winter there, through rain and frost, waiting.

And now, in early June, the waiting ends.

On a warm morning this week, from hundreds of such cases across the countryside, tiny mantises will begin to emerge — dozens from a single ootheca, each one a few millimeters long, perfectly formed, blade-arms folded, already looking for something to eat. They will scatter into the summer world within minutes. Many won't survive the day.

---

## 蟷螂生 — Kamakiri Shōzu

The name of this micro-season carries a double history.

蟷螂 (*tōrō*, or in this reading, *kamakiri*) is the classical term for praying mantis, imported from Chinese. In everyday modern Japanese, the creature is simply called *kamakiri*, which is the native word — and it reveals something about how the Japanese historically saw this insect.

*Kama* (鎌) means sickle. *Kiri* (切り) means to cut. The mantis's common name is, literally, *sickle-cutter*, a name that focuses entirely on those raptorial forelegs — the folded, serrated arms that snap closed on prey with extraordinary speed. The Chinese loanword *tōrō* contains the character for mantis alongside the character for louse; the Japanese name focuses on the weapon. These are two entirely different ways of seeing the same animal.

生 (*shōzu*) means to be born, to emerge, to appear.

*Praying mantises are born.* June, early, when the world is wet and green.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The praying mantis is a sit-and-wait predator of remarkable efficiency.

Unlike insects that patrol or chase, the mantis holds still — sometimes for hours — in a posture that, from a distance, can look like a praying human figure, which is how it got its English name. It waits for prey to come to it. When something edible passes within range, the strike takes approximately 30 to 50 milliseconds. For reference: the blink of a human eye takes about 150 milliseconds.

In Japan, the species most commonly encountered is *Tenodera sinensis*, the Chinese mantis, and *Tenodera angustipennis*, the narrow-winged mantis. Both are large — females can reach 10 centimeters — and both are voracious enough to take on prey considerably larger than themselves, including large beetles, cicadas, and — famously — small frogs and lizards.

For farmers and gardeners, mantises are unambiguous allies. They consume vast quantities of agricultural pests throughout the summer: aphids, caterpillars, beetles, flies. A garden with healthy mantis populations is a garden managing itself.

The ecological timing of the June hatching is precise. By early June, insect prey is abundant. The wheat has been harvested, the rice paddies are flooded and teeming with life, the warm rains of *tsuyu* are approaching. The new mantises hatch into a world that is, quite literally, at peak edibility.

---

## The Sickle in the Name

Japanese has an interesting relationship with bladed tools as metaphors.

The *kama* (sickle) in *kamakiri* is the same word as in the place name Kamakura — the seaside city south of Tokyo that served as Japan's seat of samurai government for over a century. The sickle was an agricultural tool of course, but also a symbol of the harvest, of the turning of seasons, of the decisive cut that separates the ready grain from the stalk.

To name an insect after this tool is to see it in a particular way: as something that cuts, that decides, that acts with precision and finality. Mantises don't hesitate. They wait, then they move, and then it is done.

There is something in Japanese aesthetics — particularly the samurai traditions and the Zen that influenced them — that admires this quality. The decisive action. The absence of wasted motion. You can see why a culture that produced *iaijutsu* (the art of drawing and cutting in a single motion) might find the mantis, of all insects, worth naming and studying.

Kobayashi Issa wrote of the mantis with his characteristic mixture of tenderness and irony:

> 蟷螂の　ねらいのろのろ　隙をまつ
> *Kamakiri no / nerai noro noro / suki wo matsu*

*The mantis, slowly, slowly —*
*taking aim,*
*waiting for its opening.*

Issa sees the patience as much as the speed. The long, still waiting. The unhurried calm that precedes the instant of action.

## A Poem for This Season

The hatching itself — that emergence from foam — has its own quiet drama that no classical poet seems to have captured directly, though the mantis as an adult figure appears regularly in summer haiku.

Issa, who wrote of small, overlooked lives with more consistency than any other poet of his era, returned to the mantis several times. In one:

> 蟷螂に　なめられたかな　親指
> *Kamakiri ni / namerareta ka na / oyayubi*

*Did the mantis*
*just lick*
*my thumb?*

The poem captures something characteristic: the sudden intimacy of an encounter with a wild thing, the slight absurdity of a giant (from the mantis's perspective) being attended to by this small, precise creature. Issa doesn't draw a lesson. He just reports what happened, with that final *kana* hanging open, full of unspent surprise.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Late June through September, you may find mantises throughout Japan — in gardens, on fence posts, waiting in herb plants where small insects congregate. They are rarely afraid of humans and will often turn their triangular heads to watch you with what feels, improbably, like genuine curiosity.

If you find one this week — small, just-hatched, barely the length of your fingernail — observe it for a moment before moving on. Notice the way it holds itself. Notice the arms.

In early June, the countryside is preparing for the first rains of *tsuyu*, the rainy season. Everything is green and growing a little too fast. The mantis hatches into all of this prepared, from birth, for what comes next. There is something almost admirable about arriving in the world knowing exactly what your tools are for.
