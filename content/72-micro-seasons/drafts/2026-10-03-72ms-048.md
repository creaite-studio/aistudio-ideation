# Forty Days From Siberia

*Micro-season #48 of 72 — October 3 to October 7*

---

They arrive without announcement.

One evening you look up at a wetland or a large lake and the birds are there — hundreds of them, sometimes thousands, whitefronts and bean geese moving through the shallows, feeding, resting, conducting the elaborate negotiations of migrating flocks. They weren't there yesterday. They are there today.

They have traveled approximately 3,000 to 4,000 kilometers from their breeding grounds in Siberia and the Arctic. They've crossed mountain ranges, marshes, river systems, areas of human habitation that didn't exist when their species first made this journey. They flew at night, navigating by stars and magnetic fields and the accumulated memory of their kind. And now they're in Japan, in a lake in Aomori or Ibaraki or Shimane, feeding on fallen grain in harvested fields, preparing for another winter at the southern end of their range.

The 72 micro-seasons have a name for this moment: 鴻雁来. The wild geese arrive.

---

## 鴻雁来 — Kōgan Kitaru

Two geese characters and an arrival.

*鴻* (kō) refers specifically to the large wild goose — the bean goose or related species. It's the larger of the two birds named in the season title, the one whose size and presence made it impressive to ancient observers.

*雁* (gan, or kari) is the more general character for wild goose. This is the *kigo* word — the season word — used in haiku to indicate autumn. *Kari* appears in the title of famous woodblock prints, in classical poems, in the image of geese flying across a full moon that recurs throughout Japanese art from the Heian period onward.

*来* (kitaru) is to come, to arrive, to approach. The same verb structure as *kuru* but more formal, more literary — the geese don't just show up, they arrive with weight, with announcement.

And this season has a mirror, earlier in the year. The 14th micro-season, 鴻雁北 (*kōgan kitaguni e kaeru*, or *kōgan kita e kaeru*) — the wild geese head north — falls in mid-April. The geese left Japan in spring; they return now. The year's other great migration bracket closes.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Japan's wintering waterfowl come from two main population sources: Siberia and Arctic Russia to the northeast, and the northern island regions. The white-fronted goose (*Anser albifrons*) is the most numerous species at many Japanese wintering sites. Bean geese (*Anser fabalis*) are also significant visitors.

The birds breed in summer on the Arctic tundra and subarctic taiga, raising their young through the brief northern summer. As daylight shortens in August and September, the trigger comes: body fat accumulates, restlessness increases — the phenomenon called *Zugunruhe* in the research literature, from German, literally "the restlessness of migration." The birds gather in staging areas, practice their V-formations, and depart.

The V-formation is not merely aesthetic. The bird at the apex of the formation breaks wind resistance and creates upwash from its wingtip vortex, which reduces the energy expenditure of birds flying slightly behind and to the side. The lead position rotates. The formation is a cooperative structure that allows the whole flock to travel further than any individual could alone.

When they arrive in Japan, they settle primarily in lowland wetlands and agricultural areas. Harvested rice fields — flooded again in some cases, or simply offering fallen grain — are important feeding sites. The drained and harvested paddies of the previous micro-season become staging grounds for the arriving geese.

## The Image That Persists

Few images in Japanese art are more recognizable than geese flying across the moon.

The combination — *kari* (geese), *tsuki* (moon) — became a standard compositional motif across all periods of Japanese art. Hiroshige's woodblock print "Wild Geese Flying under the Full Moon" is one of the most reproduced images in the Edo-period canon. The pairing appears in lacquerware, in fabric patterns, in screen paintings, in haiku. The moon of October is the harvest moon, one of the most celebrated of the year; the geese crossing it are the signature of autumn itself.

Why this image? I think partly because it captures something true about the scale of migration — the birds against the sky's largest object, making the journey visible. And partly because it captures the contrast: the stable, enormous moon; the moving, fragile, purposeful birds. Permanence and passage. The moon is always there. The geese pass through once a year and then they're gone.

Bashō wrote the most famous goose-and-moon haiku:

> 海は暗し　鴨の声　ほのかに白し
> *Umi wa kurashi / kamo no koe / honoka ni shiroshi*

*The sea is dark — / the voice of the wild ducks / faintly white.*

This is synesthesia — the voice of birds described as "faintly white." The darkness and the pale calls crossing the water. The birds are heard but not seen; their presence is registered as sound in the dark.

## A Poem for This Season

The Manyōshū contains several dozen poems about wild geese, and many of them are about the moment of arrival — hearing the birds at night, the calls coming from darkness before the birds themselves are visible.

One of the most direct:

> 秋萩に　うらびれをれば　飽田河
> 鴻鳴き渡る　夕闇の中
>
> *Aki hagi ni / urabire oreba / Akuta kawa / kō naki wataru / yūyami no naka*

*Feeling lonely / among the autumn bush clover — / over the Akuta River / wild geese calling / through the dusk.*

The poem places you in a specific emotional landscape — loneliness, the autumn clover — and then, through the geese calling, opens it upward. The birds crossing overhead expand the scene. Whatever was small and interior becomes part of a larger movement. The geese don't care about your loneliness; they pass through it with their own vast purpose.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Check wetland reports this week if you're in Japan. The timing of goose arrivals varies by location — northern sites in Hokkaido and Tohoku receive birds earlier; central Honshu sites may not see peak numbers until November. But the first arrivals are already moving now.

Large lakes with shallow margins, slow-moving rivers with gravel bars, flooded agricultural fields: these are the sites. Dawn and dusk are the best times.

Listen first. Geese are loud. The calls travel further than you expect. You may hear them before you see them, coming out of the sky in the early dark, exactly as the Manyōshū poets heard them 1,300 years ago. Same birds. Same calls. Same October sky.
