# The Rain That Smells of Plums

*Micro-season #27 of 72 — June 14 to June 20*

---

June in Japan smells a particular way.

It's not the clean, ozone smell of a thunderstorm, and it's not the earthiness of wet soil after summer rain. It's something more organic, more insistent — a smell that has body to it, that clings to clothes and hair and the air inside train cars. It is the smell of *tsuyu*, the rainy season, and underneath it, if you're paying attention, the faint, green-fruit sweetness of plums that are not yet quite ripe.

In my neighborhood, there is a ume tree in someone's front yard. I walk past it most mornings on the way to the station. For most of the year, I barely notice it. But in June, the tree becomes impossible to ignore: the fruit hangs heavy, hard and green at first, then beginning to blush toward yellow as the weeks pass, and the smell of it — even through the rain, even through the grey — is extraordinary. Sweet and sharp and alive.

The old calendar has a name for this moment.

---

## 梅子黄 — Ume no Mi Kibamu

Three characters, two of which carry the whole cultural weight.

梅 (*ume*) is the Japanese plum — technically closer to an apricot (*Prunus mume*) than to what Europeans call a plum, but the translation has stuck. The character itself has been associated with refinement and resilience in East Asian aesthetics for over a thousand years: the ume blooms in late winter, often while snow still covers the ground, and is thus a symbol of perseverance and early beauty.

子 (*mi*, here, or *ko*) means fruit or child — in this compound, specifically the fruit of the tree. The ume no mi is the plum fruit, as opposed to the ume no hana (plum blossom) celebrated in late winter.

黄 (*kibamu*) means to turn yellow — not just yellow, but the specific warm gold of ripening fruit. The verb form *kibamu* implies a process, a turning: the plum is not yellow yet, but it is yellowing.

*The plum fruit turns yellow.* A small event, a private color change happening in yards and along country lanes. But the timing matters enormously, because the plum and the rain arrive together.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The word for Japan's rainy season is *tsuyu* (梅雨). Written with the characters for plum and rain, it translates literally as "plum rain."

This is not coincidence. *Tsuyu* arrives in southern Japan in early June and moves northward through the month, bringing weeks of cloudy skies and persistent rain caused by a stationary front between cold northern air masses and warm, moisture-laden southern air. The front stalls over the archipelago and simply pours.

The plums ripen during this rain. They need the moisture — the sustained wetness is part of what makes them swell and soften, reaching their full weight and size. The earliest harvests of *ao-ume* (green plums) happen in May; by mid-June, the fruit is changing color, softening slightly, developing the complex acidity that makes it valuable. By late June, the harvest is urgent: leave them too long and they over-ripen, fall, bruise, and ferment on the ground.

This is the moment families across Japan make their annual pantry. Plums go into salt in ceramic crocks, beginning the months-long process that will produce *umeboshi*, the intensely sour, salty preserved plums that have been a fixture of Japanese cuisine for over a thousand years. Other fruits go into jars with rock sugar to produce *umeshu*, the sweet plum liqueur that is June's characteristic domestic fragrance: sweet and medicinal and quietly anticipatory.

---

## Ume Shigoto: The Work of Plums

There is a phrase in Japanese — *ume shigoto* — that means, roughly, "plum work."

It refers to the annual domestic labor of processing the ume harvest: washing the fruit, removing the stems with a toothpick, weighing and salting, placing in crocks under heavy stones, waiting for the brine to rise. Later, when *tsuyu* ends and the first clear days arrive, the umeboshi are laid out on bamboo mats to dry in the sun, a process called *doyo boshi* — three-day drying. The red shiso leaves that give traditional umeboshi their color are added in layers. The crocks are sealed again.

The whole process, start to finish, takes months. And at every stage, it requires patience that is calibrated precisely to the season — you cannot rush the brine, you cannot force the drying, you cannot skip the waiting.

*Ume shigoto* is a kind of living in sync with the calendar that is increasingly rare. Most people now buy their umeboshi at the supermarket, where they are uniform and predictable and available in January if you want them. The homemade version, by contrast, is never the same twice.

## A Poem for This Season

The ume appears in Japanese poetry more often in its blossom form than as fruit — the winter-blooming flower is the classic image. But a few poets caught the moment of the ripening itself.

This tanka from the classical tradition:

> 梅の実の　熟るるとともに　降る雨は
> 恵みの雨か　悲しみの雨か
> *Ume no mi no / ururu to tomo ni / furu ame wa*
> *megumi no ame ka / kanashimi no ame ka*

*The rain that falls*
*as the plum fruit ripens —*
*is it a rain of blessing,*
*or a rain of grief?*

The ambivalence is genuine. *Tsuyu* is both: the rain that swells the plum, that fills the rice paddies, that the whole summer harvest depends on — and the rain that never stops, that molds the tatami, that keeps children indoors, that wraps the country in grey for weeks.

Japan has always held both valences of the rainy season without resolving them. The blessing and the grief are the same rain.

---

## How to Meet This Season

If you're in Japan in June: look for green plums at the market. They're sold by the kilo, sometimes in net bags, often at farm stands and grocery stores in the produce section near the pickled goods. They smell faintly, barely — you have to hold one close to catch it.

Buy some. Make the simplest version of *umeshu*: wash the fruit, prick each one with a toothpick, layer with rock sugar in a clean jar, cover with *shōchū* or cheap vodka. Seal it. Put it somewhere dark for three months.

You won't drink it until September, or perhaps the autumn after that. But you will have participated in something — a piece of seasonal labor whose logic is older than the word for it — that connects you to every household in Japan that has made the same jar, in the same grey light, in the same month, for the past thousand years.
