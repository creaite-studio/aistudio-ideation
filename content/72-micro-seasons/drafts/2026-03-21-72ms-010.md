# Under the Eaves, a Beginning

*Micro-season #10 of 72 — March 21–25*

---

You will hear it before you see it: a quick, purposeful rustling at the gap between the roof tile and the rafter. A brief pause. Then, if you wait without moving, a small brown bird emerges, looks at you with one eye, and disappears again inside. The sparrow has decided.

The Eurasian tree sparrow (*Passer montanus*) — the *suzume* of Japanese poetry and everyday life — has been living alongside human settlements for so long that the two populations have become mutually adapted. The sparrow builds under human eaves because human eaves are perfect: sheltered from rain, elevated from predators, structurally ready to accept a small nest's architecture. In return — though it does not work this way consciously — the human gets a bird. Gets the cheeping from the roofline in the morning. Gets the sight of a small creature carrying something, always slightly too large for its beak, toward a gap in the tiles.

The tenth micro-season of the year arrives with the spring equinox (Shunbun) and names this specific activity: the sparrow's beginning. Not just the sparrow's presence — the sparrow has been here all winter — but its purposeful beginning of work.

---

## 雀始巣 — Suzume hajimete sukū

Three characters. 雀 (*suzume*) is the sparrow — one of the most recognizable bird characters in Japanese, a small square of feathers implied in the strokes. 始 (*hajimete*) means "for the first time," or "beginning to." 巣 (*sukū* / *su*) means nest — the cup-shaped or cavity construction where birds lay their eggs and raise their young.

The full phrase: "The sparrow begins to nest." The verb *sukū* implies not just the presence of the nest but the act of nest-building: gathering materials, selecting and rejecting, fitting things together, making something that did not exist before from pieces of the surrounding world.

This is the first micro-season of the Shunbun solar term — the vernal equinox, the day when day and night are equal length, the calendrical midpoint of spring. To begin this solar term with the sparrow's nesting is a choice that makes sense: the equinox is a moment of beginning, of balance tipping toward longer days, and the sparrow's nest is among the most visible signs that something new is being built.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Eurasian tree sparrows breed throughout Japan except in the far north, arriving in agricultural villages and cities where they exploit the built environment for nesting. Unlike many species that are sensitive to human disturbance, the tree sparrow has adapted to thrive in close proximity to people. It nests in cavities: the gaps between roof tiles and wooden beams (*noki no sukima*), holes in stone walls, nest boxes, and occasionally in the old nests of larger birds.

Nest construction begins in March, timed to the increasing day length and temperature. The male and female both participate in gathering materials: dry grass, plant fibers, feathers, strips of bark, and — in urban and suburban Japan — scraps of paper, bits of string, hair. The nest is a loose-woven cup lined with softer materials, tucked into the cavity with more material around it for insulation.

The sparrow's dependence on human structures means that its nesting season is partly a history of human architecture. Old farmhouses with their deep, overhanging tiled roofs and complex timber structures offered the sparrow many opportunities; modern concrete construction offers fewer. The presence of a sparrow nest under your eaves is now, in some areas, a sign that your building retains some of the character of older construction — that there are gaps and cavities worth having.

## The Sparrow in Japanese Culture

The sparrow is perhaps the most democratic bird in the Japanese cultural tradition. Unlike the crane, which symbolizes aristocratic elegance, or the plover, which appears in medieval warrior poetry, the sparrow is the bird of ordinary life. It appears in *haiku*, in *waka*, in folk tales, in proverbs — always as the familiar, unremarkable, deeply present presence at the edge of human life.

The proverb *suzume hyaku made odori wasurezu* — "a sparrow doesn't forget its dance until it's a hundred years old" — suggests both the difficulty of changing deep habits and the persistence of innate character. It is usually used somewhat ruefully, about the difficulty of changing one's ways. But it also honors the sparrow's consistency: the sparrow was a sparrow last year and will be a sparrow again this year.

In the folk tale *Shita-kiri Suzume* ("Tongue-cut Sparrow"), a kind old man befriends a sparrow that a greedy neighbor injures. The tale ends with the kind man rewarded and the greedy one punished — but what persists in the cultural memory is the sparrow's loyalty and the image of the man who talked to birds, who noticed a small hurt thing and helped it.

Sparrows appear on countless items of traditional craftsmanship: noren (split curtains), furoshiki (wrapping cloths), ceramics, and the family crests (*kamon*) of several Japanese clans. The city of Sendai has used the sparrow as its symbol, honoring the bird's long co-habitation with human settlement.

## A Poem for This Season

The sparrow (*suzume*) as a seasonal word (kigo) for spring appears throughout the haiku tradition. Bashō wrote with characteristic simplicity:

*雀の子 / そこのけそこのけ / お馬が通る*

*Suzume no ko / sokonoke sokonoke / ouma ga tōru*

"Baby sparrows — / move, move out of the way — / a great horse is coming!"

The image is comic and tender at once. The young sparrows, newly fledged and not yet wise about the world, sit in the road while something large approaches. Bashō does not specify whether they move in time. The uncertainty is part of the poem: the small and the large, the careless and the urgent, sharing a road through spring.

## How to Meet This Season

Look up. Specifically, look at the gaps between roof tiles, at the holes in old walls, at the places where a building's exterior shows signs of small habitation: a bit of grass sticking out, a telltale accumulation of droppings below a gap, the occasional quick flutter of wings disappearing into shadow.

Once you have found a sparrow's nest site, you can follow the season from that single vantage point: the nest building now, the quiet period of incubation in a few weeks, the burst of activity when the nestlings hatch and the parents begin the endless work of feeding, and then — in early summer — the first improbable fledglings, sitting in the road, not yet sure what the world expects of them.
