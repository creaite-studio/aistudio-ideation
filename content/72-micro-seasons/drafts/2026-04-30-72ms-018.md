# The King of Flowers Arrives Last

*Micro-season #18 of 72 — April 30–May 4*

---

The cherry blossoms are long gone. The peach blossoms too, and the plum before them. The whole arc of spring flowers — from the plum's austere winter blooming to the frenzied pink of cherry season to the quiet warmth of the peach — has played out over the preceding two months. And now, at the very end of spring, after all the more celebrated flowers have had their moment, the peony opens.

It does not apologize for arriving late. It has no reason to. The tree peony (*Paeonia suffruticosa*), called *botan* in Japanese, is the largest, most elaborate, most architecturally complex flower of the spring garden. Its blooms can reach the size of a dinner plate. The petals are layered and ruffled, in colors ranging from the deepest crimson through magenta, pink, pale blush, and white so pure it seems to generate its own light. The scent is rich and complex.

The ancient calendar names the peony's bloom as the final micro-season of spring. After 18 five-day chapters — from the first loosening of ice in early February to this moment — spring ends with the most extravagant flower it has to offer. The season saves its grandest gesture for last.

---

## 牡丹華 — Botan hana saku

Three characters. 牡丹 (*botan*) is the peony — specifically the tree peony (*Paeonia suffruticosa*), as opposed to the herbaceous peony. The characters do not have particularly suggestive meanings on their own (牡 means male animal, 丹 means red/cinnabar), but together they represent a name that arrived from China with the plant itself, sometime in the 8th century. The sound *botan* is a Japanese pronunciation of the Chinese *mǔdān*.

華 (*hana* / *ka*) means flower or blossom — specifically used here in its more elevated sense, as in 華麗 (*karei*, gorgeous/splendid). This is the *hana* of ceremony and beauty, not the everyday *hana* of a wildflower.

The full phrase: "The peony flower blooms." But the character choice for *hana* elevates this beyond simple description — this is a magnificent, celebratory blooming.

This is the third and final micro-season of the Kokuu solar term, and the 18th and final micro-season of spring. After this, summer begins with the next micro-season: 蛙始鳴 (*kaeru hajimete naku*) — "frogs begin to call" — another sound announcing another season's arrival.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The tree peony (*Paeonia suffruticosa*) is native to northwestern China and Tibet, where it grows wild in mountain forests. It was brought to Japan in the 8th century by Buddhist monks, who valued it both as a medicinal plant and as an object of contemplation. Buddhist texts sometimes described the peony as a symbol of wealth and honorable character, and it was planted widely in temple gardens.

Unlike the herbaceous peony, which dies back to the ground each winter, the tree peony maintains a woody stem structure year-round. It blooms once a year, in late April to early May, with flowers that are exceptionally large and complex but short-lived — the blooms typically last five to ten days, and rain or wind can shorten this dramatically. The fragility of the bloom relative to its grandeur is part of what makes the peony so central to the Japanese aesthetic of impermanence.

In the Genroku era (1688–1703), there was a "peony boom" in Japan: one gardening manual of the period listed nearly 500 varieties of tree peony. The enthusiasm for collecting and breeding peony varieties paralleled similar enthusiasms for morning glories and chrysanthemums in subsequent periods. Today, Japan has several celebrated peony gardens (*botan en*), including those at Ueno Toshogu Shrine in Tokyo and at temples throughout the country.

---

## The King of Flowers and Its Court

In both Chinese and Japanese tradition, the tree peony holds the title of 花王 (*kawa* / *hanao*) — King of Flowers. It is not a subtle designation. While the cherry blossom is the democratic flower of spring — present everywhere, enjoyed by everyone, printed on everything from yen coins to convenience store packaging — the peony is aristocratic. It grows in designated gardens. It requires care. It rewards attention with a display of excess that seems deliberately theatrical.

The peony appears extensively in:

**Irezumi (tattoo art)**: In the tradition of Japanese tattooing, the peony is one of the most important motifs, representing wealth, prosperity, good fortune, and noble character. Paired with a lion (*shishi*) or dragon, it represents power and beauty combined.

**Textile design**: Peony patterns appear on the most formal kimono and obi (sash) fabrics, particularly those associated with celebrations and ceremonies. A peony pattern signals occasion.

**Kabuki theater**: Aragoto ("rough style") kabuki costumes frequently feature elaborate peony motifs. The flower's association with drama and spectacular display suits the theatrical tradition perfectly.

**Buddhist iconography**: The bodhisattva Manjushri is sometimes depicted seated on a lion surrounded by peonies — the combination of fierce guardianship and magnificent beauty.

The Japanese poet Yosa Buson, himself a painter as well as a haiku master, was particularly drawn to the peony as a subject:

*牡丹散って / 打ち重なりぬ / 二三片*

*Botan chitte / uchi kasanarinu / ni san hen*

"Peony petals falling — / piling on top of each other / two, three at a time."

---

## A Poem for This Season

Buson's peony haiku are among the most celebrated in the tradition. Another:

*牡丹の / 百の白さの / 夕べかな*

*Botan no / hyaku no shirosa no / yūbe kana*

"The peony's / hundred whites / in the evening."

The "hundred whites" (*hyaku no shirosa*) is extraordinary — not one white but a hundred different whites, from the shadows inside the layered petals to the luminous outer surface catching the last evening light. A single white peony contains, Buson is saying, an entire vocabulary of whiteness. Evening is the right time to look, when the light is low and directional, when shadows define the petals' depth.

And one more, capturing the peony's magnificent fall:

*牡丹散って / 重なり二三片*

Petals falling on petals — the extravagance continuing even in dissolution. The peony does not fade quietly. It falls in layers.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Find a peony — in a garden, a park, a temple garden. Tree peonies are most common in Japan at dedicated peony gardens, but potted peonies appear in many garden centers in late April and early May. Look at one closely: count the layers of petals (there can be dozens in a fully double variety), find the colors within the colors, breathe the scent which is rich and complex in a way that photographs cannot convey.

Then return the following week and see what remains. The fallen petals accumulate on the ground in a specific way — soft, still colored, slightly translucent — that the haiku poets noticed and recorded. Even in falling, the peony is worth looking at.

Spring has given you its full portfolio: from the tentative east wind that loosened February's ice to this final extravagant gesture. Tomorrow, frogs will begin to call.
