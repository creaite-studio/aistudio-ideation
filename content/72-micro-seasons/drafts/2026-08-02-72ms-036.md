# The Summer That Doesn't Know It's Ending

*Micro-season #36 of 72 — August 2 to August 6*

---

Something different came through the window this morning.

I almost didn't notice it. The day was hot, the ceiling fan was running, the cicadas were doing their relentless work in the trees outside. But somewhere between waking and making coffee I felt it: a moment where the air moving through the room was not hot. Not cool exactly — nothing about early August in Japan invites the word "cool" without qualification — but cooler. A degree or two that made the difference between air that pressed and air that moved.

I stood still for a moment. The moment passed. The heat came back.

But it had happened, and the calendar knows it. This is the 36th of Japan's 72 micro-seasons: *suzukaze itaru* — cool winds arrive. It is, in some sense, the last season of summer. The next one, beginning August 7, falls within *Risshū* — the official astronomical beginning of autumn.

Summer doesn't know it's ending. But the calendar does.

---

## 涼風至 — Suzukaze Itaru

Three characters, a breath of relief.

涼 (*suzu*) means cool, refreshing, pleasantly cool. Not cold — cold is 寒 (*samu*) — but the specific quality of coolness that comes as relief after heat. The character has a water radical on the left (氵), suggesting coolness as a quality associated with water, with moving air at the water's edge.

風 (*kaze*) means wind, air, breath.

至 (*itaru*) means arrives, reaches, comes to — the same arrival character used throughout the 72-season system.

*Cool winds arrive.* Two words in English, and they carry a weight of anticipation that is difficult to overstate in the Japanese seasonal context. The cool wind — *suzukaze* — has been the longed-for thing all through July's relentless heat. It is a summer kigo, a seasonal word in haiku, precisely because it is hoped for rather than simply present. To write about the cool wind is to write about desire as much as sensation.

---

## What's Actually Happening

In the strictest meteorological sense, "cool winds" in early August is an optimistic description.

Average temperatures in most of Japan don't significantly decline until September. Tokyo in early August still regularly hits 35°C. The Pacific High is still dominant, the southerly winds still warm. What changes in early August is subtle: the position of the sun, lower now than at the solstice, begins to reduce the heat accumulation slightly in the late afternoon. Early mornings become marginally more bearable. The dew that forms overnight is slightly heavier.

But the 72-season calendar was always as much about perception and intention as about strict meteorology. To say that cool winds arrive in early August is to say: look for them. They are there, if you're paying attention. The window at dawn, before the heat reasserts itself. The moment after a *yūdachi* passes. The shadow of a cloud crossing quickly overhead, briefly dropping the temperature a perceptible degree.

What is also actually happening: the transition toward *Risshū* (立秋, Beginning of Autumn), which falls on August 7 or 8 by the traditional calendar. From *Risshū* onward, the Japanese traditional calendar considers it autumn — which produces one of the more charming social conventions in Japanese seasonal culture: on the first day of *Risshū*, it is considered proper to switch your summer greeting card (*shochumimai*) for an autumn greeting (*zanshoMimai*) — a note acknowledging that, yes, autumn has begun officially, even though it is still stiflingly hot, and the heat is therefore now "lingering" (*zanshō*) rather than expected.

The calendar imposes autumn on a landscape still sweating in summer. It is an act of faith in the direction of things.

## Obon and the Ancestors

This week also falls close to Obon, Japan's festival of the dead — typically observed August 13-15, though timing varies by region.

Obon is the period when ancestors are believed to return to visit their living descendants. Families travel home, graves are cleaned and visited, *bon odori* (Obon dances) take place in neighborhood parks and temple grounds throughout the country. The air smells of incense, and the sound of *taiko* drums marks the evening.

The cool wind of early August has always been associated, in the Japanese imagination, with the presence of ancestors — the dead, it is felt, bring something cooler with them when they come. This is not morbid. The Obon season carries a particular quality of warmth that has nothing to do with temperature: family, return, the sense of continuity across generations.

The fires lit on hillsides to guide ancestors home — *gozan okuribi* in Kyoto, the famous *Daimonji* fires — and the floating of lit lanterns (*tōrō nagashi*) on rivers to guide them back again, all happen in this sweltering week. Beauty and heat and memory, together.

## A Poem for This Season

The transition from summer to autumn — specifically the awareness that autumn has technically begun while summer heat still rules — has produced some of the most poignant haiku in the tradition.

Matsuo Bashō:

> 秋来ぬと　目にはさやかに　見えねども
> 風の音にぞ　おどろかれぬる
> *Aki kinu to / me ni wa sayaka ni / mienedomo*
> *kaze no oto ni zo / odorokarenuru*

This is a waka (31-syllable poem) by Fujiwara no Toshiyuki, not Bashō, from the *Kokinshū*:

*Autumn has come —*
*not yet visible*
*to the eye,*
*but in the sound of the wind*
*— I am startled.*

This poem, over a thousand years old, is one of the most famous in Japanese literature about exactly this moment: the season shifting before you can see it, detectable only through the quality of sound, through the slight change in the wind. The poet doesn't see autumn. But the wind tells him it's there.

*Suzukaze itaru* is that wind.

---

## What the Last Summer Season Asks

This is the final summer micro-season in Japan's 72-season calendar.

After this, the system enters the *Risshū* period: the Beginning of Autumn. The cool winds that arrive this week — barely, in the margins of the still-hot days — are the calendar's way of marking the threshold. We are at the edge of summer, and summer is still fully present, and that is worth noticing.

There is a particular quality of attention available at the edges of seasons that isn't quite available in the middle of them. In July, you are simply in summer — inside it, surrounded by it. But in early August, standing in the last days of the summer micro-seasons, you can look back at where you've been: the earthworms in May, the silk worms, the safflowers, the fireflies, the octopus of Hangesho, the lotus opening in the dark, the hawks learning to fall upward, the paulownia seeding, the weight of the air in the hottest weeks, the storms that broke without warning.

Summer arrived in the sound of frogs on a May night. It ends in a cool wind you almost missed.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Wake early this week — before six, if possible — and open a window. Note the quality of the air. Not whether it's comfortable; it may not be. But whether something in it has changed. Whether the heat has a slightly different character at this hour than it had in the same hour two weeks ago.

This is a small act of attention with no practical purpose. It will not make you more productive or better organized or calmer, necessarily. But it is a way of being in the season rather than simply moving through it — of registering the passage of time as something textured and specific rather than as a uniform background to other things.

Summer will tip into autumn shortly. The cicadas will thin out, then stop. The light will angle differently. The cool winds that arrive this week as a rumor will become, by October, the whole truth of the air.

For now: the wind. Barely cool. Arriving.
