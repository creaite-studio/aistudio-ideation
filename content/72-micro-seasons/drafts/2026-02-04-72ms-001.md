# The Wind That Remembers Spring

*Micro-season #1 of 72 — February 4–8*

---

There is a particular quality to the air on the fourth of February. It is still cold — unmistakably so. You can see your breath. The eaves still hold their ice. But somewhere in the wind that moves through the bare branches of the persimmon tree, something has shifted. It is not warmth, exactly. It is the memory of warmth. A promissory note, carried on the back of a wind that knows where it has come from and where it is going.

In Japan, this moment has a name. The ancient calendar does not wait for things to feel like spring before it declares it so. Instead, it watches: the angle of the sun, the behavior of the ice on still ponds, the direction from which the wind arrives. And on this day — the day the Japanese call Risshun, the "standing up of spring" — the calendar turns its first page.

This is where we begin. Not in warmth, not in blossoms, not in anything so obvious as birdsong. We begin in a cold wind that carries, if you pay close attention, the faintest suggestion that it will not always be this cold.

---

## 東風解凍 — Higashikaze kōri o toku

The four characters of this micro-season's name repay slow reading. 東 (*higashi*) means "east." 風 (*kaze*) means "wind." Together, 東風 (*higashikaze* or *kochи* in its classical reading) is the east wind — the wind that blows from across the continent, traveling over the Pacific to reach Japan from that direction as the seasons shift.

解 (*toku*) carries a remarkable range of meanings: to untie, to release, to dissolve, to explain, to set free. 凍 (*kori*) means ice or frost. So the full phrase is something like: "The east wind releases the ice" — or even, beautifully, "The east wind explains the ice away."

In the 72-season (*shichijūni kō*) system, this is the first of three micro-seasons within Risshun, the first of the 24 solar terms. The system originated in ancient China and was carefully adapted for Japan's climate in 1685 by the astronomer Shibukawa Shunkai, who adjusted the Chinese names to better reflect what was actually happening in the Japanese landscape. To live by this calendar is to treat the year not as four broad chapters but as 72 small acts of attention.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The east wind arrives in early February because of a large-scale shift in atmospheric pressure patterns across East Asia. The high-pressure systems that dominated winter — cold, dry air masses originating over the Siberian plateau — begin to weaken. As they do, warmer air from the south and east finds new pathways northward.

The ice on Japan's ponds, rice paddies, and slow rivers does not melt all at once. It begins to thin at the edges, at the shallows, where sunlight can reach the bottom. You might notice it first as a slight change in the quality of the ice's surface — less opaque, slightly honeyed in color. Where before it was solid and certain, it becomes contemplative.

For farmers, this was the traditional signal to begin thinking about the year ahead. The rice paddy calendar does not begin at planting. It begins here, in observation. What did winter look like? How deep was the freeze? What does the behavior of the ice suggest about the months to come? These are not idle questions — they are the opening moves of a chess game that will play out through the entire growing season.

---

## The Poetry of Direction

There is something worth pausing on in the specific direction of this wind. The east wind — *kochi* in classical Japanese — carries enormous symbolic weight in the literary tradition. The great poet Sugawara no Michizane (845–903), exiled from Kyoto to distant Kyushu, wrote one of the most celebrated poems in Japanese history about this wind:

*東風吹かば / 匂ひおこせよ / 梅の花 / 主なしとて / 春な忘れそ*

*Kochi fukaba / nioi okoseyo / ume no hana / aruji nashi tote / haru na wasure so*

"When the east wind blows, / send me your fragrance, / plum blossoms. / Though your master is gone, / do not forget the spring."

Michizane addressed this poem to the plum tree in his garden, which he was leaving behind. The east wind became the messenger between what was left behind and what lay ahead — a kind of seasonal postal system, carrying scent and memory across distance. That same east wind is now the one that, according to the ancient calendar, loosens the ice and opens the year.

---

## A Poem for This Season

Spring is famous in haiku for what is still absent. Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694) captured this quality again and again:

*春もやや / けしきととのふ / 月と梅*

*Haru mo yaya / keshiki totonou / tsuki to ume*

"Spring too, gradually / the scenery arranges itself — / moon and plum blossoms."

The word *yaya* — "gradually," "little by little" — is the key. This is not a poem about spring's arrival. It is a poem about spring's approach, still being assembled, still not quite here. The moon and the plum blossom serve as early indicators, the first pieces placed on a stage that will take weeks to fully set.

That sense of gradual becoming is the essence of this first micro-season. The ice is only beginning to loosen. The wind has only begun to shift. But the calendar — and the haiku master — both know that this barely-perceptible change is the beginning of everything.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Step outside on a cold February morning and face east. Notice the wind — not whether it is cold or warm, but where it is coming from, and whether it has changed at all from last week. Check a pond or puddle for any sign of thinning ice at the edges. Look for the quality of light: it will be subtly longer in duration now, subtly higher in the sky at noon.

If you are somewhere without obvious ice, look for other signs: are any buds beginning to swell on the bare branches? Does the soil smell different when you walk in a park? Wherever you are, the earth is responding to the same shift in the sun's position. The micro-seasons travel.

Make a small note. Tomorrow will be almost the same, and the day after that. But in five days, something will have moved.
