# The Fish That Comes Home to Die

*Micro-season #62 of 72 — December 12 to December 16*

---

The river in December smells different from any other month.

If you stand on the bank of one of Hokkaido's larger rivers in mid-December, or the mountain streams of Tōhoku or the San'in coast, the smell reaches you before the sight: something rich and faintly rank, organic, the smell of life and death happening simultaneously in the cold water. Then you look down, and the river floor is paved with fish.

Not living fish exactly — or not only living fish. Some are still pushing upstream, their bodies already disintegrating at the edges, skin going pale and ragged, dorsal fins worn to stumps. Others have already stopped moving, their spent bodies drifting slowly in the current, being picked apart by birds on the banks and animals in the shallows. And in the gravel beds of the upper reaches, there are others still: females fanning eggs into nests with their tails, males circling alongside.

The salmon have gathered. They are doing what they came to do.

---

## 鱖魚群 — Sake no Uo Muragaru

The first character of this name is extraordinary, and worth looking at carefully.

*Kō* (鱖) — the traditional kanji written here for salmon — is one of the rarest characters in Japanese everyday use. It combines the fish radical (魚) with the character for *ketsu* (厥) on the right, which historically conveyed a meaning related to the knees bending, or something collapsing. The full character has been interpreted as "the fish that exhausts itself" or "the fish that bends at the end." Whether or not this etymology is entirely accurate, there is something apt about it: the salmon at the end of its run is bent, ragged, spending its last energy in the final act.

In modern Japanese, salmon is written differently — either simply in *katakana* as サーモン (a loan word) or using the more common characters *sake* (鮭) or *masu* (鱒). The character 鱖 in this season's name is an archaic scholarly choice, and its use signals that this calendar text is drawing on ancient Chinese sources and their classical vocabulary.

*Uo* (魚) is fish — or here, used as a classifier: "salmon-fish," specifying the creature.

*Muragaru* (群) means to gather in groups, to mass together, to form a crowd or school. The character shows three people standing together under a sheep — probably meaning people gathering around a shepherd — giving it a sense of purposeful assembly rather than random aggregation. These fish are not simply appearing in the same place. They have come together with intention.

Salmon gather. The etymology suggests they do so in order to complete themselves.

## What's Actually Happening

Japan has several species of Pacific salmon (*Oncorhynchus*), with chum salmon (*sake*, or *keta*, O. keta) being the most numerous and widely distributed. Pink salmon (*karafuto masu*), sockeye, and chinook are also found in Japanese waters in smaller numbers.

The cycle of the salmon is one of the most elaborately studied stories in ecology, and it remains, even after a century of research, genuinely strange.

After hatching in freshwater gravel beds, young salmon spend weeks to months in their natal rivers, then migrate to the ocean where they will spend two to five years (depending on species) feeding in the North Pacific. Chum salmon may travel thousands of kilometers through the open ocean before, responding to some combination of day length, water temperature, and magnetic field sensing, they turn back toward the Japanese coast.

How they find their home river is still not fully understood. The most compelling evidence suggests olfactory memory: the salmon imprints on the specific chemical signature of its birth stream as a juvenile, and returns to that signature as an adult. Each river smells different — a complex profile of minerals, organic compounds, specific microbe populations. The salmon's nose, sensitive enough to detect these differences at oceanic distances, guides it home.

By December, in Japanese rivers, the runs are in their final stages. Earlier species peak in October and November; December's gatherings are primarily chum salmon pushing into the upper reaches of streams. The water temperature drops further, the fish that have already spawned begin to die, and their bodies — high in nitrogen and other nutrients — become a massive annual input of ocean-derived nutrients into the freshwater ecosystem. The salmon feeds the forest as well as the river.

## The Salmon and the Mountain Village

In the communities of Tōhoku and Hokkaido that depended on autumn salmon runs, this fish was not simply food — it was the year.

The annual run determined how well a village would eat through winter. A good run meant smoked salmon hanging from every rafter, barrels of salted roe, rendered oil for lamps and waterproofing, dried fish ground into meal for soups. A poor run meant lean months. The community gathered at the river not just to fish but to witness: the salmon's return was evidence that the cycle continued, that the sea was still generous, that the contract between humans and the natural world remained in force.

The Ainu people of Hokkaido — the indigenous inhabitants — understood salmon as a gift from the divine world. The salmon was called *kamuy chep* — "god fish" or "divine fish" — and its return was accompanied by ceremonies of welcome and gratitude. The bones and internal organs were returned to the river after eating, to allow the salmon's spirit to report back to the spirit world and ensure the return of future runs. The salmon did not die; it traveled between worlds.

This is not naive animism. It is a sophisticated ecological understanding encoded in ritual form: take only what you need, return what you don't use, honor the relationship that sustains you.

## A Poem for This Season

The haiku poet Kawahigashi Hekigoto, a student of Shiki, wrote:

> 秋鮭の　川透きとほり　昇りけり
> *aki sake no / kawa sukitōri / nobori keri*

*Autumn salmon —*
*through the clear river,*
*climbing.*

The poem is spare as a photograph. The verb *noboru* — to climb, to ascend — applies to the salmon but also, in classical usage, to the act of going upstream toward the capital, or toward the source of things. The salmon isn't just swimming upstream; it is ascending. The river's clarity allows us to see through the water to the fish below, an image at once immediate and transparent in the literal sense.

What the poem holds is the paradox of the salmon's journey: it moves toward the source while moving toward its end. Ascending and finishing at the same time.

## How to Meet This Season

If you are anywhere near a river with a salmon run this week — and they exist not only in Japan but across the Pacific Coast of North America, in parts of northern Europe — go and stand on the bank.

You don't need to fish. You don't need to do anything but watch. The salmon running in December are usually past their ocean prime: they've changed color (from silver to red and brown), their jaws have hooked, their skin has begun to deteriorate. They are not the animals they were six months ago, and yet they are doing something no fish in a tank could do — navigating by memory and smell across thousands of kilometers to arrive at the exact gravel bed where their life began.

If you can't reach a salmon river: buy whole salmon this week at a market, or salt-cured salmon if you can find it — the old preparation that made the fish a winter staple. Eat it simply. Think about where it came from, the water it traveled through, the distance between its ocean life and your table.

The fish that comes home to die fertilizes the forest with ocean nutrients. The ending feeds what comes next. This is the salmon's final teaching.
