# The First Taste of Something Coming

*Micro-season #69 of 72 — January 21 to January 25*

---

It appears at the base of a south-facing bank, right where the snow is thinnest.

A pale green dome, about the size of a marble, pushing through the dark soil. Then another. Then a cluster of three, arranged like something thinking about becoming a bouquet. The *fukinotō* — the butterbur shoot — has arrived.

Nothing about the surroundings suggests spring. The trees are bare. The air is cold enough to see your breath. Somewhere above the snowline it's still very much winter, and this hillside will freeze again tonight. But the butterbur doesn't wait for permission. It has been accumulating energy in its underground rhizome since autumn, and now it decides: now.

This small, pale-green, slightly scruffy shoot is the first edible sign of the turning year. And in Japan, it has been eaten — eagerly, ceremonially — for as long as people have lived here.

---

## 款冬華 — Fuki no Hana Saku

The characters in this name use an ancient Chinese name for the butterbur plant.

*Kan* (款) means generous, gracious, earnest — it appears in classical texts to describe a sincere or hospitable quality. *Tō* (冬) is winter. So *kantō* is the "winter-gracious" plant, or perhaps "the generous one of winter" — a plant that gives something when little else will.

*Ka* (華) is one of the more beautiful characters in the classical vocabulary. It means flower, but specifically a brilliant, showy flower — it's the character used in the word for China (*Chūka*, 中華, "the flowering center"), in *hanayaka* (華やか, gorgeous, showy), and in *eiga* (栄華, splendor, glory). This is not the modest *hana* (花) of ordinary flowers. This is *ka* — something luminous.

*Saku* means to bloom, to open.

The generous-winter-plant blooms in glory. The naming is deliberately elevated for what is, in practice, a fairly ordinary-looking shoot. But the calendar has chosen this language because it knows what the *fukinotō* means to someone who has been waiting through a Japanese winter: it is luminous precisely because of when it appears.

---

## What's Actually Happening

*Petasites japonicus* — fuki, the Japanese butterbur — is a perennial plant of the aster family. It is remarkable for two reasons. First, it has among the largest leaves of any plant native to Japan: in the right conditions, a single fuki leaf can exceed a meter across, large enough to use as an umbrella in a sudden shower. Second, its flowering shoots (*fukinotō*, 蕗の薹) emerge months before the leaves, pushing through snow in the coldest weeks of late January and February.

The plant's strategy is one of temporal competition. By blooming before any other plant — before the trees leaf out, before the early spring flowers — the *fukinotō* can take full advantage of the available pollinators without competition. The flowers are small, cream-colored, clustered in a ball about 2-3 centimeters in diameter; they open as they push through the soil and snow. Flies and early bees — cold-tolerant insects that emerge on warmer winter days — are the primary pollinators.

After pollination, the plant produces the enormous summer leaves that give it its other identity: the shade plant of river banks and moist hillsides, growing so vigorously that it shades out competitors. The summer fuki and the winter *fukinotō* seem almost like different plants, so different are they in scale and character.

The shoots are edible only before they fully open — once the bud begins to spread, the leaves turn bitter. This creates a brief window, typically two to three weeks, when the *fukinotō* is at peak flavor: closed, dense, with a distinctive taste that is best described as bitter-herbaceous, with a slight vegetal sweetness underneath.

Traditional Japanese cooks have used this bitterness — called *shibumi* (渋味) — as a feature rather than a flaw. The bitter quality of early spring foods was understood as medicinal: stimulating the liver, clearing accumulated winter stagnation from the body, waking up the digestive system after months of heavy starchy foods. *Fukinotō tempura*, deep-fried in light batter, is perhaps the most beloved preparation — the bitter shoots contrast beautifully with the savory, light crunch of the batter.

## Bears and Butterbur

The connection between bears and *fukinotō* is one of the oldest and most persistent pieces of Japanese folk ecology.

It was observed — and it appears to be accurate — that bears emerging from hibernation in late winter and early spring immediately seek out *fukinotō*. The shoots are one of the first things available after months of fasting, and bears consume them in large quantities, sometimes traveling considerable distances to find early patches.

This observation led to the traditional belief that eating *fukinotō* after a period of dormancy or illness was particularly restorative — that humans were following the bear's example by eating it in winter and early spring. The bear's instinct was taken as botanical wisdom: if the bear wakes up and eats this, the bear knows something about what the body needs.

Whether the folk medicine holds up to modern scrutiny matters less than the ecological observation it's based on: the bear, and the butterbur shoot, are both signs of the same change. Both are responding to the same seasonal signal. The bear waking and the *fukinotō* pushing through snow are parts of the same announcement.

## A Poem for This Season

The haiku poet Mizuhara Shūōshi, writing in the 20th century, captured the *fukinotō* with characteristic precision:

> ふきのとう　雪の下より　顔を出す
> *fukinotō / yuki no shita yori / kao o dasu*

*Butterbur shoot —*
*from beneath the snow,*
*putting out a face.*

*Kao o dasu* — "putting out a face" — is an idiom meaning to show oneself, to make an appearance, but it also literally means to put out a face. The butterbur shoot, emerging from snow, has a face: the round, slightly solemn dome of the closed bud, pale green, earnest. The anthropomorphism is exact and true. The *fukinotō* does look like something cautiously showing its face.

The poem is one of the most widely reproduced haiku about early spring in Japan, and its appeal is immediate: you see the image instantly, and it makes you want to find a *fukinotō* and check.

## How to Meet This Season

In Japan, *fukinotō* appear in supermarkets from around late January through February, usually displayed loose in small bundles or boxes. When you see them, buy some. They are seasonal in the truest sense — available for only a few weeks — and they taste like nothing else.

The simplest preparation: tempura. Make a light batter (flour, cold water, a little soda water) and fry the whole closed buds at moderate heat until the batter is golden. The *fukinotō* will taste bitter — this is correct and good. Eat them with grated daikon and light soy sauce.

Or: chop them finely and mix with *miso* paste to make *fukinotō miso*, which can be spread on rice or used as a condiment for weeks in the refrigerator. The bitterness mellows and integrates. It becomes something that tastes like winter ending.

If you're outside Japan: look for butterbur (*Petasites* species) in your area — they grow along streams and in moist areas in parts of North America and Europe. Or find bitter spring greens at a farmers' market — dandelion greens, chicory, early sorrel — and make a meal of bitterness. Eat what winter is releasing.

The *fukinotō*'s message is not complicated: something is coming. It arrived to tell you before the evidence was obvious. Trust the small, bitter, earnest shoot.
