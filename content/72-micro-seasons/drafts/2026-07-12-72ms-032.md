# The First Lesson Is the Longest Fall

*Micro-season #32 of 72 — July 12 to July 16*

---

I've watched a hawk fledge exactly once.

It was at a shrine in Chiba, on a July afternoon too hot to do much of anything useful, and I had stopped under the shade of a large cedar. I heard the sound first — a sharp, repeated cry that seemed too loud for the surrounding calm — and looked up to find a young black-eared kite (*tobi*) perched on a branch maybe ten meters above me, screaming at the world with the particular indignation of someone who has just realized they have to do something terrifying.

It jumped. It flapped. It fell about three meters in a completely un-hawk-like manner, caught some air, lurched sideways, crashed into the next branch lower down, and screamed some more.

Its parent circled overhead at a polite distance, apparently unconcerned.

The young hawk tried again from the lower branch. This attempt was marginally better.

## 鷹乃学習 — Taka Sunawachi Waza wo Narau

The name of this season has an old formal cadence to it.

鷹 (*taka*) means hawk — the word encompasses both the strict *taka* (hawks and falcons) and the broader category of raptors generally. In classical Japanese poetry and aesthetics, the hawk carries connotations of power, precision, nobility. Falconry (*takagari*) was a courtly and military pursuit for over a thousand years in Japan; the hawk was a symbol of the aristocracy and, later, the warrior class.

乃 (*no*) is a classical particle of possession and attribution: "of" or "belonging to."

学習 (*gakushū*) — the word that has the same kanji as modern Japanese's word for "study" or "learning" — here carries the older meaning from the compound: to practice, to train, to learn a technique. The character 習 (*narau*, to learn/practice) contains the image of a young bird (隹) beneath wings (羽): a young bird learning to use its wings. The etymology of the character *is* the thing it describes.

*Hawks practice their art.* The young hawk, this week, is doing what the character of "learning" has always depicted: the fledgling working out, one clumsy attempt at a time, what wings are for.

---

## What's Actually Happening

July is the month of fledging for many Japanese raptor species.

Black-eared kites (*Milvus lineatus*) — the most commonly seen large raptor across Japan, the ones you see circling over rivers and coastal areas, occasionally swooping for food at matsuri stalls — fledge in late spring through summer, with young birds spending July learning to handle themselves in the air.

Japanese sparrowhawks (*Accipiter gularis*) and grey-faced buzzards (*Butastur indicus*) also have summer-learning periods. But the micro-season most likely originally refers to the goshawk (*Accipiter gentilis*), the primary bird of Japanese falconry — a larger, more powerful species whose young spend the summer months in protracted learning, watched carefully by falconers who would sometimes catch them before they became fully wild.

The learning process for a raptor fledgling is exactly as clumsy as it looks. Young hawks must not only learn the mechanical basics of flight — which muscles to use, how to catch thermals, how to brake and land without crashing — but must also learn to hunt, which involves an entirely separate set of skills: how to read the behavior of prey, when to stoop, how to use cover, how to kill efficiently. Raptors raised in captivity that are released without hunting training starve; the skill is not instinctive but learned.

The summer window of learning is therefore biological necessity. What looks like awkwardness is practice, and the practice is urgent.

## The Hawk in Japanese Culture

The hawk (*taka*) holds a peculiar double role in Japanese aesthetics.

In falconry culture — which reached Japan from China via Korea and flourished from the Nara period through the Edo period — the hawk was an instrument of power and prestige. Emperors kept hawks; shoguns kept hawks. Falconry manuals were written, hawk-training techniques developed into sophisticated arts, and the bird's training was itself considered a model for human self-cultivation. A well-trained hawk that could be recalled instantly, that stooped at command, that sat calmly on the gloved fist — this was considered an achievement of both the animal and the trainer.

In contrast, in haiku and related literature, the hawk appears as something wild and solitary, a creature of sky and height that humans can observe but never fully know. Bashō's famous New Year's haiku:

> 目出度さも　ちう位也　おらが春
> *(Medetasa mo / chū kurai nari / oraga haru)*

...uses a different image, but the hawk appears elsewhere in his corpus as a symbol of solitary vision: the bird that sees the landscape from above, that comprehends a wider world than those below.

The young hawk of this micro-season is neither: not yet the controlled instrument of falconry, not yet the sovereign of the sky. It is something in between — ungainly, loud, working out what kind of creature it is.

## A Poem for This Season

The Sailor stationery company released a pen color in their *Shikiori* series called *Waka-taka* — Young Hawk — specifically for this micro-season. It is a yellow-green ink, evocative of summer fields seen from altitude. The name itself is a small poem: the young hawk (*waka-taka*), not yet the adult, the form still taking shape.

From the classical tradition:

> 鷹一つ　見付けてうれし　伊良古崎
> *Taka hitotsu / mitsukete ureshi / Irakozaki*

Matsuo Bashō, spotting a single hawk at Irako Cape in winter:

*One hawk —*
*spotted at Irako Cape:*
*the happiness of it.*

The joy is entirely in the sighting, in the single hawk against the sky. Bashō doesn't elaborate. The hawk is there; the poet sees it; there is happiness. The economy of the poem mirrors the precision of the bird.

## How to Meet This Season

Find an open sky this week and spend some time looking at it.

If you're in Japan in July, hawks are visible throughout the country — the *tobi* is omnipresent over rivers, coastlines, and anywhere food waste might be found. They are the most acrobatic thing in the ordinary sky, and watching a young one learn its craft against the background of summer heat is worth more than the brief time it takes.

Watch for the awkward ones: the birds that land badly, that overshoot their branch and have to scramble back, that take three attempts to read the wind correctly. These are this week's students, doing the urgent work of learning what they were built to do.

There is something quietly encouraging about an animal that is allowed to fail repeatedly, in public, while its parent circles overhead and does not intervene. The young hawk is not helped. It is given time, space, and the expectation that it will eventually figure it out.

This is its own form of faith.
