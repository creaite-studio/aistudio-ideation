# The Tree That Carries the Phoenix

*Micro-season #33 of 72 — July 17 to July 22*

---

The paulownia bloomed in April — great lavender spires of flowers before any leaves appeared, which made the tree look briefly like it was on fire from a distance.

By July, the flowers have long gone. The leaves, enormous and velvety, have taken over: a mature paulownia in full summer leaf is a tree of considerable authority, casting shade as dense as any of the fruit trees beside it. And now, in the heat of mid-July, something quieter is happening. Small oval seed pods are forming where the flower clusters were. They'll sit on the tree through autumn, persisting even after the leaves fall, holding their seeds through winter until the pods finally dry and split in spring.

The tree is, in this season, between its two most visible acts: flower and seed. It is gathering itself.

The 72-season calendar marks this moment — the forming of the seed pods — as the 33rd micro-season.

---

## 桐始結花 — Kiri Hajimete Hana wo Musubu

Four characters, a description of botanical process.

桐 (*kiri*) is the paulownia tree — *Paulownia tomentosa* — native to China but widely cultivated throughout East Asia and deeply embedded in Japanese cultural symbolism. The tree grows extraordinarily fast, often reaching full height within a decade, and produces wood that is light, strong, resonant, and water-resistant.

始 (*hajimete*) means "for the first time" — again, the arrival marker.

結花 (*hana wo musubu*) is the interesting part. 結 (*musubu*) means to tie, to bind, to form — and 花 (*hana*) means flower. So the compound means "the flowers bind themselves into" — referring to the formation of seed pods, the flower's fruition, the tying-up of the reproductive cycle into a contained form.

*Paulownia trees form seed pods for the first time.* The name is attentive to process: the tree is not done flowering but transforming what the flower was into what comes next.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The paulownia is one of the fastest-growing trees in the temperate world.

Under good conditions, a paulownia can grow three to five meters in its first year. A tree planted when a child is born will shade that child's wedding in many Japanese traditions — though the timeline is compressed now. Farmers in rural Japan have historically planted paulownia when daughters were born, with the intention of harvesting the timber when the daughter married: the wood was used to make the lacquered *tansu* (chest of drawers) that was part of a bride's household goods.

The wood's qualities are extraordinary: it is the lightest structural timber in commercial use, yet strong enough for furniture. It does not warp in high humidity. It resists both fire (its high water content makes ignition difficult) and insects. Most importantly for traditional use, it has excellent acoustic resonance — paulownia is the standard wood for the *koto* (the Japanese zither), for *shamisen* (the three-stringed lute), and for other traditional instruments where tonal quality depends on the wood's vibration characteristics.

The seed pods forming now in July will contain hundreds of tiny seeds — a single pod can hold 2,000 or more — that disperse by wind in spring. The tree's reproductive strategy is profligacy: make many seeds, let them scatter, trust the math.

---

## The Kiri and the Emperor

No other tree in Japan carries quite the same official weight as the paulownia.

The *go-shichi-no-kiri* — "five-seven paulownia," referring to a stylized image of a paulownia branch with five flowers on the center spike and three on each flanking spike (though "seven" is sometimes counted differently) — is the official seal of the Prime Minister of Japan. It appears on official government documents, the cover of the Japanese passport, and ceremonial contexts of all kinds.

This emblem has roots in the imperial household. The chrysanthemum (*kiku*) is the exclusive symbol of the Emperor; the paulownia was historically the symbol granted to loyal subjects by imperial favor. When a shogun was given the paulownia mon (family crest), it was a mark of the highest imperial regard. Over the centuries, the emblem spread widely — to the Ashikaga and Toyotomi clans, to various daimyo families — and eventually settled into its current role as the symbol of the Japanese government itself.

The association with power runs deeper than heraldry. A Chinese and Japanese myth holds that the *hōō* — the phoenix of East Asian mythology — will rest only in the branches of a paulownia tree. The phoenix, in this tradition, does not die and rebirth in fire (that is the European phoenix); it appears only in times of righteous rule, as a sign that the sovereign is governing well. To have a phoenix in your paulownia was to have divine endorsement.

Plant a paulownia, and you are, in some attenuated sense, inviting the possibility of that endorsement.

## A Poem for This Season

The paulownia's autumn leaves appear more often in classical poetry than the tree's summer seeding — the large, yellow, papery leaves that fall heavily in October were a classic image of seasonal melancholy. But the spring flowers also attracted attention.

Yosa Buson:

> 桐一葉　日当たりながら　落ちにけり
> *Kiri hitoha / hi atarinagara / ochini keri*

*A single paulownia leaf —*
*still in the sunlight,*
*falls.*

This haiku is from an autumn context, but its precision is applicable here too: the single leaf, the sunlight still on it, the fall happening despite the warmth. Buson was interested in the moment of transition — the thing that is still present and already going.

The seed pods of July carry the same quality: the flower is over, the tree is transitioning, and what looks like dormancy is actually a different kind of work.

---

## How to Meet This Season

The paulownia is common enough in Japan that you may walk past one regularly without knowing it. The leaves are distinctive: enormous (often 40 or 50 centimeters across), heart-shaped, with a slightly velvety texture that catches dust and holds it, making them useful as a folk measure of air quality.

Look for the seed pods on mature trees this week — they're easy to spot once you know what you're looking for. Small, oval, pale green, clustered in groups where the flowers were.

If you're interested in Japanese woodworking or musical instruments: find a music shop that carries traditional instruments and hold a *koto* sounding board, or look at the base of a *shamisen*. The wood is extraordinarily light — lighter than it looks, lighter than intuition suggests is possible for something so resonant. The tree that carries the phoenix has given its wood to the instruments that carry music.

That's not a bad trade.
