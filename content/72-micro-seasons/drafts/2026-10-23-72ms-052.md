# The Rain That Barely Falls

*Micro-season #52 of 72 — October 23 to October 27*

---

There's a kind of rain that can't make up its mind.

It doesn't pour. It doesn't drizzle consistently. It starts, stops, starts again — you step outside thinking it's over and feel the first drops return. You put down the umbrella and it picks up again. You don't quite need an umbrella; you don't quite not need one. You end up slightly damp regardless of what you decide.

In Japanese, this kind of rain has a name: *kosame* (小雨) — small rain, little rain, the diminutive form of rain. And this micro-season, 霎時施, is dedicated to it.

I find something quietly remarkable about this. The 72 micro-seasons include seasons named for grand phenomena: the thunder that shakes the summer sky, the typhoon-born fog, the wild geese crossing from Siberia. And then they include this — a five-day window in late October dedicated to rain that isn't quite sure it's happening.

The calendar said: this too deserves a name.

---

## 霎時施 — Kosame Tokidoki Furu

The name requires unpacking, because one of its characters is rare.

*霎* (shu, or kosame) is a character meaning light shower, a brief or light rain. It's composed of the rain radical (雨) combined with elements suggesting smallness and brevity. The word *kosame* appears more commonly in poetry than in everyday speech — it's the poetic register for gentle, intermittent autumn rain.

*時* (toki) means time, moment, or in this compound use: sometimes, occasionally, from time to time. *Tokidoki* (時時) is the common modern form: "sometimes," "occasionally." Here the character works with *霎* to mean the rain comes and goes with the passing of time.

*施* (furu, or se) means to bestow, to give, to apply — but in the context of rain, it reads as *furu* (降る), to fall. The rain *gives itself* to the earth. There's a generosity implied in this character, which usually means to donate or grant. The light rain is bestowing itself, a little at a time, an occasional gift.

*Light rain falls from time to time.* But the character *施* changes the feeling slightly: this rain is not unwelcome. It gives. It comes in small amounts, and that is enough.

## What's Actually Happening

Late October in Japan is characterized by the gradual increase of continental influence on the weather. The summer Pacific High has long retreated; the typhoon season is ending. What replaces them is a series of low-pressure systems moving eastward across Japan, bringing overcast skies and intermittent rain.

These late-autumn rains are not the violent convective storms of summer. They are frontal systems, slow-moving, covering large areas with gray cloud and producing steady light-to-moderate rainfall that can last for days. Between systems, there are clear periods. Within systems, the rain often comes and goes, lightening and intensifying as bands of moisture move through.

This is also the period when the atmosphere is transitioning toward its winter state. The air is drier than summer, but the cloud cover traps what moisture remains. The result is the particular quality of late-autumn weather: gray, soft, slightly damp, not quite cold, not quite warm.

In the rice-farming calendar, these late October rains fall after the harvest is complete — the grain is already in. But the rains are important for the autumn vegetable crops (daikon, leafy greens, winter leeks) that are now filling out in fields across Japan.

## Japan's Vocabulary for Rain

Japanese has an exceptionally rich vocabulary for precipitation, and understanding it reveals something about what the culture pays attention to.

*Kosame* (小雨) — light rain, the type of this micro-season.
*Shigure* (時雨) — brief autumn or winter shower, often accompanied by wind; deeply associated with sadness and autumn mood in classical poetry.
*Kirisame* (霧雨) — mist-rain, rain so fine it's almost fog.
*Harusame* (春雨) — spring rain, known for its gentle quality.
*Ōame* (大雨) — heavy rain.
*Niwakame* (俄雨) — sudden shower.
*Samidare* (五月雨) — May rain, the early summer rainy season (*tsuyu*).

The list goes on for dozens of entries in classical Japanese. A language that makes these distinctions is a language shaped by close attention to how water falls. The 72 micro-seasons fit within this tradition: the rain of this period is specific, not generic. It falls lightly. It comes and goes. It has a name.

## A Poem for This Season

*Shigure* — the autumn shower — is one of the most beloved weather phenomena in classical haiku, associated with the season's particular melancholy. While *kosame* (light rain) and *shigure* (brief shower) are different, they overlap in late October, and the poetry of one bleeds into the other.

Bashō famously loved *shigure* and used it repeatedly. One of his most celebrated:

> 旅人と　わが名よばれん　初しぐれ
> *Tabibito to / waga na yobare n / hatsu shigure*

*Let me be called / a traveler — / the first autumn shower.*

This was written as Bashō was setting out on one of his long journeys. The first *shigure* of the season and the first day of travel coincide, and Bashō asks to be defined by that coincidence: *let me be called a traveler.* The rain makes him one; the rain inaugurates the journey.

What I love about this poem is the self-definition through weather. The traveler doesn't announce themselves — the rain announces them. The first autumn shower gives the traveler their identity.

## How to Meet This Season

This one doesn't require early rising or special location.

Pay attention this week to rain that almost isn't rain. The drizzle that makes you uncertain whether to open an umbrella. The mist that accumulates on your jacket without quite being rain. The gray sky that could go either way and keeps not deciding.

Notice the light on wet surfaces — pavement, leaves, the roofs of houses. Wet late-autumn surfaces have a particular light: flat, matte, the colors of the world slightly more saturated but the reflections diffuse. It's one of the more honest aesthetics of the season.

And notice if the rain feels like it's giving something. *施* — bestowing. Intermittent, small, reluctant rain that the earth still drinks. There's no drama here. Just weather, doing what weather does in late October, in the gentlest possible way.
