# The Underground Opens Its Doors

*Micro-season #7 of 72 — March 6–10*

---

There are small holes in the earth now that were not there before. In the garden, along the bank of the path, at the base of a stone wall — tiny apertures, no bigger than a pencil, opening downward into darkness. They appeared in the last day or two. They were not dug by anything large. They were opened from within.

This is how the seventh micro-season of the year makes itself known: through small holes in the ground, through a sudden appearance of beetles that have been absent since October, through the first tentative flight of a butterfly caught by the sun — fragile, improbable, still slightly unsteady — on a warm March afternoon. The underground world has decided that the earth above is ready.

The timing is not casual. These creatures have been waiting, not simply for warmth but for a specific and sustained warmth. They carry, in whatever passes for their biological memory, a threshold: a number of accumulated warm days after which emergence is possible. The soil in early March has reached it. The doors open.

---

## 蟄虫啓戸 — Sugomori mushi to o hiraku

This is one of the most evocative names in the entire 72-season calendar, and one of the few that uses imagery of architecture. 蟄 (*chit-su*) is a rare character meaning to hibernate, to burrow in, to withdraw into concealment — specifically used for insects and other creatures that go underground in winter. 虫 (*mushi*) is the generic word for insect or creature, though historically it meant any small crawling or flying thing.

啓 (*hiraku* / *kei*) is a character meaning to open, to reveal, to enlighten — a formal word for opening that also appears in the context of knowledge and learning. 戸 (*to*) means a door, specifically the type of sliding door (*fusuma* or *shōji*) that frames rooms in traditional Japanese architecture.

So: the hibernating creatures open their doors. The image is one of interior space opening to the outside world — the insect's winter dwelling, deep in the soil, has a door, and the creature, as if it has simply been waiting on the other side, opens it and steps out into spring.

This is the first micro-season of the Keichitsu solar term — a name that itself means "awakening of insects" (*kei* = to startle awake, *chitsu* = hibernating things). In the broader East Asian calendar tradition, this solar term was understood as a moment when the spring thunder first rumbles, startling the underground creatures into wakefulness. In Japan, Keichitsu is March 6.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Insects overwinter in multiple ways: as eggs laid in sheltered locations, as pupae in soil or leaf litter, as adults in diapause (a dormancy state) in crevices, under bark, or deep in the soil. The specific creature referred to by "mushi" in this micro-season's name varies by historical interpretation, but the phenomenon is universal: with sustained soil temperatures above about 10°C (50°F), many overwintering insect species and larvae begin to move.

The first visible insects in early March Japan are often beetles and weevils that overwintered as adults, ladybugs emerging from tree crevices where they spent the winter in clusters, and the first queen bumblebees, which emerge alone to begin establishing new colonies. Ground beetles begin to appear at night.

The small circular holes in garden soil are often the work of ground beetles, or of the soil-dwelling larvae of various species beginning their pupation process. In some cases they are the burrow entrances of ground-nesting bees, which are among the spring's most important pollinators.

Earthworms, which are not insects but are equally part of this awakening, begin to appear more actively in the upper soil layers. Gardeners know the earthworm as a sign that the soil is truly alive again.

---

## Thunder and the Startled Earth

The Keichitsu solar term has an alternate name in Chinese: 驚蟄 (*jing zhe*) — "startling the hibernating things." The theory behind this name is that the first spring thunder, which often arrives around this time, physically startles creatures out of their winter torpor.

This idea — that sound could wake the sleeping earth — is both poetically rich and agriculturally practical. Thunder in early spring generally accompanies the kinds of warm, moist air masses that bring the temperature above the threshold needed for insect emergence. The two phenomena are correlated, though the thunder does not cause the emergence. But the image of creatures startled awake by the sky's first rumble of spring has the kind of vivid truthfulness that good metaphors achieve: it captures the quality of the experience even if the mechanism is more complex.

In China, farmers traditionally beat drums in their fields on the day of Jingzhe to invite the spring, echoing and perhaps amplifying the thunder's call to the sleeping world. In Japan, the imagery is softer: the doors opening, the creatures stepping out, the spring meeting them on the other side.

## A Poem for This Season

Matsuo Bashō composed a famous haiku about the spring emergence of small creatures, one that captures the sense of the newly awakened world encountering the light:

*春の海 / ひねもすのたり / のたりかな*

*Haru no umi / hinemosu notari / notari kana*

"The spring sea — / all day long, / gently swelling, gently swelling."

This is not directly about insects, but Bashō's images of spring always carry the sense of the whole world moving at the same rhythm — water, air, small creatures, all rocking in the same gentle swell. The *notari notari* — the repeated sound of gentle undulation — is what the emergence of dormant things looks like from the right distance: a world that was still becoming, suddenly, gently alive.

A more direct seasonal haiku for Keichitsu comes from Masaoka Shiki (1867–1902):

*虫出づる / 日の温かき / 庭のかな*

*Mushi izuru / hi no atatakaki / niwa no kana*

"Insects emerging — / what a warm / day in the garden."

The simplicity is deceptive. The warmth of the day, the garden, the insects coming out: three things aligned, the season making itself visible through the coincidence.

## How to Meet This Season

Spend time close to the ground. Garden-kneeling level, or sitting on a bench with your eyes directed downward into the grass and soil at your feet. Look for movement: a beetle crossing the path, a small fly briefly airborne in a patch of sunlight, the thin tracery of an insect path through disturbed soil.

Turn over a stone or a piece of old wood lying on damp ground. What you find there will tell you what the season has been doing while you weren't looking.
