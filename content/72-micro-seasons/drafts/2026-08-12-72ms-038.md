# The World Disappears Before Breakfast

*Micro-season #38 of 72 — August 12 to August 16*

---

I stepped outside one morning this week to find that my neighborhood had been replaced.

The garden was still there — roughly. I could see the nearest tree, a smeared outline of green against white. Beyond it, the shape of the fence. Beyond that, nothing. The road that runs past the house had become a suggestion. A truck moved through it somewhere, a soft rumble without a source, appearing briefly and then swallowing itself back into the white.

I stood at the edge of the porch for a long time before going back inside to make tea.

This is the fog of early autumn. In Japanese it has its own word, entirely separate from spring mist: *kiri* (霧). And this micro-season — 蒙霧升降 — belongs to it completely.

---

## 蒙霧升降 — Fukaki Kiri Matou

The name is unusual. Most of the 72 micro-season names are short and punchy: three or four characters, a crisp image. This one has four characters that take longer to arrive.

*蒙* (mō) means to cover, to obscure, to be ignorant — to have something draped over you. It's the character in *mōmō* (蒙蒙), which describes a thick, heavy obscurity. Think of the feeling of having your vision wrapped in gauze.

*霧* (kiri) is fog itself. Dense, low, the kind that stays close to the ground. In classical Japanese, the word carries specific seasonal connotations: *kiri* is autumn fog, while *kasumi* (霞) is spring mist. They are the same phenomenon — water droplets suspended in air — but the Japanese tradition insists they are different experiences, and after living through both, I think that's correct. Spring mist is soft and slightly golden; it makes things beautiful. Autumn fog is heavier, more philosophical. It makes things disappear.

*升降* (shōkō) means ascending and descending, rising and falling. Together with the characters before it, the name captures something precise: the fog doesn't simply arrive. It moves. It breathes. It wraps the land in layers, rising from valleys and settling over fields, caught between the warm earth and the cooling sky.

The traditional reading is *Fukaki Kiri Matou* — roughly, "dense fog envelops the land." *Matou* carries the sense of wearing something, of being clothed. The earth wears the fog.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The fog of this micro-season is atmospheric science made visible.

August in Japan is a month of dramatic contrasts. The days are still brutally hot — the ground, the pavement, the rice paddies have been absorbing heat for months. But the nights have begun to cool. Slowly, reluctantly, the temperature slips a degree or two lower than it did in July. The air at ground level retains warmth, but the air above it has begun to cool more quickly.

This temperature differential creates perfect conditions for radiation fog — the most common fog type in Japan. After sunset, the earth radiates its stored heat upward, cooling the immediate layer of air above the ground. When that air cools to its dew point, the water vapor it holds condenses into droplets. Fog forms. By morning, entire valleys can be filled with it, the mountains emerging above like islands in a white sea.

What makes this period distinct is timing. The days are still high summer. The fog that appears at dawn is gone by nine or ten in the morning, burned off by the sun that is still, technically, at its summer strength. The world disappears before breakfast and returns before lunch. This makes the fog of this micro-season unusually fleeting — and, perhaps for that reason, unusually worth noticing.

---

## Two Words for Fog, and Why It Matters

The distinction between *kiri* and *kasumi* is not mere poetry.

In Japanese aesthetics, spring *kasumi* has been associated for centuries with ambiguity, romance, and the veiling of uncomfortable truths. The Heian court used *kasumi* as a metaphor for things seen through the gauze of feeling — love letters half-understood, intentions left soft at the edges. It is the fog of desire.

Autumn *kiri* is different. It is associated with solitude, with separation, with the kind of silence that has weight. When Matsuo Bashō traveled through the Japan countryside, he wrote about the fog that filled the valleys as something that isolated him from the world, that put a particular quality of aloneness into the air:

> 霧しぐれ　富士を見ぬ日ぞ　おもしろき
> *Kiri shigure / Fuji wo minu hi zo / omoshiroki*

*A misty drizzle — / a day when Fuji cannot be seen / is interesting too.*

Bashō's word *omoshiroki* — "interesting," "intriguing," even "entertaining" — is the key move here. The hidden mountain is not a disappointment. The hidden mountain is an experience of its own kind. What is concealed teaches you something about what you cannot see.

---

## A Poem for This Season

One of the oldest fog poems in Japanese literature comes from the *Kokinshū* (905 CE):

> 秋の野に　霧たちわたり　見えぬまで
> 結ぼほれたる　君が心か
>
> *Aki no no ni / kiri tachi watari / mienu made / musuboretaru / kimi ga kokoro ka*

*In the autumn field, / fog spreads until nothing can be seen — / is that how tangled / your heart has become?*

The poet uses the obscuring fog as a metaphor for a state of emotional confusion — a heart so knotted and dense that, like fog, it admits no light through. But there's also something else happening: the poem places you *in* the field, in the actual fog. You feel the texture of standing in it, unable to see.

That double motion — the physical sensation and the interior state reflected through nature — is one of the core gestures of Japanese classical poetry. The fog isn't *like* confusion. The fog *is* the confusion, made visible, standing there in the autumn field.

---

## How to Meet This Season

This is a season for early rising.

The fog of 蒙霧升降 is a morning phenomenon. Set your alarm for the hour before full daylight — five-thirty, six, whatever the light requires where you are — and go outside before the sun has climbed high enough to burn the fog away. This might mean a week of earlier mornings than you're accustomed to, but I would argue it's worth it once.

Stand in it. Do not immediately photograph it, though you may photograph it. Before you do anything else, just stand in the reduced world. Notice how distance has been abolished. Notice how sound travels differently when the air is heavy with moisture. Notice the particular silence of the fog, which is not actual silence but rather a muffling, a softening of all the ordinary edges.

The 72 micro-seasons are, at their core, an invitation to notice what is already there — already happening, already beautiful, already unrepeatable. The fog will lift. It always does.
