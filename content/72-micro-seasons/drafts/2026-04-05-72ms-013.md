# The Swallows Come Home

*Micro-season #13 of 72 — April 5–9*

---

They announce themselves with a sound like scissors cutting air. One moment the sky above the train station or the market street is ordinary; the next, a swallow has sliced through it at an angle, wheels, accelerates, and is gone. Then another. Then the familiar, chittering conversation of a small colony, just arrived, finding their bearings.

The barn swallow (*Hirundo rustica*) — *tsubame* in Japanese — leaves Japan in September or October for Southeast Asia and the Philippines and Indonesia. They spend the northern winter in the south, following the insects. Then, in April, they come back. The same birds, often, to the same nests: under the same eaves of the same farmhouse, in the same shelter of the same fish market, under the same bridge over the same river.

This return has been watched and noted in Japan for over a thousand years. The 13th micro-season of the year is named for it: not "swallows are here" but "swallows arrive." The arrival is the thing. The moment of return is what the calendar honors.

---

## 玄鳥至 — Tsubame kitaru

Three characters. 玄 (*gen* / *kuro*) means dark, profound, mysterious — a character with philosophical weight, referring to a deep, dark color that is neither purely black nor purely navy but something ancient and deep. It appears in Taoist texts to describe the primal mystery of the universe. Here, it describes the bird.

鳥 (*tori* / *chō*) means bird — a pictographic character showing the outline of a bird in profile. Together, 玄鳥 (*genchō* / *tsubame*) means "the dark bird" — a classical name for the swallow, which has dark blue-black upper parts contrasting with its pale underparts and reddish throat.

至 (*kitaru* / *shi*) means to arrive, to reach, to come to a destination.

The full phrase: "The dark bird arrives." The dark bird comes home.

This is the first micro-season of the Seimei solar term — a name meaning "pure and clear" (清明), describing the quality of spring light in early April. The sky is cleaner now, the air more transparent, the light less slanted than it was in February and March. Into this clear spring air, the swallow cuts its return.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Barn swallows that breed in Japan spend their winters primarily in the Philippines, Indonesia, Malaysia, and other parts of Southeast and South Asia. The journey back — across the South China Sea, up through Taiwan and southern China, and into Japan — is timed by day length. As the northern hemisphere days lengthen, the hormonal systems of these birds trigger the urge to return. They navigate by the sun, by the stars, and by magnetic field sensitivity — mechanisms still not fully understood.

Arrival in Japan begins in the warmer southern regions (Kyushu, Shikoku) in late March, and the wave of returning swallows moves northward through April. By the time this micro-season is named, April 5–9, swallows are arriving in much of Honshu and beginning to reoccupy their traditional nest sites.

The swallow's affinity for human structures is ancient and mutual. They nest under eaves, in garages, in the open interiors of stations and markets — anywhere with a horizontal surface protected from rain and predators. Japanese people have traditionally welcomed swallows into their buildings, understanding them as good omens, and providing them with small platforms or leaving gaps in structures specifically for nest access.

The swallow's arrival also marks the true beginning of spring's insect season: the birds return precisely as flying insects become abundant enough to sustain them. The ecology of their timing is elegant.

---

## The Good Omen Under the Eaves

In Japanese tradition, a swallow nesting in your building is deeply auspicious. The swallow (*tsubame*) symbolizes:

- **Marital fidelity**: Swallows are monogamous and return to the same nest sites with the same partners year after year. The sight of a pair of swallows nesting under your eaves was taken as a sign of domestic happiness and stable relationships.

- **Prosperity**: The swallows' arrival and successful breeding in a building was associated with the good fortune of that household. If the swallows left without completing their nesting, it was considered a bad sign.

- **Protection**: In some folk beliefs, the swallow's presence protected the household from fire. This may be connected to the bird's association with water (it flies low over water to catch insects and to drink) and its role as a good-weather indicator.

Even today, in Japan, it is common to see carefully placed boards or hung cardboard sheets beneath active swallow nests to catch droppings — an accommodation made by businesses and households to protect their floors while keeping the birds welcome. The fish markets that allow swallows to nest in their ceilings are practicing a tradition older than modern Japan.

## A Poem for This Season

The swallow (*tsubame*) is a spring kigo in haiku, appearing often in contexts of return, fidelity, and the reliable renewal of the season. Bashō wrote about swallows with characteristic precision:

*古池や / 蛙飛び込む / 水の音*

That is about frogs. But for swallows, from the same haiku master:

*燕よ / われに来たれや / 里の宿*

*Tsubame yo / ware ni kitare ya / sato no yado*

"O swallow — / come to me too, / at the village inn."

Bashō, traveling as a wandering poet, invites the swallow to share his temporary lodging. The bird that finds a home in human eaves becomes, for a moment, a companion for the traveler who has no fixed home. The appeal is gentle and slightly wistful: even the bird knows where it belongs, and Bashō, on the road, envies that certainty.

## How to Meet This Season

In early April, look for swallows wherever there are open skies and human structures. They will be flying erratically — not at altitude but low, banking and sweeping, following the paths of flying insects. You can often watch them from a train platform, a market, a covered shopping arcade with open ends.

Look especially for the moment when a swallow disappears under an eave and does not immediately reappear. It is examining the nest site. It will be back tomorrow.

If you have an eave or a covered outdoor space, consider whether it is hospitable to swallows. The decision to welcome them is, in a small way, a decision to participate in something very old.
