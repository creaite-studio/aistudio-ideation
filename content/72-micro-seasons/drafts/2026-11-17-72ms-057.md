# Where Did All the Rainbows Go?

*Micro-season #57 of 72 — November 17 to November 21*

---

Think about the last rainbow you saw. Really try to place it in time.

If you're in Japan, there's a reasonable chance it was in summer or early autumn — after one of those sudden afternoon thunderstorms that scrub the sky clean and leave the air smelling of rain and ozone. You stepped outside into the wet-bright aftermath and there it was, arcing over the hills or the rice paddies or the apartment buildings, a brief, astonishing fact.

Now think about whether you've ever seen a rainbow in November. Or December. Or January.

Probably not. And the 72 micro-seasons noticed this absence eight centuries before meteorology could explain it.

---

## 虹蔵不見 — Niji Kakurete Miezu

Every character in this name is doing careful work.

*Niji* (虹) is rainbow. The character is composed of the insect radical (虫) on the left and the character for craftsmanship or work (工) on the right. This is one of those ancient observations wrapped into a kanji: in early Chinese cosmology, the rainbow was understood as a kind of creature — a great serpent or dragon drinking from the horizon. The insect radical appears not because rainbows are insects but because it was the general classifier for animals that moved in mysterious ways. The rainbow was categorized with the living and the strange.

*Kakurete* (蔵) means to hide, to conceal oneself, to retreat into storage. It's the same character used in the word for a Japanese storehouse — *kura* (蔵) — those heavy-walled warehouses of lacquered wood and white plaster that you see in old merchant towns, built to keep things safe through the cold months. The rainbow is doing the same thing: going into storage until spring.

*Miezu* (不見) means "cannot be seen." Not "has disappeared" — not gone, just hidden. The phrasing is careful. The rainbow hasn't died. It is waiting.

Put it together: *The rainbow hides away, invisible.* Even in the naming of an absence, there is a promise of return.

---

## What's Actually Happening

A rainbow requires three ingredients: sunlight, water droplets in the air, and the geometry to bring them together.

In summer, Japan has all three in abundance. The sun is high, strong, and consistent. Afternoon storms deliver buckets of large raindrops into warm, humid air. The angle between the sun, the droplets, and your eyes aligns regularly. Rainbows are almost predictable: wait for a summer afternoon storm to clear from the west, turn east, look up.

In November, the geometry begins to fail in several ways simultaneously.

First: the sun is lower. A rainbow always appears centered on the antisolar point — the spot directly opposite the sun in the sky. In summer, when the sun is high, this point is low, and a large arc of rainbow is visible above the horizon. In winter, when the sun barely clears 30 degrees above the horizon, the antisolar point is 30 degrees *below* the horizon. For a rainbow to appear, you'd need the sun to be no more than about 42 degrees above the horizon — and even then, only the very top of the arc might show.

Second: the water is different. Winter precipitation in Japan tends toward snow, sleet, or fine cold drizzle — not the large, round liquid droplets that create brilliant bows. The small droplets of winter fog and mist produce what are called fogbows, which are pale, ghostly, almost colorless. Technically present, but barely visible.

Third: the storms are different. Summer storms tend to be brief and intense, leaving rapidly clearing skies that allow sunlight to stream through while moisture is still in the air. Winter systems are often slow, grey overcast — not the bright-edged squalls that generate rainbows.

The 72 micro-seasons named this absence six hundred years before anyone understood the optics. They simply noticed: after mid-November, the rainbows stop coming.

---

## The Miracle of the Winter Rainbow

Because rainbows are so rare in winter, when one does appear it carries extraordinary weight.

In Japanese culture, a winter rainbow — *fuyu no niji* (冬の虹) — is considered deeply auspicious. The rarity makes it miraculous. Farmers and fisherfolk who spotted one in the cold months would speak of it for days. It was understood as a message from the world beyond ordinary time, a glimpse of something that shouldn't be there.

There is also the matter of color. The few winter rainbows that do appear — when conditions align just right, when a winter sun breaks through a gap in clouds just after a patch of rain — tend to be paler than their summer counterparts. The lower sun angle and smaller droplets conspire to wash out the reds and oranges. What remains is a bow of faint blue and violet, ghostly and uncertain. Some observers report that it's difficult to tell whether you're seeing a rainbow at all, or just a trick of the light.

This ambiguity is itself poetic. The season is named for hiding and not-seeing. Even when the rainbow deigns to appear in winter, it does so reluctantly, half-heartedly, as if unsure it has permission.

This season pairs perfectly with its mirror twin: *Niji hajimete arawaru* (虹始見) — "Rainbows first appear" — which falls in mid-April as the warmth returns. The calendar holds them together, one hiding what the other reveals.

## A Poem for This Season

The poet Kobayashi Issa, who was born in winter and wrote with unusual tenderness about cold and absence, gives us:

> 冬虹や　村に一人の　絵師ありて
> *fuyu niji ya / mura ni hitori no / eshi arite*

*A winter rainbow —*
*in the village, there is*
*one painter.*

The poem works because of its quietness. Something extraordinary has appeared — a winter rainbow, rare and arresting. And the only one who notices it, who can truly receive it, is the single painter in the village. Everyone else is inside, busy with winter tasks. The painter is the one who has learned to watch the sky.

There is something gentle and slightly melancholy in this. Beauty doesn't wait for us to be ready. It appears on its own schedule, in seasons we didn't expect. The question is who is looking.

---

## How to Meet This Season

This is, intentionally, a season of absence. Of not-seeing. Of noticing what is no longer there.

So the practice this week is simpler than finding a thing. It's about training your attention on a gap.

Look at the sky this week, especially after rain. Notice the absence of rainbows. Let yourself feel the way autumn's visual richness — the sudden arcs of color over wet fields — has withdrawn. Think about what else has gone quiet: the swifts have long since departed, the frogs are deep underground, the summer grasses have gone to seed and blown away.

What does it feel like to name an absence? To say: the rainbow has gone into storage?

There is something honest and consoling in this naming. The season doesn't pretend the rainbow has vanished forever. It says: hidden. In storage. Waiting, the way the *kura* warehousekeeper keeps the lacquerware through winter, knowing it will be brought out again when the season turns.

Everything is somewhere. Most things are just waiting.
