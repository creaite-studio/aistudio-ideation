# The Rice Paddies Empty Themselves

*Micro-season #47 of 72 — September 28 to October 2*

---

Walk past a rice paddy in early October and you might notice something has changed.

The water is gone.

Not dramatically — not as if the paddy was suddenly drained overnight. Gradually, over days, the water level dropped. The mud emerged. The rice plants, which stood ankle-deep in water all summer, now rise from a cracked, drying soil that looks nothing like the flooded mirror the paddy was in spring. The landscape that has been defined by reflection — the sky repeated in the water, the mountains doubled — has lost its mirror. The paddy becomes opaque. Solid. Ready.

This is the preparation for harvest. And it has its own micro-season.

---

## 水始涸 — Mizu Hajimete Karuru

Three characters, a complete agricultural story.

*水* (mizu) is water. One of the most fundamental characters in Japanese: the image of water flowing. *Mizu* is everywhere in Japanese life — in tea, in baths, in the phrase *omizu wo kudasai* (水をください, "please give me water"), in the concept of *mizu no kokoro* (水の心, "a mind like water" — calm, clear, adaptable).

*始* (hajime) — we've seen this before. Beginning, for the first time. The calendar uses *hajime* to mark threshold moments: the first frost, the first time the earth grows solemn. *Hajimete* is always urgent.

*涸* (karu or karuru) means to dry up, to run dry — specifically the drying of bodies of water. Rivers that *karu* become exposed riverbeds. Paddies that *karu* reveal their soil. This is not the gradual drying of land after rain; it's the specific emptying of something that held water.

*Water begins, for the first time, to dry.* The season doesn't say "the harvest begins" or "autumn is here." It points at the water leaving — the visible sign that something below the surface of agricultural practice has shifted.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The draining of rice paddies before harvest is called *rakusuikan* (落水管) in Japanese agricultural terminology — literally, "letting the water fall." It's a deliberate, timed process that happens approximately two to four weeks before the rice is cut.

Why drain the paddies?

Wet soil cannot support the weight of harvesting machinery without sinking. Even hand-harvest methods — which are still used in smaller traditional paddies and on steep terraced fields — require firm ground to work efficiently. Beyond the mechanical reasons, drying the field after draining allows the soil to firm up and the base of the rice plant to dry, which makes cutting cleaner and transport easier.

The timing is calibrated carefully against the grain. The paddies are drained when the rice is nearly but not fully mature — the grains still need a few more days of development after draining, which they get from moisture already stored in the plant. Drain too early and you risk under-ripe grain. Drain too late and the paddy becomes difficult to work.

The drained paddy also reveals things that were invisible all summer. The frogs, the water beetles, the small fish that enter paddies from irrigation channels — all of them suddenly exposed or forced to move. Herons and egrets know this and appear in drained paddies almost immediately, working the edges for the sudden abundance.

---

## The Circle of Water

One of the things that becomes visible when you look at the whole cycle of a rice paddy through the 72 micro-seasons is the story of water.

In spring, the paddies were flooded — sheets of standing water that turned the landscape into something vast and reflective. The frogs came with the water (#19, May). The water defined the summer: it regulated temperature, allowed the young rice roots to establish, kept the soil fertile. And now, in autumn, the water goes.

The Japanese have managed this water cycle for over 2,000 years. The irrigation networks that move water from mountain streams into paddies and out again — *suiro* (水路) — are among the most elaborate ecological engineering projects in human history, covering much of rural Japan. The water that fills a paddy in May may have begun as snowmelt on a mountain in February. By October, it's draining back through channels toward rivers that feed the sea.

What the micro-season *Mizu Hajimete Karuru* marks is a specific moment in this vast circulatory system. Not an ending — the water hasn't disappeared. It's simply returned to the larger flow. It will come back next spring, in one form or another.

---

## A Poem for This Season

The drained paddy has its own aesthetic in Japanese culture. It's called *kare田* or more evocatively *ta no kare* — the withered field. After harvest, the stalks are cut short and the field stands bare: a geometry of stubble, frost-touched in the colder months, waiting for spring.

Bashō wrote about the dried field in a haiku that captures both the visual quality and the resonance of emptiness:

> 枯野行く　夢は枯野を　かけ廻る
> *Kareno yuku / yume wa kareno wo / kake meguru*

*Walking through the withered fields — / my dreams run through / the withered fields.*

This was Bashō's final haiku, composed as he lay dying. The *kareno* (枯野, withered field) appears twice — once as physical landscape, once as the landscape of dreams. The poem collapses the boundary between waking and dreaming, between the actual field and the inner field. At the end of things, they are the same place.

The paddy empties. The water begins to dry. The year is rounding toward its close.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Find a rice paddy at this moment of transition, if you can.

In much of Japan — particularly outside major cities, in agricultural valleys, in the terraced fields of mountains — you can walk along paddy edges in early October and witness the drainage. The soil emerging from water has a particular color: dark gray-brown, still wet, beginning to crack. The remaining water pools in low spots, and the edges of those pools often attract birds.

Stand at the edge of a drained paddy and notice the crack patterns in the drying mud. They're different every time — dendritic, like dried river systems seen from above, or like the lines on a palm. The paddy is drying into a unique pattern that no paddy will have again.

The water leaves. But it doesn't disappear. It just goes somewhere else.
