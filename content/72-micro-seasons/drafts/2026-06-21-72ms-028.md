# Three Flowers, One Name, a Thousand Years of Confusion

*Micro-season #28 of 72 — June 21 to June 26*

---

Today is the summer solstice — the longest day of the year — and the calendar marks it with irises.

This feels right. The iris is a flower with a gravity to it: tall, architecturally precise, its petals structured in ways that look almost designed, almost intentional. Purple deepening toward violet at the center. The fall petals curling downward and outward, the standards rising upward. Standing beside a pond or canal filled with irises in full bloom, there is a sense of something formal and serious being conducted. The solstice deserves this kind of flower.

But there is a complication. There is always, in Japan, a linguistic complication.

---

## 菖蒲華 — Ayame Hana Sakau

The name of this season begins with 菖蒲 — and immediately, the problem arises.

菖蒲 is a kanji compound that can be read in at least three different ways, referring to at least two or three different plants, depending on context and era.

In the context of this micro-season, it is read *ayame* and refers to the iris — specifically *Iris sanguinea*, the smaller, blue-purple iris that grows in well-drained soils and has been celebrated in Japanese poetry and painting since the Heian period.

But 菖蒲 is also read *shōbu* when referring to *Acorus calamus*, the sweet flag — a completely different plant with long, sword-shaped leaves that grows in water and is associated with Boys' Day (May 5) for its blade-like form.

And in common modern usage, *hanashōbu* (花菖蒲) refers to *Iris ensata*, the large Japanese water iris cultivated in famous garden displays at places like Meiji Shrine and the Heian Shrine in Kyoto.

One written form. Three plants. A millennium of overlap and deliberate ambiguity that poets exploited freely.

華 (*sakau*) means to bloom abundantly, to flower at peak.

*Irises bloom.* The precise which-iris is, in the classical tradition, almost a secondary question.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The irises that bloom in late June are primarily *Iris ensata* — *hanashōbu* — the Japanese water iris. Unlike the *ayame* of poetry, which blooms in May and grows in dry ground, *hanashōbu* flourishes in shallow water or boggy ground and peaks precisely during the *tsuyu* rainy season.

This timing is not accidental. The plant has evolved for wet conditions; the rains of June are what it requires. In the famous iris gardens of Japan — the Horikiri Shōbu-en in Tokyo, the Meiji Jingu Inner Garden, the Heian Shrine gardens in Kyoto — the flowers are displayed in long, flooded beds at precisely the moment when the blooms are at their most intense. Walking through these gardens during peak bloom — mid-to-late June — you wade through what looks like a purple and white cloud that has settled at knee height and smells of something faintly sweet.

The flowers are large — some cultivated varieties reach 20 centimeters across — and they bloom sequentially rather than all at once, giving the garden a sustained, slowly rotating display over several weeks.

---

## The Sword and the Flower

There is a pun in the Japanese name for iris that every schoolchild learns eventually.

*Shōbu* (菖蒲, the iris/sweet flag) and *shōbu* (勝負, competition/battle) are homophonous in Japanese — they sound identical. This sonic coincidence made the plant a favorite emblem of the samurai class and of Boys' Day festivals, where the sword-like leaves of the sweet flag were floated in bath water and used to thatch rooftops as protective charms.

The iris, in this reading, is a warrior's flower. Its erect standards, its blade-like leaves, its association with competition and martial spirit — all of this made it a natural emblem for families praying for strong, vigorous sons.

This is the other face of a flower that is also, in painting and poetry, a symbol of feminine grace. The Japanese iris holds both associations simultaneously: the delicate and the martial, the ephemeral beauty of the bloom and the durable sharpness of the leaf.

---

## A Poem for This Season

The iris (*ayame* and *kakitsubata* specifically) appears in the *Kokinshū* and in later poetry with remarkable frequency. One of the most celebrated passages in classical literature is Ariwara no Narihira's poem from the *Ise Monogatari*, in which the poet, exiled to the east and sick with homesickness, sees irises blooming along the Yatsuhashi (Eight Bridges) of Mikawa Province:

> から衣　きつつなれにし　つましあれば
> はるばる来ぬる　旅をしぞ思ふ
> *Karakoromo / kitsutsu narenishi / tsuma shi areba*
> *harubaru kinuru / tabi wo shi zo omou*

*Longing for my beloved,*
*far, far away —*
*I am dressed in this court robe*
*long worn and familiar,*
*and I have come so far.*

The poem is an *oritaku* — an acrostic — with the first syllable of each line spelling *kakitsubata*, the Japanese word for rabbit-ear iris. The grief is real; the formal artistry is real; they coexist without either diminishing the other. The iris becomes the shape of the poet's longing.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Iris garden visits are one of the genuine pleasures of the Japanese June.

The major gardens — Horikiri Shōbu-en in Katsushika, Tokyo; the Meiji Shrine Inner Garden; Kenrokuen in Kanazawa — typically hold iris viewing festivals in mid-to-late June, aligned with peak bloom. Admission is small or free; crowds are manageable on weekday mornings.

If you can't visit a famous garden, look for the irises that appear in drainage channels, canal banks, and neighborhood parks throughout this season. The wild or semi-wild specimens are less spectacular than the cultivated varieties but more honest — growing where the water is, blooming in the rain, doing their seasonal work without an audience.

And take a moment today, the longest day: notice what the light does at this latitude when the sun doesn't set until well past seven. Notice where the year has brought you. The solstice is the turning point we cross through without fanfare, the moment the days start contracting again, so imperceptibly at first that you won't notice for weeks.

The irises are the calendar's marker for this hinge. They bloom at the exact moment when summer has arrived and, simultaneously, begun its turn toward autumn.
