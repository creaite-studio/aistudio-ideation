# What the Deer Lets Go

*Micro-season #64 of 72 — December 22 to December 26*

---

Once a year, the deer walks out of the forest lighter.

Not lighter in mood — or not primarily. Lighter in the most literal sense: several kilograms of bone simply drop away, leaving behind two small, velvet-capped pedestals where the antlers were. The deer turns its head, and where yesterday there was a crown of bone reaching toward the winter sky, today there is nothing.

The antlers will regrow. They always do. By spring they'll be pushing upward again in soft velvet. By summer they'll be hard and branching. By autumn they'll be weapons in the rut.

But right now, in the week after the winter solstice, the deer has put them down.

---

## 麋角解 — Sawashika no Tsuno Otsu

The creature in this season's name is the source of some debate.

*Mi* (麋) in classical Chinese referred to a large deer — often interpreted as a moose or elk (*Alces alces*), the largest of the deer family. The character combines the deer radical (鹿) with another element meaning "not" or "lacking." Some scholars read this as "the large deer" whose character was used in ancient texts for the giant elk of northern Asia.

In Japan, where true moose and elk don't exist, the character *mi* (麋) has been applied to the native *sika* deer (*Cervus nippon*) — *sawashika* (沢鹿, "marsh deer") in this context. The *sika* is Japan's own deer: elegant, spotted in summer, found from Hokkaido to Yakushima. It is the deer of the *Man'yōshū* and the *Kojiki*, the deer of Nara Park who bow for crackers, the deer of Shinto's sacred iconography.

*Tsuno* (角) is horn or antler. The character shows a horned animal's head seen from above.

*Otsu* (解) means to come apart, to dissolve, to be released. It's the same character used in words for understanding — *rikai* (理解), to comprehend, to "dissolve the reason." The antler is not broken or cut; it is *released*. The bond between bone and skull is chemically dissolved.

The deer releases its antlers.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Antlers are, by a significant margin, the fastest-growing tissue in the animal kingdom.

A male sika deer will grow a new set of antlers each year, beginning in late spring and completing by late summer. During the peak growth period, antlers can grow by more than a centimeter per day — a rate that, if maintained in any other tissue, would be classified as pathological. The antlers are supplied by blood-carrying velvet: a soft, vascular skin covering the developing bone that dries and peels as the antlers calcify.

By autumn, the antlers are fully mineralized — as hard as bone, though structurally different, being solid throughout rather than having a marrow cavity. Through the rut — the autumn mating season — the male uses them in combat and display. And then, as testosterone levels drop in late autumn and early winter, a process of osteoclast activity (bone-resorbing cells) begins at the pedicle — the base where antler meets skull. Slowly, the bond weakens. By December or January, a relatively small impact can cause the antler to simply fall away.

The timing is governed by photoperiod — day length — and it pairs with the winter solstice precisely. The lengthening days that begin after December 21st will, after a few months, trigger the hormonal cascade that starts new antler growth. The solstice is both the endpoint of the shedding and the starting signal for what comes next.

In the forests, dropped antlers are significant ecological resources. Rodents gnaw them for the minerals — calcium and phosphorus — they contain. Other deer will chew them. Eventually they dissolve into the soil, returning their mineral content to the ground.

## The Mirror of the Season

This micro-season has a counterpart earlier in the year: micro-season #25, in late June, is *Shika no tsuno oyu* (鹿角解) — "Deer shed their velvet." The two seasons bracket the antler cycle: velvet shed in summer as antlers harden, antlers shed in winter as the cycle completes.

But there's a subtler pairing in the 72-season system. The previous season — *Nattō shōzu*, selfheal sprouting — marks the winter solstice with something being born. This season — antlers falling — marks the same period with something being released. Birth and release at the same moment. Growth and letting-go happening simultaneously.

Japanese aesthetics have a name for this kind of paired understanding: *in-yō* (陰陽) — the same concept that gives us *yin and yang* in Chinese thought. Every darkness contains the seed of light; every ending contains a beginning. The deer that lets go of its antlers is already, at the cellular level, beginning to grow the next pair. The solstice that marks the year's maximum darkness is the moment from which light begins to return.

## A Poem for This Season

The *Man'yōshū* contains a famous poem by Kakinomoto no Hitomaro that uses the deer's autumn call as its central image:

> 奥山に　紅葉踏みわけ　鳴く鹿の　声聞く時ぞ　秋は悲しき
> *okuyama ni / momiji fumiwake / naku shika no / koe kiku toki zo / aki wa kanashiki*

*In the deep mountains,*
*stepping through fallen red leaves,*
*the deer cries —*
*when I hear that voice*
*autumn is most sorrowful.*

This is an autumn poem, not a winter one, but it goes to the heart of the deer's cultural significance in Japan. The autumn cry of the male sika deer is extraordinarily distinctive — a high, bugling whistle that carries across mountain valleys. For the *Man'yōshū* poets, this sound was one of the primary triggers of *aware* — that particular Japanese sense of poignant beauty, the sorrowful recognition of transience.

By the time winter arrives and this micro-season begins, the male has stopped calling. The rut is over, the battles are done, the females are pregnant. The deer walks quietly through December forests, carrying its antlers for a few final weeks before they fall. After all that autumn noise, the winter silence is conspicuous.

---

## How to Meet This Season

The week between the solstice and Christmas — or the New Year, depending on your tradition — is structurally a period of release in most cultures. The old year is ending. The new hasn't quite started. There is a gap.

The deer's lesson for this gap is physical and immediate: what is it time to put down?

Not in a self-help sense — not "what habits should you break in the new year." More literally than that. Look at what you are carrying. The commitments that have outlasted their purpose, the projects that ran their course, the ways of thinking about yourself that belong to a phase that's ending.

The antlers served their function. They grew to their full complexity, they were used in the ways they were meant to be used, and now the chemistry of the season is dissolving the bond that holds them. This is not failure. This is the proper end of a cycle.

Walk outside this week, if you can, into whatever wild or semi-wild space is near you. Look at the ground, especially in autumn-leafed forest areas, for shed antlers — though they're rare and quickly gnawed. Look at the bare deer, if you can find them, carrying the stumps of their velvet-capped pedicles in the December cold.

Then carry less.
