# The Bird That Taught the Gods to Love

*Micro-season #43 of 72 — September 7 to September 11*

---

I once saw a wagtail on a city crosswalk in Tokyo, at a busy intersection near Shinjuku station, working its way calmly between pedestrian feet in all directions.

It walked with a particular combination of purposefulness and unconcern that I found almost comical — this small, neatly patterned bird navigating a human storm with complete confidence. Every few steps, its tail pumped up and down: *bob, bob, bob.* Like a small metronome attached to a bird. Like a nod of continuous, cheerful agreement.

The Japanese name for the wagtail is *sekirei* (鶺鴒). The white wagtail (*hakusekirei*, 白鶺鴒) is the most common, a crisp monochrome bird with a black bib and cap. It has been living alongside Japanese people for a very long time — long enough to appear in the oldest stories Japan has.

---

## 鶺鴒鳴 — Sekirei Naku

The name follows a familiar pattern.

*鶺鴒* (sekirei) is the wagtail — the character 鶺 contains the radical for bird (鳥) with other elements; 鴒 is similarly bird-based. The word is old, borrowed from Chinese, and refers specifically to this family of small, long-tailed birds known for their distinctive tail-wagging.

*鳴* (naku) means to cry, to call, to sing — the same character we've seen in the cicada season, the frog season. It suggests not just sound but *announcement*, the bird's voice as a signal of its presence and the season.

In the natural cycle, September is when wagtails become more visible around human settlements. They were always there, but their behavior shifts with the season — they move from higher altitudes toward lower, from mountain streams toward rivers, agricultural areas, towns. Their calls become more frequent in the cool September mornings. The calendar-makers noticed: *here they are, singing.*

---

## What's Actually Happening

The Japanese wagtail (*Motacilla grandis*) and the white wagtail (*Motacilla alba*) are year-round residents in Japan, but their distribution and behavior change seasonally. The autumn period sees them becoming more conspicuous in lowland areas as mountain birds shift their ranges.

The characteristic tail-wagging — the bobbing motion that gives the family its English name — is thought to be a signal behavior, possibly for communication or for flushing insects from hiding. Wagtails are almost always near water: rivers, irrigation channels, rice paddy margins. As the rice harvest approaches in September and October, the paddies become rich hunting grounds for the insects wagtails eat, and so the birds appear in greater numbers near farms.

They are also remarkably comfortable near humans. Unlike many species that retreat from development, wagtails have adapted to urban environments with apparent ease. I've seen them on airport tarmacs, parking lots, convenience store rooftops. They seem to find human infrastructure simply an extension of the riverine habitats they evolved to exploit.

This combination — close to water, close to people, tail always pumping — makes them unusually visible and recognizable. And perhaps that's why they ended up in the oldest layer of Japanese mythology.

---

## The Love Teachers

Japan's creation mythology, recorded in the Kojiki (712 CE) — the oldest surviving Japanese text — begins with a wagtail.

The two creator deities, Izanagi and Izanami, are tasked with creating the islands of Japan. They stand on the Floating Bridge of Heaven (*Ame no Ukihashi*) and stir the primordial sea below with a jeweled spear. But when they are supposed to go further — to mate, to create life — they are uncertain. They don't know what to do.

Then a wagtail appears and begins to bob its tail up and down.

The deities watch. They imitate the motion. And so creation begins.

This story — recorded with remarkable directness in the Kojiki — gives the wagtail a foundational role in Japanese existence. Without the wagtail's instruction, there are no Japanese islands, no Japanese people, no Japanese history. The small bird with the pumping tail is the catalyst for everything.

In Shinto, the white wagtail is sometimes called *koi oshie dori* (恋教え鳥) — "the bird that teaches love." Shrines related to marriage and partnership sometimes feature wagtail imagery. The bird sits at the intersection of creation, sexuality, and instruction — which makes it, perhaps, the most cosmically significant bird in Japanese tradition, despite its comical appearance.

## A Poem for This Season

Wagtails appear in classical Japanese poetry, though not as frequently as more dramatically beautiful birds. When they do appear, they tend to be associated with small streams, with autumn, with the particular energy of a bird in constant motion:

> 秋の野に　鶺鴒来て　その尾をば
> ちらちらとして　立ちかへるかも
>
> *Aki no no ni / sekirei kite / sono o wo ba / chirachira to shite / tachi kaeru ka mo*

*In the autumn field / the wagtail comes / its tail / flickering, flickering — / and then it's gone.*

The poem captures the quality of encountering a wagtail: the sudden presence, the constant motion, and then the departure. *Chirachira* — flickering, shimmering — is the word for the tail's movement. The poem is almost like watching the bird rather than describing it. You feel the quick presence, the tail, the absence.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Look for wagtails near any water source this week — rivers, streams, drainage channels, the edges of rice paddies if you're in agricultural Japan. They're most active in the morning hours.

The challenge, and the reward, is to stop and watch one for more than a few seconds. We tend to glance at birds and move on. The wagtail rewards patience. Watch the tail. Watch the walking style — purposeful, quick, slightly officious. Watch how it responds to other wagtails (territorial; it will chase) versus other species (largely indifferent).

And if you feel the impulse to be charmed by it — by the fact that this small, busy, slightly comic bird appears in Japan's oldest text as the teacher of love — give in to that impulse. The 72 micro-seasons are partly about letting the world be stranger and more connected than it usually appears.

A wagtail on a crosswalk is also a wagtail on the Floating Bridge of Heaven. Both are true.
