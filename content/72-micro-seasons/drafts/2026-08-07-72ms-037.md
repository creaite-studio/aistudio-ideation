# The Cicadas That Sing of Endings

*Micro-season #37 of 72 — August 7 to August 11*

---

There is a particular quality of late afternoon in early August that I have never been able to properly describe to anyone who hasn't lived through a Japanese summer.

The heat is still ferocious. The light still hits at that crushing mid-summer angle. But somewhere in the layered noise of the day — the aburazemi drilling their relentless mechanical chorus from every tree — you start to hear something else. A different voice. Higher, more liquid, faintly melancholy. It comes in waves, like breathing. *Kana, kana, kana.*

The higurashi has started singing.

And when the higurashi starts, you know, without anyone having to tell you, that summer is turning. Not ending. Not yet. But *turning*. Something has shifted in the air, some invisible dial has moved a single notch, and the cicadas know it before we do.

---

## 寒蝉鳴 — Kansemi Naku

The 37th micro-season carries a name worth sitting with.

*寒* (kan) means cold, or — more precisely in this context — autumn. It's the character you'll find in words like 寒気 (*kanki*, cold air) and 寒波 (*kanpa*, cold wave). Used here to modify a cicada, it signals not the winter cold but something subtler: the cool edge that creeps into August mornings, barely perceptible but unmistakably present.

*蝉* (semi) is cicada — that iconic insect of Japanese summer, the one that has filled every haiku, every anime, every childhood memory of being sent outside while adults talked in quiet rooms.

*鳴* (naku) means to cry, to call, to sing — the same character used for frogs in the spring micro-season, for birds at dawn.

Together: *The autumn cicadas sing.* But the name points not to just any cicada. The star of this season is the *higurashi* (ヒグラシ, *Tanna japonensis*), whose name breaks apart as *hi* — day — and *gurashi* — to spend, to pass. "The one that passes the day." An insect whose whole existence is defined by the movement of light and time.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Japan has around 30 species of cicada, and they don't all sing at once. The summer unfolds in a cicada succession: the *niiniizemi* comes first in June, high-pitched and thin; the enormous *kumazemi* dominates the peak heat of July with its mechanical roar; the *aburazemi* — the most common species in Honshu — forms the dense wall of sound that most people simply call "summer."

And then, overlapping with the aburazemi's fading peak, comes the *higurashi*.

The higurashi is different from the others in one crucial way: it sings at dawn and at dusk, not at midday. Its call is triggered by low-light conditions — morning and evening, or on overcast days when the canopy darkens. This means its sound is woven into the liminal hours, the transitional light, the moments when summer briefly loses its grip.

Scientists call this a response to light intensity thresholds. Poets have always called it something else.

Close behind the higurashi comes the *tsukutsukuboushi* (*Meimuna opalifera*) — a smaller cicada with one of the most elaborate calls in the insect world. Its name is onomatopoeia: *tsuku-tsuku-bo-shi*, an ascending, urgent pattern that speeds up before suddenly stopping. The tsukutsukuboushi is widely understood to be among the last cicadas of summer, and hearing it for the first time each August carries a specific, hard-to-articulate weight. Summer is counting down.

---

## The Sound That Means Mono no Aware

The higurashi has occupied a special place in Japanese aesthetics for over a thousand years.

While the loud summer cicadas (especially the aburazemi) are classified as *summer* season words (kigo) in haiku, the higurashi is categorized differently. It is an *autumn* kigo — not because it appears only in autumn, but because its sound evokes autumn. There is a distinction, and the distinction matters enormously to anyone who has grown up listening to the higurashi at dusk: the sound contains something like longing. It curves downward at the end of each phrase. It fades in and out like something being remembered.

The Japanese have a word for this feeling: *mono no aware* (物の哀れ) — the pathos of things, the bittersweet awareness that everything is temporary and beautiful precisely because it will not last. The higurashi is considered one of its purest expressions in nature.

An ancient poem from the Man'yōshū (around the 8th century) captures the feeling:

> *ひぐらしの鳴く山里の夕暮れは*
> *風よりほかに問ふ人もなし*
>
> *Higurashi no / naku yamazato no / yūgure wa / kaze yori hoka ni / tou hito mo nashi*

*In the mountain village where the higurashi sing / at dusk / there is no one to visit me / but the wind.*

---

## A Poem for This Season

The most famous poem about the higurashi comes from Matsuo Bashō's disciple Yosa Buson, though Bashō himself wrote about the sound in his travel journal *Oku no Hosomichi*. But perhaps the most piercing captures come from anonymous poets of the Man'yōshū, who heard in that evening cry exactly what we still hear now — time, passing.

> *ひぐらしの鳴きぬる時はをみなえし*
> *咲きたる野辺を行きつつ見べし*
>
> *When the higurashi begins to cry / walk out to the meadow where the maiden flowers bloom / and look.*

The poem does something elegant: it pairs the sound of the cicada with the image of the autumn flower, *ominaeshi* (女郎花), a yellow meadow plant that blooms precisely in this micro-season. The instruction is simple: when you hear it, go outside. Pay attention. The season will not hold.

This is what the 72 micro-seasons keep asking of us, in different voices and different forms.

---

## How to Meet This Season

In Japan, step outside at dusk this week. Not at six o'clock. At that uncertain hour between seven and eight, when the light is going golden-gray. Listen past the aburazemi. Listen for the sound that waves and fades, the one that sounds like it might be about to say something.

If you're outside Japan, find the equivalent transition in whatever sound surrounds you. Every summer has its way of announcing that it's started to lean toward fall — some shift in the quality of light or wind or birdcall. The Japanese spent centuries naming these shifts with precise, beautiful words, because they believed that noticing was a form of care.

Listen for the voice that sounds like it's saying goodbye before anyone has asked it to leave.
