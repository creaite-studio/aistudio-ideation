# The Green Beneath the White

*Micro-season #65 of 72 — December 27 to January 4*

---

If you could lift the snow off a winter wheat field — peel it back like a white cloth — you would find something green underneath.

Not much. A few centimeters of delicate blades, pale from reduced light but unmistakably alive. The snow hasn't killed the wheat. It has been keeping it warm.

This is the paradox that this season tries to hold: that the white covering of winter is not only deprivation but also protection. That the thing you can't see — the green hidden under snow — is not dying but growing. That the year that looks finished, as we move through the last days of December, is not finished at all.

---

## 雪下出麦 — Yuki Watarite Mugi Nobiru

Four characters, two images held together.

*Yuki* (雪) is snow. The character shows rain (雨) at the top with a broom (彗, modified) below it — snow as that which is swept away, as that which falls from above the same way rain does but which can be gathered and cleared. Snow is handled rain, in a sense. Snow you can do something with.

*Shita* (下) means below, underneath. One horizontal stroke, longer than the other, with a short vertical drop: the spatial relationship of "under" made visual.

*Mugi* (麦) is wheat or barley — the character shows grain stalks with roots below the ground line. In Japan, both wheat and barley are called *mugi*; the tradition associated with this micro-season involves winter wheat specifically, though both crops were planted in autumn and grow through the cold.

*Nobiru* (出) means to come out, to emerge, to extend upward. In this context, the wheat is *coming out* from under the snow — or rather, reaching out through the snow, finding the gap between the snow's weight and the sky's light.

Snow. Below. Wheat. Emerging. The entire season in four characters.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Winter wheat (*Triticum aestivum*) is what farmers call a "cold-requiring" crop. In a process called vernalization, the wheat's biological clock needs a sustained period of temperatures close to freezing in order to transition from vegetative growth to reproductive development. Without that cold period, the plant will keep growing leaves but never produce grain.

This means that snow is not the wheat's enemy. Snow is, in many ways, its ally.

A light snow cover of even five or ten centimeters acts as insulating blanket — the *igloo effect* — keeping the soil temperature from dropping as low as it would in bare, exposed fields. While air temperatures above the snow might reach -10°C or lower on cold nights, the soil under the snow and the base of the wheat plants may remain near 0°C. This is the temperature range in which the wheat continues slow growth without freezing to death.

In Japan, there is a traditional agricultural practice associated with this season called *mugi-fumi* (麦踏み) — literally "wheat-treading." In midwinter, farmers walk through their wheat fields, pressing the seedlings down with their feet. This counterintuitive act stimulates the wheat: being physically compressed causes the plant to produce more tillers (side-shoots), leading to bushier growth and more grain heads at harvest. It also firms the soil around the roots, which can heave and loosen as the ground repeatedly freezes and thaws.

*Mugi-fumi* would have been performed during this very week in old agricultural Japan. Groups of farmers — sometimes children — walked the fields in lines, treading down the young green wheat poking up from the December soil. A practical act made ceremonial by its timing: working the fields in the dead of winter, keeping the cycle turning even when everything seemed still.

## The Year-Crossing Grass

The wheat of this season has another name that appears in old Japanese texts: *toshikoshi-gusa* (年越し草) — the year-crossing grass.

*Toshikoshi* (年越し) is the concept of "crossing over the year" — passing from one year to the next, the transition period around December 31st and January 1st. The wheat, seen pushing green through the December soil in the days around the new year, was understood as bridging the two years. It was planted in the old year; it would be harvested in the new. It crossed the year-boundary as a living thing, carrying the continuity of agricultural life from one calendar cycle to the next.

This micro-season itself spans the year boundary — running from December 27 to January 4 — which is perhaps why it carries the longest run of any micro-season, covering the New Year period. The wheat doesn't observe the new year. It is indifferent to calendars. It simply grows.

There's something consoling in this. The wheat under the snow doesn't know it's the end of December. It doesn't experience the cultural weight of year-end review, the anxiety of new beginnings, the pressure of resolutions. It grows because growth is what it does. The snow is there; it grows under it. The calendar turns; it grows through it.

## A Poem for This Season

The 19th-century poet and scholar Masaoka Shiki, who did much to revitalize haiku in the Meiji period, wrote simply:

> 麦踏みや　冬の野山の　青さかな
> *mugifumi ya / fuyu no noyama no / aosa kana*

*Treading wheat —*
*the bluish green of winter*
*hills and fields.*

The poem is physically immediate. Shiki gives you the action first — *mugifumi* — and then the surprising observation: the fields in winter are not grey but *blue-green*. The young wheat, seen at a slight distance, gives the winter landscape a particular hue that's distinct from spring green and summer green. This is a color note from someone who was paying attention.

The haiku captures the experience of doing an agricultural task — probably cold, probably tedious — and being stopped short by the quality of the color. Winter work opens the eye to winter beauty.

## How to Meet This Season

This micro-season spans the new year, which means it arrives when many of us are in the throes of year-end reflection and resolution. The wheat has a different suggestion.

Before you make new year's plans, go outside. Find the ground. If you're in a snowy climate, look for any green thing pushing through — even weeds count. If you're in a warmer place, find a patch of winter grass, or any living plant growing in the cold weeks of late December.

Crouch down and look at it. Notice that it's growing. Without a plan, without resolve, without a new year's resolution — it's growing because that's what it does.

The Japanese new year (*oshōgatsu*, お正月) is a period of purification and setting intentions, yes — but also of rest, of being with family, of *osechi* (御節) food boxes and pine decorations and a kind of deliberate slowness that acknowledges the weight of the transition. You don't have to be productive over new year. The wheat is handling that.

On one of the days between December 27 and January 4, walk somewhere you can see the earth rather than pavement. Try to find something green. You don't have to do anything about it. Just acknowledge: growth is happening, out of sight, under the white.

The green is there under the snow.
