# What the Ancients Believed Grass Became

*Micro-season #26 of 72 — June 9 to June 13*

---

The name of this season is, strictly speaking, wrong.

It says that rotting grass becomes fireflies. That the decaying matter at the edge of rice paddies, wet with rain and warm with early summer, transforms into the floating lights that drift above the water on June nights. This is not true — not biologically, not in any literal sense. Fireflies do not emerge from rotting grass. They spend their larval stage in the soil, eating snails and small invertebrates, breathing through gills in the moist earth beside streams and paddies.

But the name is 2,000 years old, inherited from a Chinese agricultural text that described the world as ancient people actually saw it: a mystery of lights appearing, as if from nowhere, exactly where the wet grass decomposed. And I find that I cannot be dismissive about it. The people who wrote that name were paying close attention. They just didn't have the conceptual tools yet to explain what they saw.

This is, in its way, the most honest thing you can say about a season.

---

## 腐草為螢 — Kusaretaru Kusa Hotaru to Naru

Four characters, a belief encoded in language.

腐 (*kusareta*) means rotted, decayed, decomposing. 草 (*kusa*) means grass, or more broadly, plant matter. 為 (*to naru*, here acting as "becomes") signals transformation. 螢 (*hotaru*) is firefly — the character itself contains the radical for fire (火) surrounded by an outer component, suggesting the flame enclosed in something, which is a fairly accurate depiction of what a firefly's abdomen does.

*Rotting grass becomes fireflies.* The sentence is a description of a transformation the writers believed they were witnessing. It is also a kind of poetry — the dark and decomposing becoming luminous, the dying becoming something that lights up the night.

Even knowing it's not literally true, there is something in the image worth keeping.

---

## What's Actually Happening

In early to mid-June, Japan enters the first weeks of *tsuyu*, the plum rains. Warm fronts clash with cooler air from the north; rain falls for days at a time, patchy and soft. The rice paddies are full of standing water. Streams run fast and clean from mountain snowmelt and recent rain.

And into this warm, wet, lush world, adult fireflies emerge from the earth.

The two most celebrated Japanese species are *Luciola cruciata* (Genji fireflies, *Genjihotaru*) and *Luciola lateralis* (Heike fireflies, *Heikehotaru*). Their names reference the two warring clans of the 12th-century Genpei War — a connection without ecological basis but with great emotional resonance. The Genji are the larger, slower flashers; the Heike blink more rapidly. Finding them together above the same stream is, in the Japanese poetic imagination, like watching the ghosts of ancient warriors reconciled in light.

The adult firefly's entire existence above ground lasts only one to two weeks. It does not eat — its adult digestive system is vestigial, incapable of processing food. It lives entirely on energy stored during its long larval stage, spending that stored energy on one purpose: finding a mate, producing the next generation, and then going dark.

The *tsuyu* rains, paradoxically, make firefly-watching possible. They clear the air of dust and humidity that would obscure the tiny lights. After a rainy day, on the brief clear evenings, the fireflies rise in numbers.

## Sei Shōnagon and the Dark Night

Japan's literary tradition has engaged with fireflies more intimately than perhaps any other culture.

Sei Shōnagon, writing her *Pillow Book* in the late 10th century, included fireflies in her famous list of beautiful things:

*"Not only when the moon shines, but on dark nights too, as the fireflies flit to and fro — how beautiful it is."*

The parenthetical emphasis matters: *dark nights.* The firefly is most itself in absolute darkness. The moon, which illuminates all other beautiful things in the classical Japanese aesthetic, actually competes with the firefly's light. The firefly needs the dark in order to be seen.

Kobayashi Issa wrote more than 100 haiku about fireflies — more than any other poet in the Japanese tradition. His engagement with them spans his entire career, from playful observation to something approaching elegy. In one of his most celebrated:

> 蛍火や　また消えてある　闇の中
> *Hotarubi ya / mata kiete aru / yami no naka*

*The firefly's light —*
*gone again,*
*in the darkness.*

The poem holds both the light and its absence. The firefly is the thing that appears and disappears, that is there and then not there, and the darkness is not an absence but a presence — the *yami* (�darkness) that the light inhabits briefly before going out.

This is the Buddhist reading that hangs over all Japanese firefly literature: the light is impermanent. The darkness is what remains.

---

## The Ancient Error as Truth

Here is what I keep returning to.

The people who wrote "rotting grass becomes fireflies" were standing at the edge of a paddy on a June night, watching lights appear from the wet, decomposing grass at the waterline. They were right that the fireflies were emerging from that place. They were right about the timing — exactly now, in this specific week. They were right that the decay and the luminescence were connected, though they had the causality backwards.

They were wrong in a way that contains a kind of truth.

The firefly larva spends years in the earth eating the things that decompose: the snails that feed on decaying plant matter, the small creatures of the soil that break down the grass. Remove the rotting grass, remove the aquatic ecosystem of the paddy edge, and there are no fireflies. The transformation the ancients believed they saw was real at some level they lacked the vocabulary to express.

*The decaying gives rise to the luminous.* Not directly, not literally. But the logic holds.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Firefly viewing — *hotarugari* — is one of the most beloved of Japan's summer rituals.

The best spots are clean streams and irrigation channels in agricultural areas, away from artificial light. Municipal parks sometimes maintain firefly habitats deliberately. The viewing happens in the hour after full dark, from about 8 to 10 p.m., during the two-week peak of adult emergence.

If you go: leave your phone in your pocket. Let your eyes adjust. Resist the urge to photograph. A firefly photograph almost never captures what it feels like to stand in a dark field while cold light drifts past at waist height, going on and off, on and off, in patterns you can almost but never quite predict.

Some things are ruined by recording. This is one of them.
