# The Wind That Knows Where to Cut

*Micro-season #58 of 72 — November 22 to November 26*

---

The last leaves don't fall the way the first ones do.

In early autumn, when the maples first begin to turn and a red leaf detaches and spirals down, there's something almost leisurely about it. The tree is trying something out. The leaf drifts.

By late November, it's different. The north wind comes, and the remaining leaves — the stubborn ones, the ones that held on through October and the first half of November — come down fast. Not gently. They rattle off the branches in gusts, rolling across pavements, piling against walls and fences. The trees are stripped. This is not a drifting. This is a clearing.

The 72 micro-seasons named this wind carefully.

---

## 朔風払葉 — Sakufū ha o Harau

There are four characters in this name, and the most interesting one comes first.

*Saku* (朔) is an ancient word for the north, but its meaning goes deeper than compass direction. *Saku* also refers to the new moon — the first day of the lunar month, when the moon is invisible, hidden in darkness. In classical Chinese and Japanese cosmology, north and darkness and beginning were all linked: the north was where winter lived, where things became cold and concealed and gathered themselves for a new cycle.

The character itself shows the moon (月) with a mark indicating its dark phase. A wind from the direction of concealment and new beginnings: there is something philosophically loaded in this name that a simpler word like "north wind" (*kitakaze*) would miss.

*Fū* (風) is simply wind. But it's also the character that shows up in *fūdo* (風土) — climate, the character of a place as shaped by wind and soil — and in *fūryū* (風流) — elegance, refined taste, the appreciation of natural beauty. Wind, in Japanese culture, is not just meteorology. It carries aesthetic and moral implications.

*Harau* (払) means to sweep, to clear away, to remove impurities. It's the verb used in Shinto purification rites — the priest waves the *haraigushi* (祓串) wand to sweep away *kegare* (impurity, spiritual pollution). The north wind is performing a ritual purification. It is sweeping the leaves from the branches the way a priest sweeps contamination from a sacred space.

*Ha* (葉) is leaf. Just leaf. Simple, unambiguous.

The north wind sweeps the leaves away. The north wind purifies.

---

## What's Actually Happening

November's cold winds in Japan arrive primarily from the northwest, down from Siberia and the Asian continent, funneled through the Japan Sea and over the mountains of the central ranges.

These aren't just cold winds — they're extraordinarily dry. As they descend from the mountains onto the Pacific side of Honshu, they warm slightly through adiabatic compression, which makes them even drier relative to the ambient air. In traditional Japanese understanding, the *karakkaze* (空っ風) — the "empty wind" of the Kantō Plain and surrounding regions — is one of the most characteristic signatures of the season. It is a wind that takes things away and leaves nothing behind.

For the trees, this wind is the final signal in a sequence that began months earlier. Leaf-fall in deciduous trees is not simply caused by cold — it's a precisely orchestrated biochemical process triggered by shortening days. As the length of darkness increases past a species-specific threshold, trees begin producing ethylene, which triggers the formation of the *abscission layer* — a thin line of cells at the base of each leaf stem that eventually severs the leaf's connection to the tree.

By late November, this process is complete in most deciduous species. The leaves were ready to fall. What the north wind does is finish the job efficiently, clearing the last attached leaves in a few vigorous days. What took weeks in the calm of October takes hours in the gust of late November.

After the wind passes, you can see what was hidden. The structure of trees, bare now, revealed against the grey sky. The nests in the high branches, suddenly visible. The shape of the canopy, which the leaves had been concealing all summer.

## The Poetry of the Empty Tree

There's an aesthetic in Japanese culture called *karumi* (軽み) — lightness, the beauty of what's not there. Bashō developed it late in his career as a counterpoint to the heavier, more elaborate aesthetic of his earlier haiku. The empty tree is an example of *karumi*: its beauty is in its stripped-down truth.

But there is also *mono no aware* — the poignant beauty of transience — and bare trees carry this too. The fallen leaves represent everything that was full and bright and lush about the past year, now reduced to wet brown layers on the ground. There is real loss here. The north wind is not gentle about it.

In old Japanese poetry, the north wind in autumn is often paired with images of travel and separation. A person on a journey, exposed to the stripping wind, is a figure of particular poignancy. The wind that removes leaves removes protection — from trees and from people. It reveals. It exposes.

Farmers, meanwhile, had a more practical relationship with this wind. The same *karakkaze* that stripped trees also dried the harvested grain spread out on reed mats, the root vegetables hung under eaves to cure, the daikon sliced and suspended on bamboo frames for *kiriboshi-daikon* (切干大根) — the dried radish strips that would last through winter. The stripping wind was also the drying wind. It had its uses.

## A Poem for This Season

Bashō, traveling in autumn, wrote:

> 木枯らしや　海に夕日を　ふき落とす
> *kogarashi ya / umi ni yūhi o / fuki otosu*

*The cold winter wind —*
*blowing the evening sun down*
*into the sea.*

*Kogarashi* (木枯らし) — literally "tree-witherer" — is the traditional name for this precise autumn-into-winter wind, and Bashō gives it a startling scale. The wind is so powerful, so absolute, that it blows the *sun itself* down into the ocean. The image is visually precise — a sun dropping fast toward a horizon, wind-hurried — and also metaphysically enormous. Everything is subject to this wind. Even light.

The poem holds the whole scale of the season in seventeen syllables. The bare tree, the dried field, the stripped hillside — all of these are compressed into a single image of the sun being swept away.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Go outside in the wind this week. Not to shelter from it — to meet it.

Find a deciduous tree that still has leaves, if you can, and watch it in a gust. Watch the way the remaining leaves stream and snap on their stems, the way some release and others hold on. Notice the sound — not the gentle rustle of October but the harder, papery rattle of late November, leaves already dry, already more dead than alive.

Then, after the gust passes: notice the silence. The deep, particular quiet that follows a wind event. The air has been swept through. The debris has settled or blown on. For a moment, everything is clean.

The wind that strips is also the wind that clarifies. After it passes, you can see through the bare branches to the sky, to the shapes of distant hills, to things that were hidden all summer long. The north wind takes the leaves, and gives you back the view.
