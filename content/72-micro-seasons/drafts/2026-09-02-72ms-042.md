# Every Blade of Grass, a Small Grief

*Micro-season #42 of 72 — September 2 to September 6*

---

Go outside early enough, before the sun has cleared the trees, and you'll find the grass covered in it.

White dew. Not water, exactly — or not only water. Something more deliberate-looking, as if each individual drop had been placed with care. On the tip of a grass blade, a sphere of such perfect roundness it seems like a small planet. The light catches it wrong and it goes dark; the light catches it right and it blazes white. Hence the name.

By eight in the morning, it's gone.

This is what September's first micro-season is about: the white dew that appears now for the first time, visible evidence of the night cooling enough to condense what the air holds. You could miss it every day of your life and not notice. Most people do. The 72 micro-seasons suggest this is a loss.

---

## 草露白 — Kusa no Tsuyu Shiroshi

The name parses beautifully, one word at a time.

*草* (kusa) is grass, or more broadly, plants and vegetation at the ground level — the stuff that grows low, the background world of a field or meadow. *Kusa* carries a connotation of ordinary, humble plants as opposed to trees or cultivated flowers. It's the landscape before you focus on anything in particular.

*露* (tsuyu) is dew — and here begins something important. *Tsuyu* in Japanese is not merely a meteorological phenomenon. It is one of the most loaded words in the language, particularly in poetry. From the 8th century *Manyōshū* onward, dew has served as the primary image for transience, for the brevity of human life, for the fragility of happiness and love. *Tsuyu no yo* (露の世) — the dewdrop world — is how classical poets referred to existence itself.

*白* (shiro) is white. But *shiro* here doesn't simply describe color. White in Japanese aesthetics carries associations with clarity, with the threshold between states, with things made pure by coolness. The white of this dew is not the white of blank paper — it's the white of something that becomes visible because of what surrounds it.

*The dew on the grass is white.* Five words in English; three characters in Japanese; a thousand years of accumulated meaning.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Dew forms when the temperature of a surface drops below the dew point — the temperature at which air can no longer hold all its water vapor, and the excess condenses.

In late summer and early autumn in Japan, the nights are now cool enough that dew forms on exposed surfaces, particularly grass and low vegetation. The dew that appears in September tends to be heavier and more visible than the barely-there moisture of mid-summer mornings, because the temperature differential between night and day is growing. The grass has cooled; the air above it, still carrying summer's moisture, releases some of what it holds.

This solar term — 白露 (*Hakuro*, "White Dew") — is the 15th of the 24 solar terms. It marks the point when, according to the traditional calendar, the dew becomes white and distinct. Earlier in summer, dew forms but is nearly invisible — too little, or too quickly evaporated. Now, with cooler nights and cleaner air, it becomes visible. The season announces itself by making the ordinary world sparkle.

---

## The Dewdrop World

No other natural phenomenon appears more frequently in classical Japanese poetry than dew.

The reason is partly visual — dew is beautiful, and traditional Japanese poets paid attention to beautiful things. But the reason is also philosophical: dew is *temporary* in the most obvious possible way. You can watch it happen. You can stand in a field at seven in the morning and watch the dew vanish as the sun rises. By ten o'clock, it is as if it was never there.

The Japanese expression *tsuyu no inochi* (露の命) — a "dewdrop life" — describes any existence that is brief and fragile. It was applied to human life in poetry going back to the Nara period. Kobayashi Issa, the great haiku poet known for writing from his own poverty and grief, made the image his own:

> つゆの世は　つゆの世ながら　さりながら
> *Tsuyu no yo wa / tsuyu no yo nagara / sari nagara*

*This world of dew / is only a world of dew — / and yet... and yet...*

Issa wrote this after the death of his infant daughter. The poem moves in three beats: the acceptance of impermanence, the qualification of that acceptance, the final surrender to grief that refuses to be philosophical about it. *And yet.* Even knowing that everything is temporary — even believing it — the loss is still real. The dew still disappears. It still hurts.

That "and yet" is one of the most honest things ever written in Japanese.

## A Poem for This Season

The Heian poet Fujiwara no Teika wrote, in the 12th century:

> 見渡せば　花も紅葉も　なかりけり
> 浦の苫屋の　秋の夕暮れ
>
> *Miwatase ba / hana mo momiji mo / nakari keri / ura no tomaya no / aki no yūgure*

*Looking out across the bay: / no cherry blossoms, no red leaves — / only a fisherman's thatched hut / in the autumn dusk.*

This poem is strange in the context of the 72 seasons because it refuses the obvious seasonal beauty. No famous flowers, no famous autumn colors — just an ordinary structure in ordinary light. Teika is doing something deliberate: insisting that the season can be found in the humble and plain, not only in the spectacular.

The white dew on the grass is in that tradition. It doesn't ask to be photographed. It asks only to be noticed before it's gone.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Set an alarm. Not an aggressive alarm — just early enough to get outside before eight.

Go to the nearest patch of grass. It doesn't need to be wild or picturesque. A park, a yard, the strip of vegetation beside a sidewalk. Crouch down close enough to see individual drops. Look at the light in them. Don't photograph immediately — just look first.

Notice how the drops hold on the tips of grass blades, how each one is its own sphere. Notice how the dew-covered grass looks silver or white from certain angles and transparent from others. Notice that by mid-morning you could walk across the same grass and find no trace of this.

The ancient calendar-makers gave this five days. I think they were being generous. The dew is there for about two hours each morning. It would deserve its own season if it lasted only one.
