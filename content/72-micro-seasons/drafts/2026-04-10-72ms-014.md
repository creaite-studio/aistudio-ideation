# Watching the Geese Disappear

*Micro-season #14 of 72 — April 10–14*

---

It is the season of departures disguised as arrivals. The swallows came last week, returning from the south. Now the geese leave, heading north to their Siberian breeding grounds. If you were watching only the swallows, spring would feel like a simple accumulation. But the geese remind you that it is also a distribution — a sorting of the world's birds into their summer territories, a continental reshuffling that involves both coming and going.

Watching geese fly north is different from watching them arrive in autumn. In autumn, their coming is a sign of cold approaching, of the days shortening, of something ending. But their spring departure carries a different emotional register. They are not leaving Japan in distress — they are leaving because the far north has finally opened, because Siberia in April is becoming a possible place, because the tundra is thawing and the breeding grounds are ready. They are leaving for something.

And they leave in formation, which has its own dignity: the long V-shapes against the April sky, the lead bird cycling back to let another take the wind, the shared effort of crossing vast distances. There is something instructive about the geese.

---

## 鴻雁北 — Kōgan kaeru

Three characters, precise and elegant. 鴻 (*kō*) refers specifically to large wild geese, particularly the Tundra bean goose and related species that winter in Japan — large, powerful birds that travel great distances. 雁 (*gan* / *kari*) is the general word for wild goose, a category of birds that has carried enormous cultural weight in East Asian poetry and painting for over two thousand years. Together, 鴻雁 (*kōgan*) means the wild geese, specifically the large ones that winter in Japan.

北 (*kita* / *hoku*) simply means north.

The full phrase: "Wild geese (go) north." The verb is implied — the Japanese grammar does not require it. The subject and the direction together are sufficient. North is where they are going. That is the whole sentence. That is the whole season.

This is the second micro-season within the Seimei solar term. The pure, clear April air that defined Seimei's opening is now filled with the sight of geese in formation, moving through it northward.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Several species of wild geese winter in Japan, with white-fronted geese (*Anser albifrons*) and bean geese (*Anser fabalis*) among the most numerous. They arrive in October and November from their Siberian breeding grounds and spend the winter in Japan's wetlands, estuaries, and agricultural fields, feeding on agricultural stubble, grass, and aquatic vegetation.

In April, as breeding instinct intensifies and day length passes a critical threshold, the geese begin to gather in preparation for departure. They become visibly restless at their wintering sites — moving more, vocalizing more, beginning to fly in the characteristic V-formations that reduce wind resistance for the birds behind the leader. The formation flight conserves energy over the vast distances involved: the breeding grounds in Siberia and arctic Russia are thousands of kilometers to the north.

The timing of departure correlates with conditions at the breeding grounds as much as with conditions in Japan. The geese seem to track, through some mechanism science does not yet fully understand, the opening of the northern summer. They leave Japan as the tundra is becoming livable again, arriving at their breeding grounds just as the snow begins to melt.

Nine species of geese have been recorded in Japan, including the rare emperor goose and the cackling goose. The most commonly seen in agricultural areas is the white-fronted goose, which makes up a significant proportion of wintering geese in western Japan.

## The Returning Goose in East Asian Poetry

The wild goose (*kari*, *gan*) occupies a special place in East Asian poetry — Chinese, Japanese, and Korean — that is difficult to overstate. For over two thousand years, the sight of geese flying in formation has been used as an image for:

- **Distance and longing**: The geese fly to places the poet cannot go, carrying the poet's thoughts toward a distant beloved or homeland.
- **The passage of time**: They come and go with the seasons; their return each year measures the passage of a life.
- **Communication**: In Chinese and Japanese poetic tradition, geese were believed to carry letters between separated lovers or friends. The goose as messenger (*karigane*) appears in countless poems.
- **Communal effort**: The formation flight, the sharing of the lead position, the collective endurance of long migration — all became metaphors for mutual support and shared purpose.

The *Man'yōshū* (c. 759) contains many poems about geese. Yamanoue no Okura wrote:

*今の我は / 雁になりたや / 高飛びて / 都に行きて / 妹に逢はまし*

"If only I could become a wild goose now / flying high / going to the capital / to meet my love."

The wish to become a goose — to have that freedom of flight, that directness of purpose — recurs throughout the tradition. The geese go where they need to go, without detour or delay.

## A Poem for This Season

From the *Kokinshū* (905), attributed to Ki no Tsurayuki:

*帰る雁 / 花の霞に / 隠るらむ / 春をば知らぬ / 山より来るか*

*Kaeru kari / hana no kasumi ni / kakururamu / haru o ba shiranu / yama yori kuru ka*

"The returning geese / hidden in the haze of blossoms — / do they come / from mountains that don't know spring / is already here?"

The poem plays with the geese's departure and the cherry blossom mist: the geese, leaving Japan where spring is in full bloom, will fly through the blossom haze and then on to places where spring has not yet arrived. They carry spring northward with them. The mountains of the far north that "don't know spring" will know it soon — the geese are bringing it.

---

## How to Meet This Season

In April, look up at the sky in the early morning and late afternoon — the times when migrating birds are most active. Listen for the high, musical calls of geese in flight; if you are near wetlands, estuaries, or large rice paddy areas in Japan, you may see the actual departures.

Even if you are not in a place to observe the geese themselves, step outside on a clear April morning and look northward. Somewhere in the sky above you, geese are moving. The direction means something. North is where the world is opening.
