# When the Wind Stopped Being Kind

*Micro-season #30 of 72 — July 2 to July 6*

---

There's a moment every summer when I notice that the wind has changed.

Through June, even on the warm days, the wind carried something in it — a residual coolness, a memory of the spring that preceded it. You'd step outside and feel the air move past your skin and think: manageable. Pleasant, even.

Then July comes.

The wind doesn't disappear in July. But it stops being a relief. When it blows now, it brings heat with it — a heat that is somehow moister and heavier than still air, that presses against you rather than refreshing you. This is the *atsukaze*: the warm wind, and the 72-season calendar marks its arrival with the 30th micro-season.

We are, beginning this week, in the full weight of summer.

---

## 温風至 — Atsukaze Itaru

Two ideas, three characters.

温風 (*atsukaze*) compounds two kanji: 温 (*atsu*) means warm, or lukewarm — not the aggressive heat of 大暑 (*taisho*, Great Heat) that comes later in July, but the specific quality of warmth that arrives with the wind from the south. 風 (*kaze*) means wind.

至 (*itaru*) means arrives, reaches, comes to. It is the same character used in other seasonal names: the arrival is the event.

*Warm winds arrive.* It is a name that acknowledges a shift that meteorologists would confirm: after the *tsuyu* rainy season ends (typically in early to mid-July, front moving northward), a high-pressure system from the Pacific dominates the weather pattern. Southerly winds carry heat and humidity from the ocean northward across the archipelago. The temperature climbs. The dew point climbs faster.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The *atsukaze* is not simply hot air. It is the beginning of Japan's specific variety of summer heat — the kind that earns the country a reputation as one of the most oppressively humid places on earth during July and August.

Japan's summer heat is driven by the combination of two factors: high temperatures (Tokyo regularly exceeds 35°C in July and August) and extremely high humidity — dew points above 25°C are common during peak heat waves. When both are present simultaneously, the apparent temperature — what it actually feels like to a human body — can exceed 45°C even on days when the thermometer reads only 35°C.

The southerly winds of early July, which the old calendar calls *atsukaze*, are classified by Japanese meteorologists as *shirahae* (white southerly wind) when they carry dry heat and *kurohae* (black southerly wind) when they carry rain-laden air beneath dark storm clouds. Both are warm. Both are the signal that *tsuyu* is ending and high summer is beginning.

The rice planted in May and June responds to this heat surge with visible vigor: the stands grow taller, greener, more dense. The heat that exhausts humans accelerates rice. By July, looking out over a paddy field, you can almost see the movement.

---

## The Sound of Summer: Fūrin

If there is a single object that captures the Japanese aesthetic relationship to heat, it is the *fūrin* — the glass wind chime.

*Fūrin* (風鈴) uses the same first character as *kaze* (wind): 風. The second character, 鈴, means bell. A wind bell. The *fūrin* is typically a small teardrop-shaped glass or metal bell with a strip of paper or metal hanging inside that catches the slightest air movement and produces a thin, high, crystalline sound.

The *fūrin* was not designed to cool the air — it cannot. But the sound of it is associated so deeply with coolness, with summer shade, with the movement of air, that hearing it produces a physiological response in people raised around it. In psychological studies of thermal perception in Japan, the sound of wind chimes has been shown to reduce the perceived ambient temperature by one to two degrees Celsius in test subjects. Hearing *fūrin* makes you feel cooler.

This is the Japanese aesthetic concept of *suzushisa*: induced coolness, coolness conjured by aesthetic means rather than mechanical ones. The *fūrin* is its most famous tool.

Since the Edo period, *fūrin* have been sold at summer festivals and hung in windows, on eaves, in the shade of verandas. Their sound travels through hot, still air and says: the wind was here a moment ago, and it will come again.

## A Poem for This Season

The warm wind as a poetic subject generates a particular kind of nostalgia in the Japanese tradition — the awareness that the wind has changed, that something has shifted, produces a backward glance at the cooler thing that preceded it.

Matsuo Bashō, traveling in summer heat:

> 野を横に　馬引きむけよ　ほととぎす
> *No wo yoko ni / uma hiki muke yo / hototogisu*

*Turn the horse*
*sideways across the field —*
*the cuckoo calls.*

This is not directly about wind but captures the summer traveler's experience: the heat, the exposure, the call of the cuckoo (*hototogisu*) that serves as a kigo for late spring and early summer. The instruction to turn the horse sideways — to expose it to whatever breeze the field might offer — is practical. Bashō was hot. The cuckoo was calling. These things happened simultaneously.

Yosa Buson, in a more direct treatment:

> ひさかたの　空より下る　暑さかな
> *Hisakata no / sora yori kudaru / atsusa kana*

*From the sky above —*
*the heat*
*descends.*

Buson was interested in materiality — in the way things exist in space — and this poem makes the heat concrete, gives it direction and weight, makes it something that *falls* from the sky rather than simply being ambient. The *kana* at the end leaves it open: this is just a thing that is happening, and it is enormous.

---

## How to Meet This Season

If you don't have a *fūrin*, buy one. They're sold at summer festivals throughout Japan, and at hardware and home goods stores. Glass ones are traditional and beautiful. They require nothing: hang them somewhere they'll catch a breeze, and then sit nearby and let the sound do its ancient work.

Open the windows in the evening, when the temperature begins to drop. Make cold things: mugicha (barley tea), kakigori (shaved ice with syrup), or simply ice water with a slice of lemon. Sit somewhere with shade.

The warm wind has arrived. July in Japan is not an experience to resist — resistance fails, and resistance is also exhausting. The practice is acceptance: the acknowledgment that heat is the season's nature, that discomfort is part of the texture of this particular time, and that the *fūrin's* voice is the calendar's way of saying *I know. I'm here too.*
