# When Blossoms Begin to Laugh

*Micro-season #8 of 72 — March 11–15*

---

The first thing to know about this micro-season's name is the verb: the peach blossoms do not simply bloom. They laugh.

This is not a translation choice or a metaphorical stretch. The original Japanese uses the character 笑 (*saku* / *warau*), which means both "to bloom" (in the context of flowers) and "to laugh" (in the context of people). The same character, the same word. The ancient writers of this calendar did not treat this ambiguity as a problem to be resolved. They treated it as a truth to be preserved.

There is something right about it. The peach blossom (*momo no hana*) is different from the austere, cold-weather plum (*ume*) that preceded it. The plum blooms in snow sometimes, alone, bare-limbed, stoic. The peach waits until the air has genuinely warmed. When it opens, it opens fully — warm pink against green foliage just beginning to appear — and the effect is not quiet or contemplative but something more exuberant. More like laughter.

---

## 桃始笑 — Momo hajimete saku

Three characters. 桃 (*momo*) is the peach — not just the fruit but the tree, with all its accumulated cultural weight. 始 (*hajimete*) means "for the first time," or "beginning to." 笑 (*saku* / *warau*) is the character that does the work of both blooming and laughing.

This doubling is intentional and ancient. In classical Japanese, *saku* was used for the opening of flowers (especially blossom-type flowers), while the same character in its other reading *warau* described human laughter and smiling. The idea seems to be that flowers opening their petals and human faces opening in laughter are, in some essential way, the same gesture. Both are things closed becoming open, things contained becoming expressive.

This is the second micro-season within the Keichitsu solar term. We have moved from the underground opening (insects emerging from their winter dwellings) to the branches opening (the first spring blossoms beginning to laugh in the warming air).

---

## What's Actually Happening

The peach tree (*Prunus persica*) has been cultivated in Japan since at least the Yayoi period (roughly 300 BCE – 300 CE), and probably arrived from China even earlier. It blooms in mid-March in the warmer parts of Japan, slightly later in cooler regions — typically about two to three weeks after the plum and a week or two before the cherry.

Unlike the cherry blossom, which blooms when the tree is still mostly bare, the peach often blooms alongside its first new leaves. The blossoms themselves are deeper pink than cherry, more saturated, almost warm to the eye. They are larger and more individual-looking than cherry blossoms, which tend to cluster in dense masses. A blooming peach tree has a slightly theatrical quality — each blossom seems deliberately placed.

The peach belongs to the same family as the plum and cherry (*Rosaceae*), and all three are important spring kigo in haiku. But while the plum (*ume*) is associated with early spring and the cherry (*sakura*) with the peak of spring, the peach occupies the transitional middle — after the austerity of winter but before the full exuberance of cherry season.

---

## Peach, Paradise, and the Hina Festival

The peach carries enormous symbolic weight in Japanese culture, inherited partly from China and partly from indigenous tradition. In Chinese mythology, the Queen Mother of the West (Xi Wangmu) kept a garden of immortal peach trees whose fruit conferred eternal life. This imagery entered Japan through Buddhism and through cultural exchange, and the peach became associated with longevity, protection from evil, and feminine beauty.

The *Hinamatsuri* (Doll Festival or Girls' Day), celebrated on March 3 — just before this micro-season — is deeply connected to the peach blossom. The festival's full traditional name is *momo no sekku*, "the peach festival," and it celebrates young girls' health and happiness. Peach branches are displayed alongside the traditional tiered display of dolls. The timing is not accidental: March 3 falls when the peach is just beginning to bloom, and the blossom's association with feminine beauty, health, and warding off misfortune is woven into the festival's meaning.

In the *Momotarō* folk tale — one of the most famous in Japan — a hero is born from inside a giant peach floating down a river. The peach as the source of extraordinary life, as a vessel for something miraculous, is a recurring motif.

## A Poem for This Season

Matsuo Bashō, who for a time in his early career used the name "Tōsei" (unripe peach), wrote sparingly about the peach blossom but deeply about the transition from plum to cherry — the season the peach inhabits:

*梅が香に / のっと日の出る / 山路かな*

*Ume ga ka ni / notto hi no deru / yamaji kana*

"In the scent of plum blossoms — / suddenly the sun rises / on the mountain path."

The abruptness of *notto* — the sun appearing suddenly, without warning — captures the quality of the transition this micro-season embodies. Something has shifted. The light has come. The peach blossoms are laughing.

For a poem more directly about the peach, Yosa Buson:

*桃の花 / 一枝ほしや / 朝の雨*

*Momo no hana / hitoeda hoshi ya / asa no ame*

"Peach blossoms — / I want just one branch / in the morning rain."

The modesty of "just one branch" (*hitoeda*) against the fullness of a blooming tree is classic Buson: restraint that makes the desire more vivid. The morning rain would make the blossoms heavier, the pink deeper. One branch would be enough.

## How to Meet This Season

If you are in Japan or in a temperate region where peach trees grow, find one now. They are in parks, in gardens, in old farmyards, along the roads near agricultural villages. Stand close enough to smell the blossoms — the fragrance is sweet and light, less complex than the plum, warmer and less sharp.

If peach trees are not part of your landscape, notice what is doing the laughing near you: whatever flowers in your region with that same exuberant quality — fully open, warm-toned, placed as if they know they are being watched.
