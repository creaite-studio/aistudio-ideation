# The Bear Knows Something We've Forgotten

*Micro-season #61 of 72 — December 7 to December 11*

---

The bear has been eating for months.

All autumn, the *tsukino-waguma* — the Asian black bear of Japan's forests — has been in a state of extraordinary consumption. Beechnuts, acorns, wild cherries, insects, roots, fish when it can find them. It has been filling itself methodically, relentlessly, accumulating the fat reserves that will keep it alive through three or four months of complete fasting. By early December, a large bear may have put on 30 kilograms since autumn. It is, biologically speaking, a machine for converting autumn's abundance into stored energy.

And then, this week, it goes into the mountain. It finds the hollow log or the rocky overhang or the excavated den under the roots of an old tree. It turns around once, twice. It lies down.

It will not eat again until spring.

---

## 熊蟄穴 — Kuma Ana ni Komoru

The middle character of this name deserves particular attention, because it carries a weight that the translation "hide" doesn't quite capture.

*Kuma* (熊) is bear — a hulking character that looks, if you squint at it, like a large animal crouching. Its etymology involves the character for fire (火) modified by various additional strokes, though scholars debate the original meaning. What matters is that it is unmistakably the bear: heavy, warm-blooded, formidable.

*Chitsu* (蟄) is the fascinating one. It means to lie dormant, to hibernate, to go underground. The character contains the insect radical (虫) combined with characters suggesting something held or stopped. It is the same character that appears in *Keichitsu* (啓蟄) — micro-season #7, which falls in early March, when insects and creatures emerge from the ground after winter. *Kei* (啓) means to open or awaken; *chitsu* means the dormancy being broken.

These two seasons — #7 and #61 — are mirror images of each other across the calendar's spine. In March, the earth opens and the dormant things emerge. In December, the living world goes back under. The bear is the visible ambassador of this movement: large, unmistakable, going down into the earth just as the calendar says it will.

*Ana* (穴) is hole, hollow, cave, den. It appears in the word for *anaguma* (穴熊), the badger — literally "hole-bear" — and in *anaba* (穴場), a hidden gem, a secret spot. A hole is not emptiness; it is a specific, shaped space.

*Komoru* (籠もる): to shut oneself in, to retire from the world, to become secluded. The word is also used for people who withdraw for religious retreat or study or illness. The bear is doing something sacred, or at least something that the culture recognized as analogous to sacred withdrawal.

## What's Actually Happening

The bear's hibernation is one of the most studied and still partially mysterious phenomena in mammalian biology.

Unlike smaller hibernators — ground squirrels, hedgehogs — bears do not reach the near-death temperatures and heart rates of true torpor. A hibernating ground squirrel can have a body temperature close to freezing and a heart rate of three or four beats per minute. A hibernating bear stays warmer — body temperature drops only 3 to 8 degrees Celsius — and its heart rate slows to around eight beats per minute but remains rhythmically active.

What this means is that the bear is not truly "asleep" in any simple sense. It is in a state of metabolic suppression that allows it to survive without eating, drinking, urinating, or defecating for months — an achievement that no human can come close to replicating. Sleeping humans must get up, must eat, must process waste. The bear seals all of these loops.

In the mountains of central Honshu, where the Japanese black bear (*Ursus thibetanus japonicus*) lives, dens tend to be found at elevations above 1,000 meters in old-growth forest, in natural cavities — hollow trees, rock overhangs, spaces beneath root systems. The bear typically dens in early to mid-December, precisely the period this micro-season marks. Female bears with cubs den earliest and latest; they will give birth to one or two tiny cubs — weighing only 300 grams each — in the den during January, nursing them while still in a semi-dormant state.

## The Mountain God Goes to Sleep

In mountain communities across Japan, bears have long occupied a complex cultural position: feared, respected, hunted, but also understood as powerful spiritual presences.

The *matagi* (マタギ) — Japan's traditional mountain hunters of the Tōhoku region — maintained an elaborate ritual relationship with bears. To kill a bear was an act requiring spiritual preparation and subsequent ceremony. The bear's death was not treated as simple hunting but as an exchange between human and divine, requiring that the bear's spirit be properly honored and sent on its way. The bear was understood as a messenger or avatar of the mountain kami.

In this context, the bear's hibernation in early December was not mere animal behavior but a kind of divine withdrawal. The mountain itself was going to sleep. The paths that were passable in autumn became treacherous with snow. The *matagi* restricted their own movements. Even the mountain's guardian had retired for the season.

There is a word in Japanese — *yukuefumei* (行方不明) — for someone who has gone somewhere unknown, whose whereabouts cannot be determined. For three months of winter, every bear in the Japanese mountains is *yukuefumei*. Somewhere in the dark of the mountain, doing something that no one can see. This invisibility was understood as part of the bear's power.

## A Poem for This Season

The haiku poet Taniguchi Buson captured the stillness of the season with an image that gestures toward the hidden bear:

> 山奥や　雪の底なる　熊の穴
> *yama oku ya / yuki no soko naru / kuma no ana*

*Deep in the mountains —*
*at the bottom of the snow,*
*the bear's den.*

The poem creates a kind of vertical depth: the mountains, then the snow piled on them, and at the bottom of all that white silence, the dark hollow where the bear sleeps. The viewer — the poet, the reader — is on the surface. The bear is far below, unreachable, beyond the reach of ordinary attention.

The poem is about unknowability. We can name the den. We cannot reach it. The bear's winter is entirely its own.

## How to Meet This Season

The bear goes in, and we might ask: what does it mean to *winter* properly?

This season has become a quietly popular concept in contemporary slow-living culture — the idea that winter is not just a less-pleasant version of summer, not a deficiency to be compensated for, but a necessary phase of inwardness and rest. The bear is its patron saint.

The practice for this week is unglamorous and simple: go to bed earlier. Not dramatically — maybe thirty minutes, maybe an hour. Go to sleep when it's dark, and see if you wake before your alarm in the early morning quiet. Notice what it feels like to align your sleep, even slightly, with the rhythm the season is suggesting.

Then, in the extra morning quiet before the day starts: do nothing productive. Lie in the darkness. Let your mind do what it does in the hour before full waking — the associative wandering, the slow resolution of unformed thoughts. The bear is not asleep; it is in a state of maintained biological awareness, processing slowly. You can do a version of this.

The bear knows something we have forgotten: that rest is not failure. It is preparation. The fat reserves of autumn become the energy of spring. What you store in the dark months is what you will run on when the light returns.
