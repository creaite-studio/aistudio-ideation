# The Plant That Lives Backward

*Micro-season #63 of 72 — December 17 to December 21*

---

There is a plant in Japan that has its seasons reversed.

When summer is at its hottest and most lush — when every other plant is in full leaf, reaching toward the maximum light — this plant shrivels and dies. Its leaves brown, its stems collapse, its above-ground presence disappears entirely. For the months of July and August, it doesn't exist.

And then, as the rest of the world turns toward winter and dormancy, this plant wakes up. In November, small shoots push through the cooling soil. By December — by the week of the winter solstice — it has fresh new leaves, green and growing while frost forms on the ground around it.

The Japanese name for this plant is *natsukarekusa* (夏枯草): the summer-withering grass. But the name in this micro-season is older and stranger: *nattō* (乃東).

---

## 乃東生 — Nattō Shōzu

The name breaks into two parts, and the first requires some explanation.

*Nattō* (乃東) is an archaic Chinese botanical name for what we now call *Prunella vulgaris* — selfheal, heal-all, carpenter's herb. The characters 乃 (*nai*, a classical particle meaning "then" or "thereupon") and 東 (*tō*, east) combine in a name that doesn't quite translate to anything botanical in a modern sense. It is a name preserved from classical Chinese medical texts, kept in the seasonal calendar even as everyday Japanese speakers switched to *natsukarekusa* or the more recent *utsubo-gusa* (ウツボ草).

*Shōzu* (生) means to be born, to spring up, to come into existence. It's the most optimistic character in the seasonal vocabulary: pure, green, upward life.

The plant-that-has-no-modern-Japanese-name sprouts.

---

## What's Actually Happening

*Prunella vulgaris* (*utsubo-gusa* in modern Japanese) is a perennial herb of the mint family, found across temperate zones in Europe, Asia, and North America. In Japan, it grows in grasslands, roadsides, and forest margins across most of the archipelago.

Its strange seasonal rhythm is what sets it apart. Unlike most temperate perennials, which grow actively in spring and summer and die back to roots in winter, *Prunella* follows what botanists call a "summer-deciduous" pattern in warmer parts of its range. It germinates in autumn, grows actively through the mild months of winter, produces its purple flower spikes in late spring, sets seed, and then — as summer heat intensifies — wilts and goes dormant above ground. The roots persist underground through the hot months, and then the cycle begins again.

This makes the plant almost uniquely legible as a marker of the solstice period. Its sprouting in December aligns with remarkable precision with the winter solstice (冬至, *Tōji*) itself, which falls on December 21st or 22nd. The plant seems to respond to the turn of the light — the moment when days stop shortening and begin, imperceptibly, to lengthen again.

Traditional Chinese medicine, which influenced Japanese herbal practice deeply, classified *Prunella* as a cooling herb (*kan* 寒) with particular properties related to clearing heat from the liver and eyes. The fact that it grows in winter — the cool season — and dies in summer — the hot season — was understood as reflecting its medicinal nature. The plant embodies the quality it treats. It lives in coolness and dies in heat; it is therefore useful for reducing heat in the body.

---

## The Healer at the Solstice

The winter solstice — *Tōji* — falls at the end of this micro-season, and the two are closely linked in traditional culture.

*Tōji* is one of the most important turning points in the Japanese calendar: the longest night, the shortest day, the moment when the *in* (陰, yin, dark, cold, retreating) energy reaches its absolute maximum and the *yō* (陽, yang, light, warm, returning) energy begins its long return. It is a day of significant ritual significance — perhaps the most important day in the solar year in traditional cosmology.

The practice of eating pumpkin (*kabocha*) on the winter solstice is still maintained in many Japanese households: the orange flesh of the squash is understood to supplement the body's warmth and vitality through the darkest period. Bathing in *yuzu* (柚子) citrus baths — the sharp fragrance and vitamin-rich oils of the fruit protecting the skin through cold months — is another *Tōji* custom still practiced.

And in this cosmological context, the sprouting of *nattō* at the solstice carries meaning. Here, at the darkest moment, when the year reaches its farthest point from light, the ground produces something green. The healing herb appears precisely when the direction of the year changes. The plant that lives backward — dying in summer's heat, growing in winter's cold — marks the turning of the wheel.

---

## A Poem for This Season

The poet Yosa Buson, writing in the 18th century, captured the quiet of the solstice season:

> 冬至梅　雪の内より　花あれば
> *tōji ume / yuki no uchi yori / hana areba*

*Winter solstice plum —*
*even from beneath the snow,*
*a flower.*

Buson's poem is about plum (*ume*) rather than selfheal, but the image is the same: something growing in impossible cold, emerging from snow rather than retreating from it. The poem refuses to read this as miraculous — it uses *areba*, "if there is," almost matter-of-factly. Of course there is a flower. Even in the darkest week, there is something alive and reaching.

The poem holds the contradiction without trying to resolve it. Cold and flower, snow and color, the darkest season and the beginning of return. The solstice is not rescue from winter. It's something more interesting than that: it's the moment when winter becomes capable of spring.

---

## How to Meet This Season

Selfheal (*Prunella vulgaris*) is one of the most common "weeds" in temperate lawns and gardens worldwide — you've almost certainly walked past it without noticing. Now is the time to look.

If you have a garden or access to a lawn, find a patch of *Prunella* before it's been mowed. The leaves are oval, opposite, slightly hairy, with a faintly minty smell if you crush them. In mid-December, new growth — small, fresh, obviously alive — will be visible at the base of the stems or pushing up through the soil.

Pull a small clump, smell it. The scent is faintly medicinal, clean, cool. This is the plant the calendar chose to mark the winter solstice: not a spectacular flower, not a dramatic phenomenon, but a small common herb growing quietly in the frost.

On the solstice itself — December 21st or 22nd — do something to mark the turning. Eat pumpkin, take a yuzu bath, light candles at dusk and leave them burning past the early dark. Notice how early it gets dark. And then notice: from tomorrow, it will be infinitesimally lighter. The *nattō* has known this was coming for weeks. It started growing in anticipation.

The longest night is not the ending. It is the hinge.
