# The Bridge Between Rain and Sun

*Micro-season #15 of 72 — April 15–19*

---

A rainbow requires two things simultaneously: rain and sun. It can only exist at the intersection of a shower and clear sky, at the moment when the departing rain is illuminated from behind. In winter, this combination rarely occurs — the storms are too uniform, the sky too consistently overcast. But in spring, when weather systems move through quickly and patches of blue appear between shower bands, the conditions for rainbows multiply.

The 15th micro-season of the year is named for the first rainbows. Not for their beauty — though they are beautiful — but for what their appearance means: the atmosphere has become warm and complex enough, the sun strong enough against the rain, to produce this optical phenomenon. A rainbow is a measurement of the season's progress.

Standing in a Japanese field or on a hillside road in mid-April after a passing shower, watching a rainbow form over the rice paddy valley below, you understand why the ancient observers thought this worth naming. It is not just pretty. It is specific. The rainbow means something.

---

## 虹始見 — Niji hajimete arawaru

Three characters. 虹 (*niji*) is the rainbow — a character that combines the "insect/creature" radical (虫) with a phonetic element, reflecting an ancient belief that the rainbow was a dragon, a living creature arching across the sky. The radical pairing is telling: in classical Chinese and Japanese thought, the rainbow was not merely a meteorological phenomenon. It was a being.

始 (*hajimete*) means "for the first time," beginning. 見 (*miru* / *gen*) means to see, to appear, to be visible.

The full phrase: "The rainbow is first seen." Or: "For the first time, the rainbow appears."

This is the third and final micro-season of the Seimei solar term. We have moved from the pure, clear air of early April to the swallows arriving and the geese departing, and now to the first rainbows — the light playing through the spring showers that are now frequent enough to produce them.

---

## What's Actually Happening

A rainbow forms when sunlight enters a water droplet, refracts (bends) at the surface, reflects off the back interior of the droplet, and refracts again as it exits. Different wavelengths of light bend at slightly different angles, which separates the spectrum into the distinct colors we see: red on the outside of the arc, through orange, yellow, green, blue, to violet on the inside.

For a rainbow to be visible, the sun must be behind the observer and the rain must be ahead. The sun must also be low enough in the sky — below approximately 42 degrees above the horizon — which is why rainbows are most commonly seen in the morning (when the sun is in the east and the shower is to the west) or in the afternoon and evening (when the sun is in the west and the shower is to the east).

In mid-April in Japan, the angle of the sun and the frequency of passing shower systems conspire to produce rainbow conditions regularly. The calendar's creators noticed that mid-April — and specifically this five-day window — is when rainbows begin to appear reliably after their winter absence. They were right.

## The Rainbow Dragon

The traditional Japanese view of the rainbow is inseparable from the dragon (*ryū* or *tatsu*). The rainbow was understood as a dragon drinking from the water below — the arch of its body visible in the colored light, the dragon's head and tail hidden in cloud and rain. The rainbow season was therefore also, in a sense, the dragon season.

This matters for agriculture. Dragons in Japanese folk belief (inherited partly from Chinese tradition) presided over water: rivers, lakes, rain, and the flooded rice paddies. A dragon appearing in the sky — manifesting as a rainbow after a spring rain — was understood as a good sign for the paddies. The water deity was visible, was present, was drinking from the land's water and would return it as rain.

In other folk traditions, the rainbow was connected more ambiguously to weather forecasting. A morning rainbow (sun in the east, rain to the west) often means rain is approaching; an evening rainbow (sun in the west, rain to the east) often means the storm has passed. The experienced farmer read the rainbow's position as information.

In Buddhist iconography, the rainbow appears in images of paradise: the Pure Land is often depicted with rainbow light around it, the arc of light representing the bridge between the ordinary world and the realm of enlightenment.

## A Poem for This Season

The rainbow (*niji*) as a spring kigo in haiku is relatively rare — it is a difficult image to work with, easily falling into the picturesque. But Yosa Buson, who was also a painter, found a way:

*夕虹の / 片はた消えて / 春の暮れ*

*Yūniji no / katakata kiete / haru no kure*

"The evening rainbow — / one half already gone / in the spring dusk."

The rainbow is disappearing even as it is seen. Buson, characteristically, catches the transition rather than the thing itself: not the rainbow at its peak, but the rainbow fading, one side already absorbed back into the evening sky. The spring dusk is doing what spring does — everything here is briefly, beautifully present, and then not.

## How to Meet This Season

Face east in the late afternoon after a spring rain shower has passed. If the sun is out behind you, look for the arc beginning to form low in the eastern sky. The full arc — if the conditions are right — can span 84 degrees of sky, which is enormous. You need to look at a broad sweep of sky, not a narrow window.

If you see a secondary rainbow above the primary one, look carefully: the colors are reversed (red on the inside, violet on the outside) because the light has bounced twice inside the raindrops. This is less common and worth noticing.

The rainbow exists only in a specific relationship between you, the rain, and the sun. Move to either side and the arc shifts. It is always centered on the point directly opposite the sun from your eye — it is, in a literal sense, your personal phenomenon. No one standing beside you is seeing the same rainbow. Each observer has their own.
