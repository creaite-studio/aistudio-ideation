# The Sound of Forty Thousand Mouths

*Micro-season #22 of 72 — May 20 to May 24*

---

A farmer who raised silkworms once told me that the sound is the thing that stays with you.

He was old — in his eighties, raised in Gunma Prefecture when sericulture was still the economic backbone of entire regions — and he described it as a sound like light rain falling on dry leaves. Except it was inside, in a warm room, and the sound was eating. Thousands of silkworms working through fresh mulberry leaves in unison, a soft, continuous rustling that filled the whole house.

"You'd hear it at night," he said. "Even from the bedroom, through the wall. They never stop."

---

## 蚕起食桑 — Kaiko Okite Kuwa wo Hamu

Four characters, three distinct ideas.

蚕 (*kaiko*) means silkworm — the larva of *Bombyx mori*, the domesticated silk moth. The character itself is composed of the radical for insect (虫) beneath the radical for heaven or sky (天), a compound that has been interpreted variously: some see it as an insect that produces something as valuable as the heavens. Others note simply that silk was, for most of human history, among the most precious substances in the world.

起 (*okite*) means "rises" or "wakes" — from 起きる (*okiru*), to get up, to stir.

食桑 (*kuwa wo hamu*) means "eats mulberry." 桑 (*kuwa*) is the mulberry tree; 食む (*hamu*) is an older verb meaning to eat or to chew.

*Silkworms rise and eat mulberry.* The name of this season contains an entire agricultural sequence: the hatching, the waking, the single-minded act of consumption that transforms leaf into silk.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The life cycle of the silkworm is one of the stranger arrangements in the animal kingdom.

Adult silk moths (*Bombyx mori*) cannot eat. They exist solely to mate and lay eggs. The eggs are the size of a pinhead and can be stored through winter in cool, dry conditions — traditionally on paper, tucked in a box. In late spring, when mulberry trees have leafed out and temperatures have risen, the eggs hatch. What emerges is tiny, black, hairy, voracious.

A silkworm eats almost continuously for approximately 30 days. It will increase its body weight by 10,000 times during this period. It eats nothing but mulberry leaves — *Bombyx mori* has been domesticated so thoroughly over thousands of years that it has lost the ability to survive on anything else. The mulberry timing, the silkworm timing, and the human farming calendar are intertwined at a deep level, each calibrated to the others.

When silkworms are feeding at peak volume, the sound is genuinely audible from outside a well-stocked rearing room. That light-rain-on-dry-leaves sound the farmer described is real, measurable, and to generations of Japanese farm families, as familiar as any ambient sound of home.

---

## A Thread That Changed the World

Japan's silk history spans nearly two thousand years.

Sericulture — the cultivation of silkworms for silk production — is believed to have reached Japan from China and Korea sometime in the 3rd or 4th century CE. It took root first in courtly contexts, silk being a material of ceremony and rank, before spreading slowly to rural farm households over the centuries.

By the Edo period, silkworm cultivation had become integrated into the agricultural calendar of many regions, particularly in the Kanto plain and the mountains of central Honshu. Farmers grew mulberry trees on hillsides too steep for rice, and the twice-yearly silkworm cycles — spring and sometimes autumn — provided cash income that balanced the subsistence economy of rice.

The scale it eventually reached is difficult to comprehend. At its peak in 1930, nearly 40% of all farming households in Japan were engaged in sericulture in some form. Japanese silk exports accounted for roughly 60% of global silk production. The fabric that became Kyoto's celebrated textiles, that was woven into kimonos worn at every formal occasion from weddings to funerals, that traveled the Silk Road westward — much of it began in rooms like the one that farmer remembered, filled with that soft and relentless sound.

## A Poem for This Season

The silkworm does not appear often in haiku — it is too agricultural, too domestic, not wild enough for most poets of the classical tradition. But Matsuo Bashō, who traveled the provinces and wrote with equal attention to peasant life as to high culture, touched it in his prose journals. And later poets of the Shōwa era returned to it as a site of memory and mourning, as sericulture declined after World War II.

One of the most evocative comes from the folk-song tradition rather than haiku proper:

> 糸を引く蚕の　声聞けば　春の夜長も　なかりけり
> *Ito wo hiku kaiko no / koe kikeba / haru no yonaga mo / nakarikeri*

*Hearing the silkworms*
*drawing their thread —*
*the spring night, however long,*
*does not feel long.*

The image is of someone sitting up with the silkworms, listening to them work. It captures something honest about the intimacy of this labor — you weren't just feeding them; you were keeping them company, attending to them, learning their sounds.

---

## How to Meet This Season

The practical reality is that very few people in Japan still raise silkworms at home. Sericulture as a livelihood has largely disappeared. But some schools and agricultural museums maintain small silkworm programs — often with children feeding the larvae as an educational project — and you can sometimes find mulberry trees growing in old neighborhoods, their broad leaves untouched now that the industry has moved on.

If you happen upon a mulberry tree this week, pick a leaf. Hold it. Feel how smooth it is, how thick. This is what forty thousand silkworms would need, continuously, for a month, to produce a few kilograms of raw silk — which would then be reeled, washed, woven, and possibly dyed before becoming a strip of fabric a few centimeters wide.

Every length of silk is, at its origin, an act of near-incomprehensible labor by a creature the size of your finger that has forgotten how to eat anything else.
