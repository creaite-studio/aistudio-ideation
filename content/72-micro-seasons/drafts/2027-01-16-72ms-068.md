# The Bird That Announces Spring Before It Arrives

*Micro-season #68 of 72 — January 16 to January 20*

---

In the coldest week of the Japanese year, a male pheasant starts calling.

This seems, on the face of it, improbable. The pheasant's call is a territorial and mating signal — the kind of sound that makes sense in the mild energy of spring, when breeding season is approaching and males compete for females and territory. It's not an obvious sound for January, when the frost is hard and the days are still short and everything sensible seems to be hunkered down and waiting.

But the pheasant is operating on a different schedule than our intuitions suggest. It has been listening to the lengthening days since the solstice — four weeks ago now, barely noticeable to humans but detectable to a bird calibrated for exactly this change — and has determined that it is time to begin.

The pheasant announces spring before spring exists. It calls it into being.

---

## 雉始雊 — Kiji Hajimete Naku

The structure of this name follows a pattern we've seen before in the 72 micro-seasons.

*Kiji* (雉) is the green pheasant (*Phasianus versicolor*), Japan's national bird. The character contains the bird radical (隹, a simplified bird form) and additional elements that, in their historical development, evoked the colorful markings of the pheasant. The character is specific to this one bird in a way that many bird characters are not — *kiji* is not a general bird classifier but a specific kanji home built for the green pheasant.

*Hajimete* (始) — "for the first time" — again signals threshold, crossing. We have seen this character mark the first freezing of the earth (#55), the first time the tachibana turns yellow, the first time frogs sing in spring. Now it marks the first call of the pheasant in the new year.

*Naku* (雊) — this is the interesting one. The usual character for calling or singing in animals is 鳴 (*naku*, containing the mouth radical). But here, the season uses a rarer character: 雊, which is specifically the clucking or calling sound of a pheasant or chicken. This is the sound the bird itself makes, distinguished from the generic animal call. The calendar was choosing its words precisely.

The green pheasant calls for the first time. A very specific bird makes a very specific sound.

## What's Actually Happening

The green pheasant (*Phasianus versicolor*) is endemic to Japan — found nowhere else in the world in its natural state, though it has been introduced to various countries. It is distributed throughout Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu in open woodland, farmland edges, riverside vegetation, and forest margins. Hokkaido has a different subspecies (*P. v. robustipes*), while the Ryukyu islands have their own distinct populations.

The male is one of Japan's most visually dramatic birds: an iridescent green body, red wattles around the eyes, an extraordinarily long barred tail that can exceed sixty centimeters. In winter plumage it's somewhat duller than its summer peak, but still unmistakable.

The calling behavior this season describes — *naku* — is specifically the male's advertisement call: a loud, harsh double-bark (*ko-keh*!) followed by vigorous wing-drumming, a sound called *hogeki* (砲撃, like artillery). The call carries across open country for hundreds of meters. Males use it both to advertise their presence to females and to warn other males away from their territory.

In mid-January, the days have been lengthening for nearly a month. For pheasants — like many birds — the primary cue for reproductive behavior is not temperature but photoperiod: the length of the light portion of the day. The pheasant's hypothalamus is measuring light, and by mid-January, the lengthening days have crossed the threshold that triggers the hormonal cascade leading to reproductive behavior. The male begins to call.

This is a striking example of a bird "knowing" spring is coming weeks before any thermal evidence appears. The pheasant's call in January is not confusion — it is accurate long-range forecasting based on the most reliable seasonal signal available.

## The Divine Messenger

The kiji occupies a singular place in Japanese mythology and folklore.

In the *Nihon Shoki* and *Kojiki*, Japan's founding chronicles, the pheasant appears as a messenger of the gods — specifically as the messenger of Amaterasu, the sun goddess, sent to investigate the suspicious noises coming from the cave where Amaterasu had hidden herself and plunged the world into darkness. The pheasant's call was the signal that preceded the world's re-illumination.

In the folk tale of *Momotarō* (Peach Boy), Japan's most beloved folklore narrative, the pheasant is one of the three animal companions the hero recruits — along with a dog and a monkey — to defeat the demons of Onigashima. Each animal represents a quality: the dog, loyalty; the monkey, cleverness; the pheasant, aerial scouting and the ability to see what others miss from above.

The pheasant's sensitivity to earthquakes is documented in Japanese traditional knowledge: people in seismically active areas noted that pheasants called irregularly and moved strangely before large earthquakes, their hearing (or some other sense) apparently detecting the infrasound or ground vibrations that preceded a major tremor. This gave the bird a reputation as an early-warning system, a creature that sensed danger before humans could.

In this context, the pheasant's call in January is not just a mating signal. It is the bird's nature to announce what is coming. The pheasant calls, and then the thing arrives.

## A Poem for This Season

The *Man'yōshū* contains a famous poem that uses the pheasant's call to express the poignancy of love and separation:

> あしひきの　山鳥の尾の　しだり尾の　ながながし夜を　ひとりかも寝む
> *ashihikino / yamadori no o no / shidari o no / naganagashi yo o / hitori kamo nemu*

*Like the drooping tail*
*of the copper pheasant*
*in the hill-shadow —*
*through this long, long night*
*am I to sleep alone?*

The poem uses the image of the pheasant's extraordinarily long tail — in the copper pheasant (*Syrmaticus soemmerringii*, *yamadori*), a close relative of the green pheasant — as a metaphor for a long night of lonely waiting. The tail droops; the night lengthens. Both are images of something beautiful and mournful.

The poem has been attributed to Kakinomoto no Hitomaro, one of the great voices of the *Man'yōshū*, and it captures something essential about how the pheasant figured in classical Japanese imagination: a bird of long, solitary things. Long tails. Long calls. Long nights.

But the calling pheasant of January is not solitary in intention — it's calling precisely to end solitude. It calls because spring is coming. It calls to make spring happen.

## How to Meet This Season

Go outside this week with the specific intention of hearing birds.

Not just noticing birds — listening for them. In January, birdsong is sparse compared to the dawn chorus of May, but it is meaningful precisely because of its sparseness. Each call stands out in the winter quiet. Learn what the sounds are around you: the house sparrows in the eaves, the crows traversing the sky, the *shijūkara* (great tit) beginning their territorial songs in hedgerows.

If you're in rural Japan near suitable pheasant habitat — open farmland, riverside scrub, forest edges — keep your ears open for the *ko-keh!* call and the deep wing-drumming that follows it. The male calls from an elevated position — a fence post, a mound, a fallen tree — and the sound carries further than you'd expect.

If you're outside Japan: find the equivalent bird in your own landscape — whatever is beginning to move and make sound in the cold weeks of January. In North America it might be chickadees establishing territory. In Europe, great tits and blue tits. The first bold voices of winter, announcing what they know is coming.

The pheasant calls because it has read the light and made its determination. The information is there, in the shortening nights. Something is on its way.
