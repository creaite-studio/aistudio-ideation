# The Hunt for Red Leaves

*Micro-season #53 of 72 — October 28 to November 1*

---

There is a Japanese word — *momijigari* — that translates as "hunting autumn leaves."

The word is strange if you think about it too carefully. You don't hunt leaves. They don't flee. You can't catch one and bring it home (well, you can bring one home, but that's not quite the point). The "hunting" is the act of going in search of the most spectacular display, of traveling to famous *koyo* sites, of walking through mountain forests or temple gardens precisely when the maples have reached peak color. The leaves are the quarry, and the hunting is a form of rapturous attention.

This week, the maples are beginning to turn. The 72 micro-seasons say: *楓蔦黄* — maples and ivy, going yellow. The hunt can begin.

---

## 楓蔦黄 — Momiji Tsuta Kibamu

Three characters, two trees, one color.

*楓* (kaede, or momiji in one reading) is the Japanese maple — specifically, the family of *Acer* species that Japan has cultivated and celebrated for centuries. The character contains the wood radical on the left and elements suggesting wind on the right. Maples and wind: the leaves that tremble and turn, that catch the light and release it in multiple reds and oranges.

*蔦* (tsuta) is ivy — specifically Japanese ivy (*Parthenocissus tricuspidata*), the climbing plant that covers temple walls, old stone fences, abandoned buildings. Unlike maple, which grows upward on its own, ivy is a colonizer: it spreads across surfaces, following whatever it touches. In autumn, ivy turns before the maples do, its color often appearing at the edges of forest clearings and along stone walls while the trees are still green.

*黄* (kibamu, or kii) — yellow. The character itself is yellow; the compound form *kibamu* means "to become yellow," "to turn golden." Note that the season says *yellow*, not red — though the maples are famous for their reds and oranges. The yellow here points to the beginning of the turning process, when chlorophyll first begins to break down and the underlying yellows become visible. Red comes later, as anthocyanins develop. The season catches the trees at the threshold.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The color change of deciduous trees in autumn is triggered by a combination of factors: shorter days (less light for photosynthesis), cooler temperatures, and in some species, drought stress.

As daylight decreases in October, the tree begins to seal off the connection between leaf and branch — a process involving the growth of cells at the leaf base called the abscission zone. As this zone develops, the flow of nutrients in and out of the leaf slows. Chlorophyll, which requires constant synthesis to maintain (it degrades rapidly in light), stops being replaced and begins to break down. As green fades, the yellow carotenoids — which were always present in the leaf but masked by green chlorophyll — become visible.

The reds and oranges of maple are different: these come from anthocyanins, which are actually produced during the color-change process in response to bright light and cool temperatures. Sunny days and cold nights produce the most intense reds. Cloudy autumns produce more muted yellows and browns.

In Japan, the *koyo* forecast — the autumn leaf color front — is tracked with the same attention as the *sakura* (cherry blossom) forecast in spring. The Japan Meteorological Corporation publishes weekly updates; television news shows daily progress maps; websites allow users to report peak conditions from their local areas. The *koyo front* moves southward from Hokkaido in September, reaching the high mountains of central Honshu in October, the lowlands and Kyoto in November.

## Momijigari: 1,300 Years of Leaf-Hunting

The tradition of *momijigari* — traveling to observe and celebrate autumn foliage — is one of the oldest recreational customs in Japanese history.

The earliest records come from the Nara period (710-794), with poems about autumn leaves in the *Manyōshū*. But the tradition crystallized during the Heian period (794-1185), when the aristocratic court organized elaborate leaf-viewing excursions to mountain valleys near Kyoto. These were not casual walks. They involved processions, picnics, poetry competitions, music. The court would travel to famous *momiji* sites — Arashiyama, Takao, the mountains visible from the old capital — and spend days composing poems and celebrating the season.

The aesthetic foundation was *mono no aware* (物の哀れ) — the beauty of impermanence. The red maple is beautiful precisely because it will not last. Unlike cherry blossoms, which fall in days, autumn leaves hold for a week or two, long enough to travel to see them. But the knowledge that they are falling even as you watch them — that next week this mountain will be bare — is part of the experience. You are watching beauty end. The watching is the celebration.

In the Edo period, *momijigari* became popular among common people, not just aristocrats. Merchants organized group excursions; the culture of going to see autumn leaves in specific famous locations became something that all urban Japanese recognized as seasonal duty, seasonal pleasure. It remains so today. The human desire to travel specifically to witness a natural event that is happening right now, briefly, and will not be happening next week — this desire is very old in Japan.

## A Poem for This Season

The *Manyōshū* is full of maple poems. One of the most direct is by Kakinomoto no Hitomaro (7th-8th century):

> 秋山の　木の葉を見ては　うちなびく
> わが心さへ　ふりにけるかも
>
> *Akiyama no / ko no ha wo mite wa / uchinabiku / waga kokoro sae / furi ni keru kamo*

*Watching the leaves / of the autumn mountain / my swaying heart too / has grown / old.*

The poem makes an unexpected move: the speaker watches the autumn leaves and realizes, through the watching, that something in them mirrors something in himself. *Furi ni keru* — to have become old, to have aged. The autumn leaves don't just represent aging; they trigger the awareness of aging. The mountain turns; the speaker turns with it.

*Uchinabiku* — swaying, bending, yielding. The heart sways like a leaf in wind, like a branch that bends rather than breaks. The poem is about the way autumn enters the body through the eyes.

## How to Meet This Season

Go looking for the leaves this week, but don't just go to the famous places.

The famous places — Arashiyama, Nikko, Nara, Kyoto's temple gardens — are beautiful, but they're also crowded, and the crowd changes the experience. Before you go to the famous place, find the leaves nearest you: the small-leaved maple in the parking lot, the ivy on the old fence along the train track, the ginkgo on the street corner.

Find one tree that is currently in peak color and stand in front of it until you are really looking at it. Not photographing it — looking. The structure of the color, the way different leaves on the same tree are at different stages. The transparency of leaves backlit by the autumn sky. The way the color isn't one color but a range from yellow-green at the center to deep red at the edges, or gold at the top and rust below.

The Heian court traveled for days to see this. You might only have to walk to the corner. The leaves don't care how far you came.
