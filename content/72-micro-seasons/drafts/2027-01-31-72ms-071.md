# The Egg That Ends the Year

*Micro-season #71 of 72 — January 31 to February 3*

---

The last season in the 72-season cycle belongs to a hen.

Not a bear. Not a salmon. Not a mountain-crossing wind or a vanishing rainbow or a magnificent pheasant calling across frozen fields. The final entry in this ancient calendar — the thing that closes the year before the wheel turns to spring — is a hen returning to her nest box and leaving an egg.

I've sat with this for a while. It seems almost deliberately anticlimactic. And then I think about what the 72 micro-seasons have been trying to tell us all year long, and I realize the hen is the perfect ending.

The cycle closes not with spectacle but with the quiet recommencement of ordinary things.

---

## 鶏始乳 — Niwatori Hajimete Toya ni Tsuku

The name is one of the more specific and intimate in the entire 72-season collection.

*Niwatori* (鶏) is domestic chicken. The character contains the bird radical (隹) and the character for a rooster crowing at dawn (奚, related to the idea of waking and beginning). The chicken is the alarm-clock bird, the domestic bird that marks morning and meals and ordinary household time.

*Hajimete* (始) — for the first time, we have seen this word mark thresholds throughout the winter cycle. First frost. First call. First sprouting. Now, finally: the first laying of the new season.

*Toya ni tsuku* (鳥屋に就く) — "to settle in the henhouse," specifically the roosting box where hens lay eggs. *Toya* (鳥屋) is an old word for a bird-keeping space, a hen-house. *Tsuku* means to settle, to take a position, to commit to a place. The hen does not merely lay an egg; she *settles into* the laying box. There is intention in the verb.

Hens, for the first time, settle into their laying boxes.

---

## What's Actually Happening

In unmanaged traditional flocks — hens living without artificial lighting, as they would have lived in the farmhouses of classical Japan — egg production is governed primarily by light.

Hens require approximately fourteen hours of light per day to maintain full egg production. Below this threshold, the hormonal cascade that triggers ovulation and egg formation slows, and egg-laying decreases or stops entirely through the shortest days of the year. Traditional farmyard hens in pre-industrial Japan would have stopped laying in November or early December and remained largely non-productive through the winter months.

By late January, the days have lengthened enough — just barely — to cross the threshold. The increasing light stimulates the hen's pineal gland, triggering the hormonal changes that resume the reproductive cycle. The hen returns to the laying box.

The egg itself is a remarkable biological achievement: a complete, self-contained system for nurturing new life, produced in approximately twenty-four hours, requiring an enormous investment of calcium, protein, and energy. A laying hen who has been fasting from reproduction for six weeks is returning to this expenditure now, in mid-winter, responding to the faint but decisive increase in daily light.

In traditional Japan, this event was significant beyond its farm economics. Eggs were not the commodity they are today — year-round, inexpensive, available in any quantity. They were seasonal and precious. A winter egg was a luxury. The first egg of the new laying season, in late January or early February, was a specific, noticed thing.

---

## The Ordinary Is the Sacred

In Shinto and in the broader Japanese approach to the natural world, the distinction between sacred and mundane is much more permeable than it might seem.

*Ke* (褻) and *hare* (晴れ) — the ordinary day and the sacred celebration — are understood as complementary poles rather than separate categories. The *ke* of daily work — feeding chickens, gathering eggs, carrying water — exists in dynamic relation to the *hare* of festival and ritual. The sacred is not lifted above the ordinary; it is concentrated ordinary, the ordinary seen clearly.

The hen settling into the laying box is a *ke* event — as domestic, as unglamorous, as daily as anything in the 72-season calendar. And yet the calendar chose to end the cycle here. The ancient scholars who compiled these seasons could have ended with anything. They ended with a hen making an egg.

I think they knew exactly what they were doing.

After 71 seasons of watching the world — the frozen earth, the hiding rainbows, the bears going under and the salmon coming home and the butterbur pushing through snow — the cycle closes with this: the most ordinary domestic animal, in its henhouse, recommencing its ordinary work.

This is the wisdom of the 72 micro-seasons in its most concentrated form. The natural world is not made of dramatic events and spectacular phenomena. It is made of patterns. Of returns. Of the reliable, daily recommitment of living things to their own natures. The hen lays an egg because it is a hen, and the days are getting longer, and this is what hens do.

---

## Setsubun: The Night Before Spring

This micro-season ends on February 3rd — *Setsubun* (節分), the last day before *Risshun* (立春), the traditional beginning of spring.

*Setsubun* means "division of the seasons" — it marks the boundary between winter and spring in the old lunisolar calendar. The evening of *Setsubun* is celebrated across Japan with *mamemaki* (豆撒き), the throwing of roasted soybeans to drive out demons and invite fortune: *oni wa soto, fuku wa uchi* — "demons out, good luck in."

Families throw beans from their doorways into the dark February evening, shouting the formula. Children chase the beans. The oldest male member of the household may wear an oni mask to play the demon. In temples and shrines across the country, celebrity bean-throwers perform the ritual for large crowds.

It is, deliberately, a threshold ceremony. The old year's demons — illness, bad fortune, stagnation — are expelled on this last night before spring. The new cycle begins clean.

The 72 micro-seasons end at this threshold. The hen has resumed laying. The seeds of renewal are already in the ground. The pheasant has been calling for two weeks. The butterbur is pushing through the snow. And on *Setsubun* evening, the beans fly through the cold air: out with the old, in with the new.

## A Poem for This Season and This Closing

The poet Kobayashi Issa, writing near the end of his troubled life, produced a poem that feels like a fitting close to a year of careful attention:

> めでたさも　中くらいなり　おらが春
> *medetasa mo / chū kurai nari / oraga haru*

*The joy of spring —*
*somewhere in the middle.*
*My own spring.*

The poem is quietly extraordinary. Issa doesn't say spring is magnificent, or beautiful, or triumphant. He says: it's there. Somewhere in the middle — not the best, not the worst. His spring. *Oraga* is intimate and humble: "my own," in a rural dialect. It's not "the glorious spring." It's the one that arrived for him, this year, this time.

He wrote this poem in the first months of a year in which he would lose his only surviving child. He was not naive about the world. But spring came anyway. And it was his.

This is the grace the 72-season calendar has been pointing toward all year. Not the dramatic spring of poetry and postcards. But your own spring — the one arriving now, in the particular landscape and circumstances you actually inhabit, with its specific quality of light and its specific sounds and its specific small signs.

The hen has laid an egg. The year is ready to begin again.

---

## How to Meet This Season

On *Setsubun* — February 3rd — participate in the ritual of *mamemaki* if you can, or invent your own version of it.

The logic of the ceremony is not about superstition. It is about the psychological value of naming what you want to leave behind and actively expelling it, and naming what you want to invite and opening your door to it. Throw the beans. Say the words. Let the old year's demons go.

Then, in the morning: find an egg. This week, eggs are what the 72 micro-seasons have given you. Boil it, or fry it simply. Eat it slowly. Think about the hen in her henhouse, responding to six more minutes of light per day than there were six weeks ago. Think about the light accumulating. Think about the wheel turning.

After 71 seasons of watching the world closely — its frost and rainbows and bears and salmon and wheat and stars and ice and flowers — the invitation the final season offers is this: go back to the beginning. The first micro-season is called *Higashi kaze kōri o toku* (東風解凍) — "East winds thaw the ice." It starts in early February, just days from now.

Everything is about to start again.
