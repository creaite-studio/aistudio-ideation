# The Mountains Soften at Their Edges

*Micro-season #5 of 72 — February 24–28*

---

In winter, the mountains are sharp. Their ridgelines cut the sky cleanly; every pine, every cedar, every bare deciduous tree is individually visible against the pale blue cold. The world in deep winter is a world of clear edges.

Then, in late February, something happens to the distance. The far mountains soften. Not because the light has dimmed or the weather has turned cloudy — the sky might be perfectly clear — but because something has entered the atmosphere between you and those ridgelines. A slight haziness, a gentle blurring of edges. The mountains are still there, still large, still the same mountains. But they have softened.

This is *kasumi* — spring mist — and its appearance in the last days of February marks the fifth micro-season of the year. The ancient calendar did not treat this as mere weather. It treated it as a signal, as a quality that only the late-winter-turning-to-spring atmosphere produces. *Kasumi* is not fog, which is denser and closer. It is not cloud, which belongs to the sky. *Kasumi* belongs to the middle distance, to the space between the close and the far, and it has the specific effect of making the world look like a painting.

---

## 霞始靆 — Kasumi hajimete tanabiku

The characters of this name are beautiful and slightly rare. 霞 (*kasumi*) is the spring mist itself — a character that combines the radical for rain or weather with an element suggesting redness or dawn light. There is something already painterly in the character itself: mist associated with the color of morning.

始 (*hajimete*) means "for the first time," or "beginning to." 靆 (*tanabiku*) is a classical verb meaning to trail, to drift, to linger horizontally through space — used specifically for mist, clouds, and smoke that hang across a landscape rather than falling or rising. It is a word that requires a subject worth watching: *kasumi* is the kind of thing that *tanabiku*.

The full phrase: "The spring mist begins to trail across the landscape." Or, more literally: "For the first time, the mist drifts."

This is the second micro-season within the Usui ("rainwater") solar term. It follows the moistening of the soil and now turns upward: the moisture that has entered the earth and the air begins to manifest as this characteristic haze.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The spring mist is not a mystery — it is physics. As temperatures rise slightly, the air can hold more water vapor. When warm, moist air masses begin to move northward and encounter the still-cold landscape of late February Japan, the moisture near the surface condenses into fine suspended droplets. Unlike the freezing fog of winter, this is a gentler, warmer haze.

In Japan's landscape, where mountains rise steeply from valleys and coastal plains, temperature inversions are common in late winter and early spring: warmer air sits above cooler air near the ground, trapping moisture in the lower atmospheric layers. The result is the characteristic spring mist that hovers at middle elevations on mountain slopes, that fills the valleys between ridges, that softens the view of distant peaks.

Agricultural communities learned to read this mist carefully. *Kasumi* that forms and then burns off by mid-morning indicated a warming trend. *Kasumi* that persisted all day suggested continued cold or incoming rain. Like all elements of the 72-season calendar, it was never purely aesthetic — it was also information.

---

## Spring Mist and the Japanese Aesthetic

Few atmospheric effects have been more thoroughly absorbed into Japanese aesthetics than *kasumi*. It appears in:

**Ink painting** (*sumi-e*): The blank, unpainted areas of Japanese ink paintings often represent not empty space but *kasumi* — the mist that obscures the middle ground of a mountain landscape, allowing the eye to move from foreground to distant peaks without seeing what lies between. This technique was borrowed from Chinese painting but became even more central to Japanese artistic sensibility.

**The *Tale of Genji***: Murasaki Shikibu uses *kasumi* throughout her 11th-century novel to mark seasonal transitions and to create atmospheric settings for emotional scenes. When *kasumi* appears in the text, you know the season is turning.

**Screen paintings** (*byōbu*): The famous gold-leaf screens of the Momoyama and Edo periods frequently use stylized *kasumi* clouds — horizontal bands of gold-leafed mist — to separate narrative scenes or to suggest the passage of time. The gold *kasumi* became a hallmark of Japanese decorative art.

**The *mono no aware* aesthetic**: *Kasumi* is inherently transient. It appears, it softens the world, and then it dissipates. For a culture that has elevated the appreciation of beautiful impermanence to a philosophical principle, the spring mist is a natural emblem.

## A Poem for This Season

The spring mist features in some of the earliest Japanese poetry. From the *Kokinshū* (905), Japan's first imperial anthology of poetry:

*春立てば / 霞と共に / 立ちにけり / 春霞たつ山里の / 夕暮れかな*

*Haru tateba / kasumi to tomo ni / tachi ni keri / harugasumi tatsu yamazato no / yūgure kana*

"When spring arrives / the mist rises with it — / together they stand. / In the mountain village where spring mist rises, / the evening falls."

The poem entwines the arrival of spring and the rising of mist so thoroughly that they become the same event. The mountain village at dusk, wrapped in spring haze, is both a physical place and a state of mind — suspended, softened, neither quite one season nor the other.

## How to Meet This Season

In the morning, before the sun has fully risen, look toward the most distant thing you can see. A hillside, a tree line, a building several blocks away. Note how sharply you can see it. Then check again mid-morning: has anything changed? Has anything softened?

The best way to observe spring mist is from a slight elevation — a hill, a bridge over a wide river, an upper-floor window looking out over a valley. The mist lies in layers, and from above you can see it pooling in the low places, filling the spaces between things.

Carry this softness into how you see the day.
