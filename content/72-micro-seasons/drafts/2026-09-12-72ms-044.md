# The Dark Birds Are Gone

*Micro-season #44 of 72 — September 12 to September 16*

---

One morning in April, I noticed that the eaves above the front door had acquired a pair of residents overnight.

Two barn swallows, working with extraordinary energy and apparent confidence, had decided that the narrow ledge above the door — where I have never put anything, because it's too narrow to hold anything — was the ideal location for a mud nest. They worked most of the morning. By afternoon, the skeleton of a cup was taking shape. By evening, they were done with the first layer.

I spent the summer watching the nest. Two clutches. Six fledglings in total. The parents coming and going at speeds that seemed physically impossible for something that size, skimming low across the garden and banking hard through the gate without slowing. The chicks, in the last days before fledging, crammed into the nest in a pile that didn't look like it could contain them.

And then, sometime in the second week of September, I noticed the nest was empty and the air above the garden was quiet.

They leave while you're not looking.

---

## 玄鳥去 — Tsubame Saru

This season is the mirror of the 13th micro-season, 玄鳥至 — *Tsubame Kitaru* — which falls in early April and marks the swallows' arrival.

The same two characters begin both names: *玄鳥* (gensho, or tsubame — swallow). They share the same bird. But the third character changes everything.

*玄* (gen) means dark, deep, mysterious — and specifically, in Chinese cosmological tradition, the dark north, the principle of yin. It's not a coincidence that swallows, birds with dark backs and fast, mysterious movements, carry this character. Their blackness, their speed, their annual disappearance and return placed them in the category of things that came from and returned to the world beyond sight.

*鳥* (tori) is bird — the most fundamental of bird characters, itself a pictograph of a bird in profile.

*去* (saru) is the opposite of *kitaru* (至, arrival): it means to leave, to depart, to go away from. In classical Japanese, *saru* also appears in expressions of regret and farewell. Things that *saru* do so completely — they don't linger.

*The dark birds leave.* Five months after they arrived, 玄鳥去 closes the bracket.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Barn swallows (*Hirundo rustica*) travel one of the world's longest migration routes. The Japanese population winters in Southeast Asia — the Philippines, Indonesia, Borneo — and returns each April to the same nests, in the same eaves, sometimes to the same individual structures they used the previous year.

A swallow can live for several years, meaning the same bird may make this 5,000-kilometer journey half a dozen times in its lifetime. Banding studies have documented swallows returning to the same nest sites with remarkable precision. The barn above the farmhouse, the station platform in Okayama, the convenience store in Kochi — swallows know exactly where they are going.

In September, the triggers for departure are a combination of shortening daylength (sensed through the eyes) and cooling temperature. The birds aggregate first — you'll sometimes see dozens of swallows sitting together on telephone wires in early September, a behavior that's almost never seen in summer, when they're territorial. Then, over a few days, they thin out. And then they're gone.

In traditional Japanese farm culture, swallows nesting under the eaves of a house were considered deeply auspicious. The birds were associated with protection, with good harvests, and with the well-being of the family. Disturbing a swallow's nest was believed to bring bad luck. So farmers watched the swallows, fed them indirectly (by tolerating the mess), and marked their arrival and departure with something close to reverence.

## The Story the Calendar Tells

The 72 micro-seasons, spread across a year, create a kind of narrative. Events that happen to the same creatures or phenomena at opposite ends of the calendar mirror each other, creating structure.

The swallow story is the most explicitly narrated. In April: *玄鳥至* — the dark birds arrive. In September: *玄鳥去* — the dark birds leave. Between those two seasons stretches the entire Japanese summer: the planting of rice, the flood of heat, the cicadas, the typhoons, the August festivals, the first hints of autumn. The swallows frame it all.

What this structure does is make the year legible. Instead of a formless procession of days, the 72 micro-seasons divide time into chapters, each with its own character, and the recurring arrivals and departures of swallows provide something like chapter markers — the same motif appearing in different light, signaling where we are in the larger story.

When the swallows go, you know without being told: summer is over. The bracket has closed.

## A Poem for This Season

Bashō wrote his most explicit swallow poem as a departure poem — a kind of elegy for summer leaving with the birds:

> 去年より　また悲しうや　秋の暮れ
> *Kyonen yori / mata kanashiu ya / aki no kure*

*More sorrowful than last year — / the autumn dusk.*

The poem doesn't mention swallows directly. It doesn't need to. In the context of the 72 micro-seasons, *aki no kure* — the autumn dusk, or the autumn's end — carries the weight of all the departures: the swallows, the cicadas' voices growing quieter, the light going sideways and golden, the heat withdrawing. More sorrowful than last year. Each autumn deeper into the experience of autumn, more saturated with the knowledge that this is how it goes.

## How to Meet This Season

Watch the telephone wires and power lines near your home this week, especially in the mornings.

If swallows are gathering before departure, you'll see them lined up in numbers — five, ten, sometimes dozens — sitting in a way that's unusual. Not the individual perching of a bird resting, but a collective, directional waiting. Something is being coordinated in a way we can't fully see.

If the swallows near you have already gone: look for the empty nest. Look for the absence, which is its own kind of presence. A nest with no birds in it is not the same as a space that never had a nest. The emptiness has a shape.

The Japanese have a word for the awareness of absence: *mono no aware* — the pathos of things. The swallows leave and take with them something specific and irreplaceable. This is not sadness, exactly. It's recognition.
