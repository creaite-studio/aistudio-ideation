# The Most Vigorous Word for January

*Micro-season #66 of 72 — January 6 to January 10*

---

The word the calendar chose for this season is not "grows." It's not "appears" or "survives" or "persists in the cold."

It's *sakau* (栄): to flourish. To thrive. To prosper. The same character that appears in *han'ei* (繁栄), the flourishing of a civilization or a business. The same root that gives us *eiyō* (栄養), nutrition, literally "flourishing nourishment."

In the middle of January — in Japan's coldest weeks, when ice covers the low-lying ponds and most plants have retreated to roots or seeds — the water parsley is not surviving. It is flourishing.

The calendar is making a point. Vitality doesn't follow our schedules.

---

## 芹乃栄 — Seri Sunawachi Sakau

The name has a particular grammatical construction worth noticing.

*Seri* (芹) is water parsley, water dropwort, or Japanese parsley — the plant *Oenanthe javanica*. The character combines the plant radical (艹) at the top with a character below suggesting order or arrangement, perhaps evoking the plant's tidy, pinnate leaf structure.

*Sunawachi* (乃) is a classical connective particle — it appears in classical Chinese and Japanese texts as "thereupon," "thus," or "namely." It creates a slightly ceremonial rhythm: "Seri — thereupon — flourishes." As if to say: this is the plant's defining act, its natural conclusion.

*Sakau* (栄) is the flourishing already described. And it's worth noting how specifically this character was chosen. The previous seasons described the wheat "coming out" (*nobiru*, 出), the selfheal "sprouting" (*shōzu*, 生). These are humble verbs. The seri doesn't just sprout. It *flourishes*. The calendar gives the water parsley the most emphatic growth-verb in its vocabulary.

---

## What's Actually Happening

*Oenanthe javanica* — seri — is a semi-aquatic perennial herb that grows in shallow water, along stream banks, and in wet rice paddies across Japan. In summer, it grows upright and produces small white flowers. In winter, it does something unusual.

Most semi-aquatic plants either die back entirely or go dormant above water level in the cold months. Seri does neither. It sends new growth into the cold water and along the saturated banks, producing fresh, tender, bright green leaves precisely during January. Unlike the older summer growth, which can become slightly coarse and strong-tasting, the winter harvest of seri is mild, crisp, and delicately fragrant.

This timing connects directly to one of Japan's oldest culinary traditions: *nanakusa-gayu* (七草粥), the seven-herb rice porridge eaten on January 7th — the day called *Jinjitsu* (人日) or "human day," the seventh day of the new year, when people in classical Japan wished each other good health.

The seven spring herbs (*nanakusa*, 七草) of this porridge are seri (water parsley), nazuna (shepherd's purse), gogyo (cudweed), hakobera (chickweed), hotokenoza (henbit), suzuna (turnip), and suzushiro (daikon radish). All of them are winter-growing plants that can be gathered in early January. Seri, the first named in the tradition, is the most important and the most flavorful.

The porridge is intentionally simple — plain rice gruel with mild herbs — and it was understood as a restorative after the richness of the new year's *osechi* feast food. The herbs provided vitamins, minerals, and digestive support after days of celebratory eating. Farmers who foraged their own seri from the streams near their villages were also, simply, eating what was available and abundant at exactly this moment.

---

## The Herb of Impartial Water

There is a classical poem about seri that cuts to the heart of what the plant means in Japanese culture.

In the *Shin Kokinshū* (新古今和歌集), the major poetry anthology of the early 13th century, there is a concept that *seri* embodies particularly well: *musubi* (結び), the binding or connection of things that might seem unrelated. The seri grows where fresh water meets still, shallow edges — the liminal zone between river and field, between flowing and standing, between what is used and what is wild.

For centuries, the women of Japanese rural communities gathered seri in midwinter from these margins: the edges of paddies, the banks of irrigation channels, the shallows of slow streams. It was women's work — quiet, cold, requiring knowledge of the landscape to know where the best patches grew. The gathering was a form of knowing the land that was transmitted from mothers to daughters alongside everything else domestic.

This is one of the things the 72-season calendar preserves that a simple ecological description might miss: the way seasonal plants were entangled with specific human practices, specific bodies of work, specific kinds of knowledge. The flourishing of seri in January is also the flourishing of the people who gathered it.

---

## A Poem for This Season

The *Man'yōshū* contains a poem that has become one of the most famous in Japanese literature in part because of its imagery of *seri*:

> 春の野に　すみれ摘みにと　来しわれそ　野をなつかしみ　一夜寝にける
> *haru no no ni / sumire tsumi ni to / kishi ware so / no wo natsukashimi / hitoyo ni ni keru*

This is actually a spring poem about violets rather than seri, but the impulse it describes — going out to gather a wild plant and becoming so absorbed in the beauty of the landscape that you lose track of time entirely — captures something true about the seri-gatherer's experience.

For a winter poem more specific to this season, there is a haiku by Matsuo Bashō's contemporary and friend Mukai Kyorai:

> 芹の根の　白きを洗ふ　清水かな
> *seri no ne no / shiroki o arau / shimizu kana*

*The white roots of seri,*
*washing them clean*
*in the clear cold water.*

The image is tactile and precise. Someone is standing in cold January water, washing the mud from seri roots. The roots are white — *shiroi*, clean, almost glowing against the dark water. The cold water is *shimizu* — clear spring water, water with a particular clarity and purity associated with sacred sources.

The poem is about a mundane task made beautiful by attention. Cold hands, white roots, clear water. This is the winter harvest.

---

## How to Meet This Season

*Seri* can still be bought at Japanese supermarkets in January — look for it bundled near the Chinese chives and mitsuba, usually in the fresh herb section. It has a distinctive fragrance, a little like celery with a green edge.

If you can find it, make *nanakusa-gayu* on January 7th. The recipe is simple: prepare plain rice porridge, then stir in the finely chopped seven herbs — or whatever winter-growing herbs you can find, seri among them. Add a pinch of salt. Eat it in the morning, quietly, at the table. It's a meal that asks nothing of you except presence.

If you're outside Japan: *Oenanthe javanica* grows in rice paddies across South and Southeast Asia and is cultivated in specialty Asian groceries in many countries. Water cress (*Nasturtium officinale*) is a reasonable substitute — also semi-aquatic, also vigorous in cold water, also mild and green.

The invitation of this season is not complicated: find something that is flourishing in the cold, and honor it by eating it. The seri is not surviving winter. It is doing exactly what it's supposed to do, at exactly the right time, without complaint.

That is a kind of flourishing worth noticing.
