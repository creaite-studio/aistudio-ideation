# What the Soil Knows Before You Do

*Micro-season #20 of 72 — May 10 to May 14*

---

This morning I knelt in the garden for longer than I meant to.

I was pulling weeds near the base of the persimmon tree — a mindless task, the kind that empties your head out — when I turned over a clod of earth and found them: three earthworms, pale pink and glistening, recoiling from the sudden light. I set the soil back carefully and stayed crouching for a moment, watching the spot as if I'd stumbled on something sacred.

In a way, I had.

The old calendar knows about this moment. It has a name for it. For the past five days, Japan was in the micro-season of frogs beginning to sing. Now, with the same quiet exactness the calendar always carries, we step into the 20th of 72: the season when earthworms surface.

---

## 蚯蚓出 — Mimizu Izuru

The name is three characters.

蚯蚓 (*mimizu*) means earthworm. The kanji are rarely used in everyday writing — most people write the word in hiragana or katakana — but the characters themselves are interesting: both contain the radical for *insect* (虫), with additional components suggesting something that moves in the earth. The word *mimizu* in spoken Japanese is thought to derive from an older form meaning something that writhes or coils.

出 (*izuru*) means to emerge, to come out, to surface.

Together: *Earthworms surface.* Five characters in Japanese, three in English, and a whole season's worth of meaning compressed into an image most of us walk past without noticing.

The 72 micro-seasons were adapted into Japan from the Chinese *qi min yao shu*, an ancient agricultural text, and later refined for the Japanese climate during the Edo period. Each of the 72 periods lasts approximately five days, tracking not the dramatic turns of the year — not the first snow, not the cherry blossoms — but the subtle, easily-missed shifts that only a careful observer would catch. The surfacing of earthworms is exactly that kind of shift. You have to be paying a particular kind of attention to notice it.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Earthworms don't hibernate exactly, but when winter soil temperatures drop, they descend deep into the earth — sometimes several feet — where it stays warmer and moist. They slow down, reduce their activity, and wait.

By mid-May in Japan, soil temperatures in most of the country have risen enough that this waiting ends. Earthworms begin working their way back toward the surface, aerating the soil as they go, pulling organic matter downward, depositing their castings — one of the richest natural fertilizers on earth — in the layers where plant roots can reach them.

For farmers, this is not incidental. The return of earthworms to the upper soil signals that the earth is ready: warm enough, biologically active, metabolically awake. It's one of the more reliable indicators that the window for planting has truly opened. Long before soil thermometers, before agricultural extension services, a farmer who paid attention to the earthworms already knew what the calendar was trying to tell them.

There's something humbling about this. The soil is doing complex, essential work — continuous, largely invisible, indispensable — and the creatures doing it are ones we step on without thinking.

---

## The Workers No One Praises

Every culture has a way of reckoning with the unglamorous and the necessary.

In Japan, earthworms don't carry the symbolic weight of the crane or the pine or even the humble frog. They don't appear in the great anthologies. They are not emblems of longevity or luck. But they appear in the 72-season calendar, which is itself a kind of praise — an act of attention extended toward something most people would overlook.

Kobayashi Issa, the haiku poet known for his affection for small, overlooked creatures, wrote about earthworms at least once in his long body of work, capturing that thin, persistent wriggling life in seventeen syllables. The image is slight, almost offhand — which is exactly right. Earthworms resist grandeur. They're the ones working while everyone else is looking elsewhere.

There is a folk belief, still occasionally repeated in rural areas, that if you step on an earthworm, it will rain. Farmers tracked this kind of thing not as superstition but as observation — the worms come closer to the surface when barometric pressure drops and rain is coming. What looks like a folk belief is often the residue of centuries of careful watching.

---

## A Poem for This Season

> みみずなく　秋の夜ふけの　さびしさよ
> *Mimizu naku / aki no yofuke no / sabishisa yo*

This haiku, attributed to the tradition of Issa's followers, translates roughly as:

*The earthworm singing —*
*deep in the autumn night's*
*loneliness.*

There's an interesting twist here worth noting: when classical poets wrote of "earthworms singing" on autumn evenings, they were almost certainly hearing mole-crickets (*kera*), not earthworms at all. Earthworms don't produce sound. But the image — something small and soil-dwelling, making its invisible presence felt in the dark — carried emotional truth regardless of biological accuracy. Poets have always been more interested in what a creature means than in what it technically does.

In May, there is no melancholy. The earthworm's return is not lonely. It is the earth waking up.

---

## How to Meet This Season

If you have any patch of earth available — a garden, a planter, even a pot of soil on a balcony — turn some of it over this week. Not to plant anything yet, necessarily. Just to look.

Notice the color of the soil. Is it dark and crumbly, threaded with roots, or pale and compacted? The presence of earthworms is one of the best indicators of soil health: rich, living soil has them; depleted, chemically exhausted soil often doesn't.

If you find them, put the earth back. Let them continue their work.

And if you find nothing, that too is information — an invitation to add compost, to mulch, to stop using the products that kill what you can't see. The season of earthworms surfacing is also, quietly, a season for asking what we might be doing underground, in the dark, that we've been too busy to notice.
