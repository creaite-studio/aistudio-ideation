# The Undying Orange

*Micro-season #59 of 72 — November 27 to December 1*

---

In a country full of cherished seasonal plants, the tachibana occupies a particular kind of reverence. It is on the imperial crest. It gives its name to a month in the old lunar calendar. It appears in the oldest written texts Japan has produced. And yet most people in modern Japan have never eaten one, and many have never seen one growing.

It's a small, sour, rather unremarkable citrus — a distant relative of the bitter orange family, about the size of a golf ball, turning yellow in late November as the leaves fall from everything around it. It wouldn't win a competition with a mandarin on any practical basis. But it has been keeping its vigil in Japanese orchards and temple grounds for over two thousand years, outlasting empires and fashions and tastes.

There is something the tachibana knows about persistence that we have forgotten.

---

## 橘始黄 — Tachibana Hajimete Kibamu

The season's name is again one of those slow unfoldings characteristic of the 72-season system.

*Tachibana* (橘) is the name of the fruit itself — Japan's native citrus. The character is composed of the wood radical (木) on the left, indicating a tree, and a combination of characters on the right that historically conveyed the idea of a fruit borne on long branches. It is one of those specialized kanji that exists only for this one thing, a character with no other use: a word that the language built a house for and left standing, even as the thing itself became rare.

*Hajimete* (始) — again we encounter this character for "the first time," the same one that appeared in micro-season #55 when the earth first began to freeze. The 72-season system loves this word. It marks the moment of threshold, the day something tips from not-yet to now.

*Kibamu* (黄) contains within it the character for yellow (黄, *ki*) — the color of ripeness, of autumn light, of aged ivory. The -mu ending makes it into a verb: to *become* yellow, to *take on* yellowness. Not simply to be yellow. The tachibana is in the process of changing, and the season catches it mid-transformation.

The tachibana citrus begins to become yellow. The season names the verb, not the noun.

---

## What's Actually Happening

*Citrus tachibana* — the scientific name honors the Japanese common name directly — is the one citrus species native to Japan, rather than introduced from China or Southeast Asia. It is believed to have been cultivated in Japan since at least the 3rd century CE, and likely much earlier in the mild coastal regions of Shikoku and Kyushu.

As a plant, it is hardy in ways that the more commercially valued citrus varieties are not. It tolerates relatively cold winters, frost, poor soil. Where mandarin oranges or yuzu require careful management, the tachibana persists almost stubbornly. It keeps its leaves through winter. In the bare landscape of December, the evergreen tachibana, now heavy with small yellow fruit, stands out with particular brightness.

The fruit ripens precisely now — late November through December — when the surrounding landscape has gone grey and brown. This timing is not accidental in ecological terms: the yellow fruit against bare branches is a signal to birds, which eat the fruit and disperse the seeds. But in the Japanese aesthetic it is also read as a kind of faithfulness. The tachibana holds its color when everything else has surrendered.

---

## The Grove of the Immortals

The deeper cultural resonance of the tachibana goes back to the earliest Japanese myths.

In the *Nihon Shoki* (720 CE), one of Japan's oldest chronicles, there is a story about the Emperor Keikō sending an envoy across the sea to seek the fruit of immortality. The envoy returns with a miraculous citrus from a distant island where the dead do not decay and the seasons do not change. This fruit — *tokijiku no kagu no ko no mi*, "the fragrant fruit of the everchanging season" — is identified in later texts as the tachibana.

The fruit was planted in the imperial gardens, and from this tree sprang all the tachibana cultivated in Japan thereafter. The tree became a symbol of the court's connection to immortality and divine favor. The left side of the imperial throne room at Kyoto's Gosho palace still features a ceremonial tachibana tree, counterpart to the cherry tree on the right — one side eternal, one side ephemeral.

This is the weight the tachibana carries: the fruit that came from the land of the deathless, that stayed green when everything else withered. There's something almost uncomfortable about eating it — something that feels like it should be preserved rather than consumed. And perhaps this is why, despite being one of Japan's oldest citrus varieties, it's mostly left on the branch. It's an ornamental now, more symbol than food.

---

## A Poem for This Season

The *Man'yōshū* (万葉集), Japan's earliest poetry anthology compiled in the 8th century, contains several poems about the tachibana. Among the most famous is by Kakimoto no Hitomaro:

> 橘の　にほへる香かも　ほととぎす　鳴く夜の雨に　うつろひぬらん
> *tachibana no / nioheru ka kamo / hototogisu / naku yo no ame ni / utsuroinuran*

*The fragrance of tachibana — perhaps the cuckoo's*
*scent, carried off*
*in the rain of the night it calls.*

This is a summer poem — the cuckoo (*hototogisu*) signals late spring and early summer — but the tachibana here is in its flowering stage, filling the night air with white-blossom fragrance. The poem is about impermanence: the fragrance drifts away in rain, the cuckoo's call fades. Even the undying orange has a fleeting moment of fragrance that cannot be held.

The paradox that haunts the tachibana across all its appearances in Japanese poetry: it is the fruit of immortality, and yet its fragrance, its moment of bloom, its brief yellow brightness in November — all of these pass. Even the symbol of the deathless knows transience.

---

## How to Meet This Season

The tachibana is genuinely difficult to find in modern Japan unless you know where to look: temple gardens, traditional residences, botanical gardens specializing in classical plants. The Kyoto Imperial Palace grounds are a good bet if you're in the area.

But the season's invitation is broader than one specific fruit. Find something yellow and persistent in the grey landscape of late November. A yuzu on a neighbor's tree, heavy and golden. A persimmon that has outlasted the leaves. Even a yellow ginkgo leaf, pressed against wet pavement — still holding its color when everything around it has gone brown.

The tachibana is a prompt to think about what persists. What in your own life keeps its color through the stripping season? What is the small, unremarkable thing — not spectacular, not fashionable — that simply stays? That shows up yellow in November when everything else has decided it's finished?

The symbol of immortality is a small sour citrus that no one eats anymore. There is something exactly right about that.
