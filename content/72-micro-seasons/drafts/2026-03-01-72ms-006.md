# The Green Urgency of Living Things

*Micro-season #6 of 72 — March 1–5*

---

It begins at the tips of things. The very ends of the branches, where there was nothing last week — or what seemed like nothing — now show the faintest swelling. Look at the forsythia, the magnolia, the willow along the riverbank. The buds are not open. They may not open for weeks. But they are visibly, unmistakably different from how they were.

This is not a subtle phenomenon if you have been watching. A gardener knows this moment. A farmer knows it. A person who takes the same walk through the same grove of trees every day in February and March will tell you exactly when it happened: there was a morning when everything looked slightly different, when the trees seemed more somehow, more present, more about to do something.

The sixth micro-season of the year is named for this moment: the first visible greening, the first stirring of the growing impulse in the vegetation of Japan. After weeks of weather changes — wind, rain, mist — now the plants themselves respond.

---

## 草木萌動 — Sōmoku mebae izuru

Four characters, each carrying weight. 草 (*kusa*) means grass or small plants — ground-level vegetation, the kind underfoot. 木 (*ki* or *moku*) means tree or wood — the tall and woody things. Together, 草木 (*sōmoku*) means all of plant life, the full sweep of vegetation from the smallest ground herb to the tallest tree.

萌 (*mebae* or *moe*) is one of the most resonant characters in the seasonal vocabulary. It means to sprout, to bud, to emerge — but it also carries connotations of the first stirring of any living impulse, the very beginning of emergence. In modern Japanese slang, *moe* has taken on an entirely different meaning relating to enthusiastic affection, but in its original classical sense it is about the green urgency of growth beginning.

動 (*ugoki* or *dō*) means to move, to stir, to be in motion. So the full name says something like: "Grasses and trees begin to stir and sprout." Or: "All vegetation begins to move."

This is the third and final micro-season of the Usui solar term. We have come from the moistening of the soil to the drifting of mist to, now, the visible response of plant life.

---

## What's Actually Happening

Bud break — the moment when the growing tip of a dormant plant begins to swell and ultimately to open — is one of the most studied phenomena in phenology, the science of biological timing and seasonal cycles. It is triggered by a combination of factors: accumulated warmth units (called growing degree days), day length, and the release from a dormancy that requires a period of cold to break.

In Japan's cool-temperate regions, the timing of bud break moves from south to north through March and April, following the northward progression of warmth. The famous *sakura* (cherry blossom) front — tracked by the Japanese Meteorological Corporation and reported as national news — is essentially a bud-break story, the most celebrated version of what this micro-season describes.

But bud break happens first in the plants that flower before the cherries: the plum (*ume*), already past peak by now; the forsythia, whose branches will soon run yellow; the magnolias, whose fat flower buds are some of the most dramatic in early spring. The willows (*yanagi*) that grow along rivers and canals develop a haze of thin new green shoots before any individual leaf is fully open.

What the eye perceives as "the trees waking up" is actually thousands of individual biological decisions, each plant species responding to its own particular combination of accumulated warmth and day length. The 72-season calendar names the moment when this process becomes visible to a human observer paying attention.

---

## The Character That Does the Work: 萌

The character 萌 (*moe* / *mebae*) deserves its own attention. Structurally, it shows the "grass/plant" radical at the top (艹) above the character for the sun just rising over the horizon (明, meaning "bright" or "dawn"). It is a character that literally pictures vegetation in the morning light, the first light of the growing season.

In classical literature, *moe* and *mebae* appear when something is just beginning — the first flicker of feeling, the first suggestion of beauty in a landscape that was previously inert. The poet Fujiwara no Teika, one of the architects of classical Japanese poetics, understood *moe* as a quality of perception as much as of the plant world: the greening of the world and the greening of attention occur together.

This doubling — the natural world budding and the human attention sharpening in response — is fundamental to how the 72-season calendar works. It is not just a record of what nature does. It is an instruction to notice.

## A Poem for This Season

From the *Man'yōshū*, attributed to the Emperor Kōtoku (7th century):

*春の野に / 霞たなびき / うら悲し / この夕影に / うぐいす鳴くも*

*Haru no no ni / kasumi tanabiki / uragakashi / kono yūkage ni / uguisu naku mo*

"In the spring meadow / where mist trails across — / a faint sadness / in this evening light / the uguisu calls."

The poem brings together the mist of the previous micro-season and the birdsong of the season before that, and sets them in a meadow just beginning to stir with new growth. The "faint sadness" (*uragakashi*) is not melancholy but something more subtle: the poignant beauty of things that are just beginning, that are still fragile, that may not last. The uguisu's call in the evening field resonates with the green that is only just now beginning to appear.

## How to Meet This Season

Go to your nearest tree and look closely at the branch tips. If you find buds, study them: are they smooth or rough, sticky or dry, pointed or rounded? Measure them against your fingernail. Come back in five days and look again.

This is the season to begin keeping a plant diary, even a simple one. Note what is changing, where. The micro-seasons reward longitudinal attention — watching the same tree across multiple visits — more than any single perfect observation.

Rake up your garden bed if you have one. The soil that was moistened last week is now ready to receive seeds. The plants know. Your role is to notice that they know.
