# The Night Japan Fills With Frogs

*Micro-season #19 of 72 — May 5 to May 9*

---

Last night, something shifted.

I noticed it just after ten o'clock, when I stepped outside to take in the cool air before bed. The neighborhood was quiet — or what passes for quiet here in early May. And then it hit me: the frogs had started.

Not one or two. Thousands.

From the rice paddies a few minutes' walk from my house came a sound I can only describe as the countryside *turning on* — a wet, pulsing chorus that seemed to rise from the ground itself. *Gero gero gero.* Relentless, rhythmic, alive.

I stood there longer than I meant to.

---

## 蛙始鳴 — Kawazu Hajimete Naku

Today marks the beginning of the 19th of Japan's 72 micro-seasons.

The name breaks apart beautifully: *kawazu* (蛙) means frog. *Hajimete* (始めて) means "for the first time." *Naku* (鳴く) means to cry, to call, to sing.

*Frogs begin to sing.*

Five words. A whole season.

In Japan, the traditional calendar doesn't recognize just four seasons. It divides the year into 24 solar terms, and each of those into three micro-seasons of roughly five days each — 72 in total. Each one carries a name drawn from nature: the day the east wind thaws the ice, the week the bush warbler first sings, the moment the paulownia tree begins to seed.

This one belongs to the frogs.

---

## What's Actually Happening

The timing isn't random. It never is with the 72 micro-seasons.

In early May across Japan, farmers begin flooding their rice paddies. Water flows in from irrigation channels, turning dry brown fields into shallow mirrors reflecting the sky. And where there is still, shallow water and warm spring air — frogs appear, almost immediately, as if they were waiting underground for exactly this moment. Because they were.

The Japanese tree frog (*Hyla japonica*, the bright green one you've seen in photos) is the most vocal. But in my area it's the *tono-sama gaeru* — the "lord frog," a leopard frog — that dominates. Its call is lower, more authoritative. Hence the nickname.

For a few weeks in May and June, the sound of rural Japan at night is the sound of frogs. Farmers have listened to this chorus for thousands of years. Before thermometers, before weather apps, the chorus of frogs told you the water temperature was right, the season had truly turned, and it was time to plant.

---

## The Word That Means Two Things

Here's something that doesn't translate.

The Japanese word for frog is *kaeru* (蛙). But *kaeru* (帰る) also means "to return home."

Same sound. Different characters. A coincidence so persistent it became culture.

In Japan, frog figurines are kept in wallets to make money *return*. They're placed near doorways to ensure travelers *return* safely. You'll find small ceramic frogs tucked into the corners of old shops and izakayas, quietly doing their homophonic work.

I have one on my desk. A small green fellow, slightly chipped, that my grandmother gave me years ago. I used to think it was just a folk belief. Now I find it quietly beautiful — the way language, superstition, and nature have been wound together for so long that no one quite remembers where one ends and another begins.

---

## Bashō Heard It Too

The most famous haiku in the Japanese language is about a frog.

> 古池や　蛙飛び込む　水の音
> *furuike ya / kawazu tobikomu / mizu no oto*

Matsuo Bashō wrote it in 1686. It goes:

*An old silent pond.*
*A frog jumps into the pond.*
*Splash! Silence again.*

Scholars have spilled centuries of ink over those seventeen syllables. What did Bashō mean? Was it about Zen emptiness? The nature of sound and silence? The sudden interruption of the eternal?

Maybe. But I think part of what makes it hold is simpler than that: Bashō was sitting somewhere in late spring, listening to frogs, and he noticed. He paid attention to something small and ordinary that most people walked past. And then he wrote it down in a way that made other people notice too.

That's what the 72 micro-seasons ask of us, I think.

Not to chase the dramatic and the Instagram-worthy. But to notice the texture of *right now* — this particular five-day window, this specific quality of air and sound and light that will not come again until next May.

---

## How to Meet This Season

You don't need to live near rice paddies to find this one.

If you're in Japan: step outside after dark this week, somewhere away from the loudest streets. Even in the suburbs, if you find a canal or a park with standing water, you may hear them.

If you're not in Japan: this is a good week to be near any still water as dusk falls. Frogs begin singing at the same threshold of warmth and moisture everywhere. Listen for what's already there, in whatever season surrounds you right now.

And if you have a frog figurine somewhere: take it out. Put it somewhere you'll see it. Let it remind you that *return* is woven into the name of this small noisy creature — that the things we lose have a way, sometimes, of coming back.
